## A25. Building a Learning Organization

- **Level:** Advanced, self-directed
- **Audience:** managers, directors and senior leaders
- **Domain:** Culture; Okun lens: power hoarding, worship of the written word, either/or thinking
- **Summary:** Leaders shape culture through the systems they build: how decisions are made, who hears about problems and what gets reviewed after something goes wrong. This course helps leaders design routines that let a division learn from its own work, including from the people closest to the community.
- **Outcomes:** By the end, learners can (1) map how information about problems reaches them today, (2) run a blameless review of a decision that went wrong, and (3) share one decision with the people it affects before it is final.

### Slide outline

1. **Title:** One DSD People, Access and Culture / Building a Learning Organization / Leading so the whole division can learn / Advanced self-directed learning
2. **Opening case:** A policy change Nadia's director approved has caused confusion for months. Frontline staff knew in week one. The news reached the director in month four, in a complaint from a legislator's office.
3. **What a learning organization is:** A learning organization notices what isn't working and changes, without waiting for a crisis. It depends less on smart individuals and more on routines that move what people know to where decisions are made.
4. **Culture is built by systems:** Leaders teach culture through what they pay attention to, how they react to bad news and who they promote. Those signals outweigh any values statement.
5. **Power and information:** When decisions sit with a few people, so does information. Staff stop sharing what leaders can't act on, and leaders decide with half the picture.
6. **The written word and other ways of knowing:** A memo is not the only form of evidence. What a case manager hears at a kitchen table, or what a county partner sees in the waiting room, is data too.
7. **Either/or thinking:** "Either we follow the policy or we make exceptions" hides the third option: learn why the policy isn't working and fix it.
8. **Practice 1:** see below.
9. **Mapping how bad news travels:** Draw how a problem on the front line reaches you. Count the steps. Each step is a place where the message can soften, stall or stop.
10. **Blameless review:** After something goes wrong, ask what happened, what made sense to people at the time and what the system made easy or hard. Leave out who to blame. People tell the truth when it is safe to.
11. **Running the review:** Invite the people who did the work, including partners. Set the ground rules aloud. End with two or three changes and an owner for each.
12. **Practice 2:** see below.
13. **Sharing decisions before they are final:** Bring a draft to the people it affects. Ask what you missed. You keep the decision. You lose the surprises.
14. **Feedback loops that last:** A quarterly listening session with frontline staff. A standing agenda item for partner concerns. A rule that every new process gets a 60-day check.
15. **Practice 3:** see below.
16. **Your own reaction is the signal:** The first time someone brings you bad news, your reaction tells them whether to do it again. Thank them before you respond to the content.
17. **What this is not:** A learning organization still makes decisions and holds people accountable. It just learns before it blames.
18. **Rewrite activity:** Rewrite this leadership update so it invites learning: "The new intake process is working as designed. Please follow it."
19. **Your division's next step:** Choose one recent decision. Plan a 45-minute blameless review with the people closest to it.
20. **Shared culture, real responsibilities:** Everyone can raise what they see. Leaders decide whether the system hears it, so the design of feedback loops is a leadership responsibility.
21. **Reflection:** What is something your division knows that you, as a leader, may not?
22. **Closing:** A division learns at the speed its leaders can hear bad news.

### Practice slides

Each practice slide ends with: "In Slide Show view, select a response to explore its effects."

**Practice 1.** In a leadership meeting, someone says frontline staff are "resisting" the new intake process. What does the director say?

- **A.** "Remind them it's required." *Feedback: It restates the rule and misses the chance to learn why staff are struggling.*
- **B.** "What are they seeing that we aren't?" *Feedback: This treats resistance as information and opens a path to fix the process.*
- **C.** "Let's give it more time." *Feedback: Patience can help, and without a question attached it can also delay a needed fix.*

**Practice 2.** In a blameless review, a supervisor starts to say which case manager made the error. What does the facilitator do?

- **A.** Lets them finish. *Feedback: The room learns that this review is about blame after all.*
- **B.** Gently redirects: "Let's stay with what the process made easy or hard." *Feedback: This keeps the ground rules and keeps people willing to speak.*
- **C.** Ends the review. *Feedback: It avoids conflict and loses the learning.*

**Practice 3.** Nadia has a draft policy ready to send. Rosa's county partners haven't seen it. What does Nadia do?

- **A.** Sends it and invites feedback afterward. *Feedback: Feedback after a decision is final rarely changes it, and partners know that.*
- **B.** Shares the draft with Rosa's group for one week, with two questions. *Feedback: A short, focused review catches problems early and builds trust.*
- **C.** Holds it until every partner agrees. *Feedback: Consensus can stall urgent work. Sharing before deciding is not the same as needing agreement.*

### Narration

- **Opening (after slide 1):** "Every division learns something every day. The question is whether that learning reaches the people who make decisions. In this course, you'll look at the systems you lead and how they carry, or block, what your people know."
- **Explainer (after slide 5):** When information can only travel up, it often doesn't. Look for the places where it stops.
- **Check-in (after slide 11):** Think of the last time something went wrong. Was there a review, and who was in the room?
- **Explainer (after slide 16):** Your reaction to bad news is a system, too. People learn it fast.

### Video script: "Month Four" (about 3 minutes)

**Scene 1. Front desk, week one.**

NARRATOR: A new intake rule starts on Monday. By Wednesday, the front line knows something is wrong.

SAM: (to Priya) Families are coming back twice because the checklist asks for a form they don't have yet.

PRIYA: I'll mention it up the chain.

**Scene 2. Priya and Nadia, hallway, week two.**

PRIYA: The checklist is causing return visits.

NADIA: Leadership signed off on it. Let's give it a few weeks.

**Scene 3. Director's office, month four.**

NARRATOR: Month four. The news arrives another way.

NADIA: (reading) A legislator's office is asking why families are waiting twice as long.

DIRECTOR (voice only): Why am I hearing about this now?

NADIA: Honestly? Because we made it hard to hear sooner.

**Scene 4. Blameless review.**

NADIA: Ground rules: we're here to understand what happened, not who to blame. Sam, what did you see first?

SAM: The form problem, in week one.

ROSA: Our county staff saw it too. We didn't know where to send it.

NADIA: So two changes. A 60-day check on every new process. And a direct line for partners, with a named owner. Me.

NARRATOR: The problem was found in week one. The system took four months to hear it.

### Put this course to work

Pick one recent decision and schedule a 45-minute blameless review with the people closest to it, partners included.
