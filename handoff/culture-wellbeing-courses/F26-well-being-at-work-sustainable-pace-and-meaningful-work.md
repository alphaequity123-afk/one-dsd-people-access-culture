## F26. Well-Being at Work: Sustainable Pace and Meaningful Work

- **Level:** Foundation, self-directed
- **Audience:** all staff
- **Domain:** Well-Being
- **Summary:** Work in human services matters, and it can wear people down. This course helps staff notice their own pace, protect the parts of the work that give it meaning and ask for support early, without treating well-being as one more thing to get right.
- **Outcomes:** By the end, learners can (1) describe what a sustainable week looks like for them, (2) name two early signs that their pace isn't working, and (3) find the support options their workplace offers and know how to ask for them.

### Slide outline

1. **Title:** One DSD People, Access and Culture / Well-Being at Work: Sustainable Pace and Meaningful Work / Doing work that matters without running on empty / Foundation self-directed learning
2. **Opening case:** Sam has carried a full caseload for two years and is good at it. Lately Sam answers email at 10 p.m., skips lunch and feels flat even after a good outcome for a family.
3. **Why well-being belongs at work:** How people feel at work shapes how they treat clients, colleagues and themselves. Well-being isn't a perk. It is part of doing the job well over time.
4. **Well-being is shared:** Personal habits matter, and so do workload, staffing and how a team runs. This course covers both, and never asks you to fix alone what the system needs to fix.
5. **What sustainable pace means:** A sustainable pace is one you could keep for a year. Some weeks will be heavier. The question is whether you recover between them.
6. **Meaning as a source of energy:** Most people came to this work for a reason. Remembering that reason, and noticing when the work still delivers it, is one of the strongest supports there is.
7. **Early signs your pace isn't working:** Short temper, trouble switching off, dreading tasks you used to like and feeling numb after good news. These are signals, not failures.
8. **Practice 1:** see below.
9. **Small protections:** A real lunch break. A fixed time you stop checking email. A five-minute pause after a hard call. Small protections add up.
10. **Boundaries that serve clients too:** A rested case manager catches more and listens better. Protecting your time is part of protecting the people you serve.
11. **Asking for support early:** It is easier to adjust a workload or a schedule before a crisis than after. Asking early is a skill, not a weakness.
12. **Practice 2:** see below.
13. **Supporting a colleague:** Notice, ask and listen. "You've seemed stretched lately. How are you doing?" You don't need to fix it. You can point to support.
14. **Where support lives:** Your supervisor, the Employee Assistance Program and your agency's leave and flexibility options. Find out what your workplace offers before you need it.
15. **Practice 3:** see below.
16. **When it's more than stress:** If you are struggling to cope, or thinking about harming yourself, reach out now. In the U.S., you can call or text 988. You don't need to be in crisis to talk to someone.
17. **What this is not:** This course doesn't measure anyone's well-being or ask you to share anything personal. It offers ideas you can use as you choose.
18. **Rewrite activity:** Rewrite this out-of-office note so it sets a clear, kind boundary: "I'll try to check messages while I'm out."
19. **Your next step:** Pick one small protection to try for the next two weeks.
20. **Shared culture, real responsibilities:** Everyone can look out for each other. Supervisors and managers control workload and schedules, so they carry more of the responsibility for a sustainable pace.
21. **Reflection:** What part of your work still gives you energy, and how can you protect it?
22. **Closing:** Good work over the long run needs people who can keep doing it.

### Practice slides

Each practice slide ends with: "In Slide Show view, select a response to explore its effects."

**Practice 1.** It's 9:45 p.m. Sam sees a non-urgent email from a colleague. What does Sam do?

- **A.** Answers it now so it's done. *Feedback: It clears the inbox and teaches everyone, Sam included, that evenings are work time.*
- **B.** Leaves it for the morning. *Feedback: Nothing is lost, and Sam gets real time off.*
- **C.** Schedules a reply to send at 8 a.m. *Feedback: A good middle path. It keeps the work off the evening for others, too.*

**Practice 2.** Sam's caseload has grown by a third since spring. What does Sam do?

- **A.** Keeps going, since everyone is busy. *Feedback: Common, and the cost builds quietly until it shows up in the work or in Sam's health.*
- **B.** Raises it with Priya and brings the numbers. *Feedback: Asking early, with facts, gives Priya something to act on.*
- **C.** Starts looking for another job without saying anything. *Feedback: That may be Sam's right choice in the end, and the team never got the chance to fix the workload.*

**Practice 3.** Jordan notices Sam seems withdrawn in meetings. What does Jordan do?

- **A.** Says nothing, since it isn't Jordan's business. *Feedback: Respecting privacy matters, and a kind check-in doesn't invade it.*
- **B.** Asks privately, "How are you doing? You've seemed stretched lately." *Feedback: Simple and caring. Sam can share as much or as little as Sam wants.*
- **C.** Mentions it to the whole team. *Feedback: Well meant, and it puts Sam on the spot.*

### Narration

- **Opening (after slide 1):** "The work you do matters to the people you serve. To keep doing it well, you need a pace you can sustain. This course isn't about adding self-care to your to-do list. It's about noticing what you need, and knowing where to get support."
- **Explainer (after slide 4):** Well-being is partly personal and partly about how work is set up. Both deserve attention.
- **Check-in (after slide 7):** Have you noticed any of these signs lately? You don't need to share. Just notice.
- **Explainer (after slide 16):** If any part of this course feels heavy, pause. Support is available, and you can come back when you're ready.

### Video script: "Running on Empty" (about 3 minutes)

**Scene 1. Sam's kitchen, evening.**

NARRATOR: Sam is one of the best case managers on the team. Lately, the work comes home.

SAM: (typing on a phone, to no one) Just one more.

**Scene 2. Team room, next day.**

JORDAN: The Lee family got approved. That's your case, right?

SAM: Yeah. Good.

JORDAN: You don't sound like it's good.

SAM: I'm just tired.

**Scene 3. Priya's office, one-on-one.**

SAM: I wanted to talk about my caseload. It's up about a third since spring.

PRIYA: Thank you for telling me. What's the hardest part right now?

SAM: I can't switch off. I'm answering email at ten at night.

PRIYA: Let's look at the numbers together this week. And please stop answering at night. Nothing I send after five needs an answer until morning.

**Scene 4. Narrator close.**

NARRATOR: Sam didn't wait for a crisis. That made the fix smaller.

### Put this course to work

Pick one small protection, such as a set time to stop checking email, and try it for two weeks. Then look up your workplace's Employee Assistance Program so you know where it is.
