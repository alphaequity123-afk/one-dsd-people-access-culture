# sm-why-this-is-supervisor-work: Why Equity Is a Supervisor's Work

Subtitle: A frank look at what training does and does not do, and why the everyday decisions you make as a supervisor are where equity is actually decided.
Duration: 62 minutes
Content type: foundation
Status: reviewed
Last reviewed: October 31, 2026
Evidence date: October 2026

Where this course mentions law, it is general information as of October 2026, not legal advice.

Course objectives:
- Describe honestly what the research shows about equity training, including its limits.
- Identify the everyday decisions where outcomes for people on your team are actually set.
- Explain why tools tied to decisions are a better bet than awareness alone.
- Set up feedback and follow-through that you can run yourself, without scores or reports to anyone.
- Avoid the most likely failure: finishing a course and believing the work is handled.

Applied next step: Choose one recurring decision you own, such as who gets stretch work, and for the next 90 days use one tool from this course on every instance of it. At the end, compare what you see with what you expected.

## Lesson 1: What training does, and what it does not

Summary: Most equity training teaches people things. Much less of it changes what they do at work. This lesson tells you what the evidence shows, including what it means for the course you are reading now.
Minutes: 13
Objectives:
- Summarize the pattern across major studies of diversity and bias training.
- Explain why large average effects in reviews can mislead.
- Describe what is unsettled about mandatory and voluntary training.
- State what this app can and cannot do for you.
Takeaways:
- Training reliably moves knowledge and what people say right afterward. Evidence that it moves job behavior is weak.
- No rigorous evaluation of equity training for human services supervisors in government was found.
- This course is a tool. It is not the change.

### Text
You deserve a straight answer about whether this kind of learning works. Here it is. Training is good at teaching people things. It is much weaker at changing what people do on a Tuesday afternoon, and weaker still at changing outcomes across an organization.

The largest review is Bezrukova and colleagues, 2016, covering 260 studies. It reports effects that look solid. Those effects were largest for how people felt about the training and for what they learned, and smaller for attitudes. Read the fine print, though. The typical follow-up came less than a month after training. The median program ran 28 hours, far longer than an online course. "Behavioral learning" in that review includes self-reports and situational tests, not just observed behavior. Results such as grievances, turnover or recruitment were rarely measured.

The closest study to online learning like this is Chang and colleagues, 2019, a randomized trial at a global company. About 3,000 employees took part, out of nearly 11,000 invited, and the course ran about an hour. Attitudes moved at the end of training. Overall behavior, such as choosing women for informal mentoring or recognizing them for excellence, did not. Some subgroups did shift, mostly employees who were themselves women or people of color. The authors concluded one-off training is unlikely to be a solution on its own. And since the participants chose to take part, those results may be generous.

Bias awareness in particular has a poor record. Forscher and colleagues, 2019, reviewed nearly 500 studies and found that implicit measures can shift a little, but behavior changes were generally trivial, and changing implicit scores did not explain changes in behavior. Lai and colleagues, 2016, tested nine brief interventions. All worked at first, and none was effective after a delay of a few hours to a few days.

Government evidence is no brighter. A large randomized rollout in the New York City Police Department trained about 14,700 officers. It found moderate knowledge gains, and it found insufficient evidence that disparities in enforcement actions shrank. Police work differs from human services, so be careful with that comparison. But the evaluators said something useful: training is likely to do more when it is backed by other forces in the organization, and they named immediate supervisors as perhaps the most important. In December 2020, UK ministers concluded that unconscious bias training did not achieve its aims and announced they would phase it out of the Civil Service and fold inclusion into core training. Critics called that premature, and a press report said some training continued. The approach itself has not been evaluated.

### Accordion
Item: Doesn't this mean training is useless?
Body: No. Knowledge matters. You cannot route an accommodation request you do not recognize. You cannot apply Muldrow if you have never heard of it. The point is that knowing is not enough. The research supports teaching skills tied to real decisions, with practice, spacing and follow-up.

Item: Is mandatory or voluntary better?
Body: The evidence conflicts and no one should tell you otherwise. Dobbin and Kalev's long-running data on over 800 US firms link mandatory manager training with no gain, and with declines for some groups, and link voluntary training with gains. That is an association, not a randomized result, and the firms were private. Bezrukova found no significant overall difference, with different results by outcome. The researchers behind one review say the mechanisms have not been established. No randomized test of mandatory against voluntary diversity training was found.

Item: Does a voluntary app reach the people who need it?
Body: Possibly not. One study found that more competent trainees were more likely to attend voluntary diversity training. A voluntary course reaches people already inclined to take it, and the app has no way to change that. If that describes you, the useful question is what you do with your decisions, not whether others have read this.

Item: Could training cause harm?
Body: Backfire is documented, mostly in laboratory studies. Field trials more often show nothing than harm. The more likely risk is no behavior change, plus the false comfort of having addressed the problem. Lesson 4 returns to this.

### Statement
This course will not make you fair. Your decisions, and how you check them, might.

### Text
So here is what this app is and is not. It is voluntary. It does not satisfy Minn. Stat. 43A.21. The state's own training does that. It keeps no scores and tells no one what you did. It cannot claim to reduce disparities, and it does not. The research for this course found no rigorous evaluation of equity training for public-sector supervisors in human services, and no randomized study that trains supervisors and measures retention of their staff by race, gender or disability. You are working in a field with real gaps in its evidence.

What it can do is give you knowledge, practice with realistic decisions, and tools you can use the same day. The evidence suggests the rest depends on what you do next.

### Leader move
Control: You control what you do with a course after you close it.
Failure: You treat finishing as the result. The content feels useful, nothing about your decisions changes, and you feel better anyway.
Next: Before you finish this course, name the one decision you will change. If you cannot name one, the course has not yet done anything for your team.

### Scenario
Context: Your unit's manager asks each supervisor what they are doing about equity this year. You have completed several courses in this app and found them helpful. You are drafting your reply.
Prompt: What do you write?
Option A: Report the courses you took and say your team is now in good shape on equity.
Response A: This presents learning as a result. It also invites the false assurance that research warns about. Nothing here shows anything changed for your team.
Option B: Say training has been shown not to work, so you are not doing anything further.
Response B: The evidence shows limits, not that the subject should be dropped. Supervisors make decisions every day whether they study them or not.
Option C: Name one or two decisions you are changing, such as how stretch work is assigned, how you will check them, and what you do not yet know.
Response C: This is honest about the limits of learning, ties the effort to real decisions, and gives you something you can look at later.
Recommended: C

### Knowledge check
Question: What is the most accurate summary of the evidence on equity training?
Option A: It reliably changes workplace outcomes when programs are long enough.
Option B: It reliably teaches people things and shifts what they report soon afterward, while evidence of lasting change in job behavior and outcomes is weak.
Option C: Studies show it always backfires.
Correct answer: B
Feedback correct: Right. The strongest field studies show small, narrow or fading effects on behavior, and clearer effects on knowledge.
Feedback incorrect: Longer programs show larger effects in reviews, but mostly on near-term and partly self-reported measures. Backfire is documented, mostly in laboratory work, and field trials more often show no effect than harm.

### Transfer prompt
If I finish a course and feel I have made progress, then I will ask which decision changed, and I will name one before I count the learning as done.

## Lesson 2: Where equity gets decided

Summary: Fair treatment on your team is set less by policy statements than by a run of small decisions you make: who gets time, work, recognition, a rating, a chance.
Minutes: 12
Objectives:
- Define equity in terms of the behavior this course asks of you.
- Identify the everyday decisions that shape careers on your team.
- Explain what the evidence says about supervisors' effects on staff.
- Identify your own highest-stakes recurring decisions.
Takeaways:
- Your people-management decisions are among the better-supported influences on whether staff stay.
- Equal ratings do not guarantee equal rewards unless someone checks.
- Time with you is itself a resource you hand out.

### Text
A word on what this course means by equity, since people use the term many ways. Here it means three things you can do. Apply one standard to everyone, in fact and not only on paper. Find and remove barriers that stop people from doing the work or being seen doing it. Look at the results of your decisions and change your process when they show a problem. This is direct, ordinary work, and it covers everyone on your team. It requires no one to adopt a belief. It also does not mean turning a blind eye when your unit's pattern falls along lines of race, disability, gender or language.

The evidence on supervisors is stronger than the evidence on training. Hoffman and Tadelis, 2021, studied a large technology firm. Their designs support a causal reading that better people-management skills in supervisors lead to lower turnover among their staff. Lazear and colleagues found, in another single firm, that better bosses raise output and that workers under them are less likely to leave. These are private-sector firms, so treat them as a guide and not a promise for a state agency. Still, the supervisor's effect on whether someone stays is one of the better-supported claims in this whole field.

The supervisor's daily habits matter too. Cullen and Perez-Truglia, 2023, used manager rotation at a financial firm. More face time with a manager raised promotion rates, enough to possibly explain about a third of that firm's gender promotion gap. You probably do not think of who you chat with as an equity decision. In that firm, it may have worked like one.

And the formal step does not close the gap on its own. Castilla, 2008, studied personnel data at one large employer. Gaps in salary growth by gender, race and nationality persisted among people with the same performance rating. So the gap can open after the rating, when someone decides what the rating is worth.

### Tabs
Tab: Assignments
Body: Who gets the waiver redesign, the legislative briefing, the hard county call. Who gets note-taking and the shared drive. These decide what someone will have to show at their next interview.

Tab: Time and access
Body: Who gets a regular one-to-one that actually happens. Whose ideas you carry to the director. Who you think of when something opens up.

Tab: Ratings and rewards
Body: What you write, how you back it up, what follows from it. Whether a rating means the same thing for two people.

Tab: Hiring
Body: Who is told about the opening. What you decide you are looking for, and when you decide it. How you score.

Tab: Access
Body: Whether you recognize an accommodation request when you hear one. Whether a colleague who uses an interpreter can take part in the meeting or only receive the minutes. Whether your documents can be read.

Tab: Discipline and complaints
Body: What you overlook for one person and write up for another. What you do in the hour after someone tells you about a problem.

### List
Why these belong to you and not to the equity office:
- You make them, often without anyone else seeing.
- They happen many times a year, so small patterns add up.
- Policy cannot reach them unless someone uses it at the moment of the decision.
- The law now reaches them too. After Muldrow and Ames, a routine transfer or schedule change motivated by a protected trait can be challenged, by anyone. See the course on the rules for fall 2026.
- The same habits help the person who was overlooked, and protect you and the person who was chosen.

### Statement
Equity is decided in the assignment you make before lunch.

### Leader move
Control: You control who gets your time, your attention and your recommendations.
Failure: You rely on who comes to mind first. It feels like judgment. The same few names keep coming up, and you cannot say why.
Next: Look at the last month. Write down whose one-to-ones actually happened, who you recommended, and who got the visible work. Look at what you find before you decide what it means.

### Scenario
Context: You supervise a team of case consultants. A director asks you to name someone to join a statewide workgroup on direct support, which will be visible and useful for promotion. One consultant came to mind immediately because you talk most days. Two others have relevant experience, but you know less about their current work. You need to reply by tomorrow.
Prompt: What do you do next?
Option A: Name the person who came to mind. You know their work best, and that is a legitimate reason.
Response A: Knowing someone's work best may be a product of how you spend your time, not of who is best placed. You have not checked anyone else.
Option B: Write down what the workgroup needs, tell the team about it today, ask who is interested, and choose against what you wrote.
Response B: This is quick, it fits the deadline, and it gives you a reason you can show anyone. It also tells you things about your team you did not know.
Option C: Name the consultant who is most different from the rest of the group, so that the workgroup is more diverse.
Response C: Choosing an individual by protected trait is the kind of decision Title VII reaches, whichever direction it goes. Widen who can be considered, then choose on the work.
Option D: Decline to name anyone, so that you avoid being unfair.
Response D: This removes an opportunity from the whole team. Not deciding is still a decision, and it costs them something.
Recommended: B

### Knowledge check
Question: What did Castilla's 2008 study of personnel data suggest about performance ratings?
Option A: Equal ratings ensured equal pay growth across groups.
Option B: Gaps in salary growth by gender, race and nationality persisted among employees with the same ratings.
Option C: Ratings were the only point at which gaps could arise.
Correct answer: B
Feedback correct: Right. That study was at one employer, and it shows why looking at what follows a rating matters as much as the rating.
Feedback incorrect: That study found gaps among employees with equal ratings. So a fair-looking rating does not settle the question. What follows it needs a look too.

### Transfer prompt
If an opportunity comes across my desk and a name comes to mind right away, then I will write down what the work requires and who else could do it before I reply.

## Lesson 3: Tools beat good intentions

Summary: Instruments you use at the moment of a decision have better evidence than awareness. They also have limits, and imposed rules can backfire if people work around them.
Minutes: 13
Objectives:
- Name the decision tools with the best evidence.
- Explain why reviewable decisions matter as well as rules.
- Recognize that formal tools do not automatically help.
- Pick one tool to use on one decision.
Takeaways:
- Structure the decision, then make it reviewable.
- A rule with no one looking at the results may do little.
- Do not assume a popular fix works. Check it where you are.

### Text
Good intentions are not enough, because good intentions are present whether or not the decision goes well. Here is what has somewhat better support. None of it is a magic answer.

Structured interviews. Reviews rank them as a leading selection method, although that ranking has faced criticism. Huffcutt and Roth, 1998, found smaller group differences in ratings in high-structure interviews. McCarthy and colleagues, 2010, looked at nearly 20,000 applicants at one organization. In highly structured interviews, neither applicant race or gender nor similarity to the interviewer affected ratings. That study was observational. No randomized study links structured interviews to who ends up represented in a workforce.

Rating design. Rivera and Tilcsik, 2019, found that changing a rating scale from 10 points to 6 changed a gender gap in teaching ratings in male-dominated fields. Bohnet and colleagues showed in the lab that comparing candidates side by side moved people from stereotypes toward performance. Correll and colleagues, 2020, found that the same language in reviews, such as "taking charge," went with higher ratings for men and not for women. Those studies were in universities, in the lab and in a corporate data set, so use them as prompts, not as rules.

Accountability. Castilla, 2015, reports that reward gaps for women, minorities and non-US-born staff shrank at one company after it added accountability and transparency procedures. That was a before-and-after study with no comparison group. Kalev, Dobbin and Kelly, 2006, looking at 708 private establishments over decades, found that establishing responsibility had the broadest associations with gains, and that it improved how other programs worked. Training was among the least effective on its own. That is observational too, but the pattern points the same way. In an experiment outside equity work, Friebel and colleagues found that when a CEO simply asked store managers to do what they could about turnover, turnover fell by about a quarter for nine months.

### Accordion
Item: Why do some formal tools backfire?
Body: Dobbin and colleagues, 2015, reported on 816 workplaces. Some reforms meant to limit discretion, such as job tests and certain performance evaluation systems, appeared to activate discrimination instead of reducing it. Written job tests for managers were followed by declines for several groups. So formalization does not help automatically.

Item: So are rules good or bad?
Body: A reasonable reading, though not a tested result, is that rules imposed with no engagement invite workarounds, while rules plus decisions someone can review actually constrain what happens. Do not just adopt a form. Use it, and let the results be seen.

Item: What about blind resume review?
Body: Results are mixed. French firms that opted into anonymous resumes became less likely to interview and hire minority candidates in a randomized trial. Australian public servants in an exercise shortlisted identified minority candidates more often than de-identified ones. Check how your own process works before importing a fix that sounds right.

Item: What about telling people that everyone has bias?
Body: Duguid and Thomas-Hunt found in the lab that telling people stereotyping is common increased stereotype expression, and that adding a message that most people work to overcome it reduced this. If you talk about bias, talk about effort and what people do about it.

### List
Tools you can start without anyone's permission:
- Assignment log: list significant and routine work, note who did it, and review it monthly.
- One-to-one schedule: same frequency, same core agenda, and note the ones you cancel.
- Criteria first: before you pick anyone for anything, write what you are looking for.
- Interview guide: same questions, scored at the time, on a scale you set before you met anyone. Follow your Human Resources process.
- Side-by-side rating check: put two reviews next to each other. Compare the words used and the evidence cited.
- Decision note: one sentence of job-related reason, written at the time.
- Access check: before any meeting, ask whether everyone can follow and contribute.

### Statement
Write the criteria first. Then let someone see the result, even if the someone is you next month.

### Leader move
Control: You control whether your decisions have a record and criteria, at least in your own unit.
Failure: You adopt the form, fill it in after you have decided, and treat the paperwork as proof of fairness.
Next: Pick the tool that fits the next decision on your calendar. Use it once before the end of the week, and notice whether it changed anything.

### Scenario
Context: You are writing year-end reviews for six program staff. On your own first drafts, you notice you wrote "strong presence" about one person and "needs to speak up more" about another whose written work shaped two policy decisions. You have an assignment log for only the last quarter.
Prompt: What do you do next?
Option A: Leave the reviews as written. They are your honest impression, and that is what reviews are for.
Response A: Impressions matter, but "presence" is hard to tie to work and easy to apply unevenly. You have noticed something, and you can check it.
Option B: Rewrite so that everyone's reviews sound similar and contain no criticism.
Response B: This hides the issue and leaves people without useful feedback. Uniform praise is not the same as a fair standard.
Option C: Put the drafts side by side, replace style comments with specific work and its effect, keep any real development goal with an example, and start your assignment log earlier next year.
Response C: This uses a simple tool at the moment of the decision. It keeps your standards and ties them to evidence, and it does not ask anyone to change who they are.
Recommended: C

### Knowledge check
Question: What is the best-supported reading of the evidence on formal decision tools?
Option A: Any written process removes bias from a decision.
Option B: Structured tools have better support than awareness, but formalization does not automatically help, so the results need review.
Option C: Formal tools always backfire, so judgment alone is safer.
Correct answer: B
Feedback correct: Right. Structured interviews and rating design have some good evidence. Some reforms have been followed by declines, so check what actually happens.
Feedback incorrect: Studies show both benefit and backfire. Unchecked judgment has its own record: gaps persist among people with equal ratings. Use the tool and review the results.

### Transfer prompt
If I am about to make a hire, assignment or rating, then I will write my criteria first and look at the results across my team afterward, so that the check is built in.

## Lesson 4: Feedback, follow-through, and the false finish

Summary: Successful supervisor efforts share a pattern: specific behaviors, practice, and feedback from your own team. The biggest risk is deciding you are done. This lesson covers how to keep going without scores or anyone watching.
Minutes: 12
Objectives:
- Describe the ingredients that recur in supervisor interventions that worked.
- Explain false assurance and how to guard against it.
- Explain why an average can hide harm to some people.
- Ask your team for feedback without forcing disclosure.
Takeaways:
- Feedback from your own team has unusually good evidence.
- Having a program can make people see an organization as fairer than it is.
- A change can help some people and harm others. Check, and do not assume.

### Text
Look at the supervisor programs that did change something. None relies on bias awareness. They share other ingredients.

Zohar and Polachek, 2014, ran a randomized trial with no course at all. Supervisors got two individual feedback sessions, based on what their own workers said about recent messages. Safety climate, teamwork and independently audited safety scores improved. That was about safety, not equity. It still shows how far feedback from your own team can go.

Kelly and colleagues, 2014, trained supervisors and changed work practices together in a randomized IT workplace trial. They found modest improvements in work-family conflict and larger changes in schedule control and supervisor support. Taylor and colleagues, 2005, reviewed 117 studies of training that models behavior. Transfer to the job was strongest with both good and poor examples, practice scenarios the trainees wrote themselves, goal setting, and bosses who had also been trained. And job behavior held up over time even as recall of facts faded.

Now the warning. Hammer and colleagues, 2011, trained grocery supervisors in family-supportive behavior. Outcomes improved for employees with high family-to-work conflict and got worse for those with low conflict. An average near zero can hide both gains and harms. Do not assume your change helped everyone.

### Accordion
Item: False assurance
Body: Kaiser and colleagues, 2013, ran six experiments. When organizations had diversity structures, including training, high-status people saw them as fairer even with clear evidence of unfairness, and they reacted more harshly to people who claimed discrimination. So after a course, watch for your own impulse to say, "We handle that here." If someone raises a problem soon after you have done the work on the subject, listen harder, not less.

Item: Framing that backfires
Body: Legault and colleagues, 2011, found in lab experiments that pressure-based messages about prejudice increased prejudice, compared with no intervention, and that messages supporting people's own motivation reduced it. Do not lead with legal threat when you talk to your team, and never treat one group of staff as the problem.

Item: Supervisors as helpers
Body: Dobbin and Kalev, 2019, looked at 805 companies over 32 years. Employee training and grievance procedures were followed by fewer women managers. Manager training was followed by more. This is correlational, and it needs caution. One reading, not a tested result, is that it helps to treat supervisors as people who step in and fix things, not as suspects.

### List
Asking your team, without creating harm:
- Ask about the work, not about identity. Never ask anyone to speak for a group.
- Ask everyone the same questions, such as: What gets in your way? Is there work you would like a chance at? Do you know how assignments get decided here? What should I do differently?
- Offer more than one way to answer, including in writing and later.
- Do not ask anyone to disclose a disability, a background or a personal experience.
- Report back what you heard and what you will do. Then do it.
- In a small team, patterns by race, disability or gender can identify a person. Do not build a breakdown that exposes someone. Look at the process instead.
- Do not retaliate, or seem to, when you hear something you do not like.

### Text
A note on what to watch. If you want to know whether anything is changing, completion and satisfaction will not tell you. They would look fine either way. Look at things you control: whose one-to-ones happened, who got which work, who stayed, who asked to leave your team, and whether concerns are reaching you sooner or not at all. This app will not do this for you and will not report it to anyone. It is your own record. Keep it free of medical or other private details, and follow Human Resources guidance on anything that belongs in a personnel record.

### Statement
You are never finished. Keep asking.

### Leader move
Control: You control whether you ask, and how you respond when the answer is uncomfortable.
Failure: You ask once, get polite answers, and take silence as evidence that all is well.
Next: In your next round of one-to-ones, ask every person the same two questions about the work, write down what you hear, and bring one change back to the team.

### Scenario
Context: You have spent six months using an assignment log and regular one-to-ones. You are pleased with the result. A consultant now tells you that the unit's informal Friday lunch is where much of the real planning happens, and that those who work remotely or have care duties miss it. You had not heard this before, and your first reaction is that you have already done a lot.
Prompt: What do you do next?
Option A: Point to your log and one-to-ones, and explain that the process is now fair.
Response A: This is the false assurance the research describes. Your tools do not cover this, and you have just discouraged the next person from telling you something.
Option B: Thank the consultant, ask what decisions have been made at those lunches, make sure planning decisions move to a channel everyone can reach, and check with the team whether that works.
Response B: This treats the report as useful information, changes the process for everyone, and lets the lunch stay as a social occasion rather than a place where decisions get made.
Option C: Cancel the lunch so no one is left out.
Response C: This removes something people value without addressing where decisions happen, and it may make the consultant the person blamed for losing it.
Recommended: B

### Knowledge check
Question: What did Hammer and colleagues' 2011 study of supervisor training show that matters to you?
Option A: Supervisor training has no effect on employees.
Option B: Outcomes improved for some employees and worsened for others, so an average can hide harm.
Option C: Self-paced training is always better than group training.
Correct answer: B
Feedback correct: Right. Check how a change is landing for different people, within the limits of privacy in a small team.
Feedback incorrect: The study found effects going in different directions for different employees. It did not show zero effect, and it did not settle self-paced against group formats.

### Transfer prompt
If I find myself saying that my team has dealt with fairness, then I will ask one person a new question about how work actually gets decided here, and I will act on what I hear.

## Lesson 5: What is yours, what is not, and starting Monday

Summary: Your authority has limits. Some inequities are in systems you cannot change alone. This lesson sorts what you own from what you should pass up, and helps you build a small plan you can actually keep.
Minutes: 12
Objectives:
- Separate what you can change from what you need to escalate.
- Use disparity data as a prompt rather than a decision rule.
- Write a specific plan tied to a real decision.
- Plan for what you will do when the plan slips.
Takeaways:
- Do not take on the whole system, and do not use the system as an excuse.
- Raise patterns with facts and a specific request.
- If-then plans with a real date beat general intentions.

### Text
You cannot fix everything from your position. You did not set the pay plan, the job classes, the waiver rules or the state's hiring system. People of color, disabled staff and others can face barriers that reach far beyond your unit, and a supervisor who says otherwise is misleading the team. But the structural nature of a problem is not a reason to leave your own decisions unexamined. Both things are true.

Sort it this way. Some decisions are yours, such as assignments, time, what you write, and how fast you move a request. Some you share with Human Resources or labor relations, such as discipline, hiring steps and work rules. Check the contract first. Only the MAPE 2025-2027 contract was read for this course, so read your own for AFSCME, MMA or the Managerial Plan. Some are above you, such as budget, classification and agency policy. For those, your job is to see the pattern and raise it well.

### Tabs
Tab: Yours
Body: Who gets the visible work and the routine work. When you meet and what you ask. What you write in a review. How quickly you forward an accommodation request. How you run a meeting. Reporting a concern today.

Tab: Shared
Body: Discipline. Hiring process. Schedule changes under a contract. New work rules. Ask Human Resources or labor relations before you act, and act once you know.

Tab: To raise
Body: A job class that seems to block some people. A form or a system that does not work with a screen reader. Interpreter funding you cannot find. A pattern across units. Bring facts, name the request, and say who is affected without identifying anyone.

Tab: Not to take on alone
Body: An investigation. A medical determination. A legal judgment. Speaking for a community you do not belong to. Asking the one person who shares a background to fix it for you.

### Accordion
Item: What do I do with data showing a gap?
Body: Treat it as a question. Ask what steps produce it: who is told, what the form requires, how long it takes, who decides. Then change those steps for everyone. This is a recommendation based on the current law as described in the rules course. Disparity data is a prompt to review process, and it is never a reason to make an individual decision based on race.

Item: How do I raise something without burning trust?
Body: Describe what you saw, and when. Say what it costs the work. Name one thing you are asking for. If your manager cannot act, ask who can. Keep a dated note for yourself, without private details. Your manager is more likely to act on a specific request than on a general concern.

Item: What if I am the only one doing this?
Body: You may be. Some research points to benefits from peers and from trained bosses, but you do not need either to run your own log. If you want company, an optional peer check-in with another supervisor can help. Do not make it about individual employees.

Item: What if I belong to a group that is underrepresented here?
Body: You did not become responsible for all of it. Doing this work is part of every supervisor's job. Your own load has its own course in this set.

### Statement
Small and specific, on a real date, beats large and general.

### List
Write your plan. Keep it to these four lines, for yourself only.
- The decision: one recurring decision you own. For example: who gets legislative briefings.
- The tool: one thing you will use. For example: write what the work needs, tell the team, then pick.
- The date: the next time it comes up, or a date you put on your calendar.
- The check: what you will look at and when. For example: the log at 90 days, and two questions in one-to-ones.

Do not submit this anywhere. This app does not collect it. Do not include anyone's name or private details.

### Leader move
Control: You control one decision, one tool and one date.
Failure: You write a sweeping commitment, nothing changes by March, and you conclude that this work is impossible.
Next: Write your four lines now. If the plan slips, restart it at the next instance. No one is scoring you.

### Scenario
Context: Using your log, you find that staff in one job class on your team rarely get visible work, because the class definition limits who can present to the commissioner's office. Many in that class are people of color, and some have raised this with you. You do not control class definitions.
Prompt: What do you do next?
Option A: Tell staff it is above your level and leave it there.
Response A: It may well be above your level, but you can still raise it and change what you control. Stopping here tells people that there is no point telling you.
Option B: Quietly let people from that class present anyway, and do not mention it to anyone, regardless of what the job rules say.
Response B: Well meant, but you may be stepping outside the class rules or the contract, and you put those people at risk. Check first.
Option C: Check with Human Resources what the class allows, open up every part of the visible work you can, and bring your manager a short written note describing the pattern, its cost to the work, and one specific request.
Response C: This acts where you have authority, checks the limit rather than assuming it, and passes the rest up in a form someone can act on. Tell staff what you did and what you do not yet know.
Recommended: C

### Knowledge check
Question: What is the most useful way to use a data pattern showing a gap on your team?
Option A: As a prompt to review the steps that produce it, changing those steps for everyone and raising what you cannot change.
Option B: As a reason to choose the next person from the group with the lower numbers.
Option C: As a reason to stop looking, since the cause is structural.
Correct answer: A
Feedback correct: Right. Review the process, change what you own, and raise the rest with specifics.
Feedback incorrect: Choosing an individual by protected trait is the kind of decision the law reaches, in any direction. Stopping because the cause is structural leaves your own process unexamined. Review the steps instead.

### Transfer prompt
If I see a pattern on my team that I cannot fix by myself, then I will change what I control this week, check the limits with Human Resources or labor relations, and bring my manager one specific written request by the end of the month.

## Job aid

Purpose: A one-page reminder of why this is a supervisor's work, what evidence supports, and what to do next.

Remember:
- Training teaches. Your decisions decide.
- Apart from the Equity Analysis Toolkit, which is required of all DHS staff, this app is voluntary. It keeps no scores, sends nothing to your supervisor or employer, and does not satisfy Minn. Stat. 43A.21.
- Nothing here promises to reduce disparities. Check what happens in your own unit.

Sections:

What the evidence supports
- Knowledge and near-term attitudes can move. Lasting behavior and outcomes are harder.
- Awareness alone has a poor record.
- Better support: structured decisions, visible criteria, named responsibility, and feedback from your own team.
- Mandatory versus voluntary is unsettled. Formal tools can backfire without review.

Where equity gets decided
- Who gets your time.
- Who gets visible and routine work.
- What you write, and what follows from a rating.
- How you hire.
- How fast you move requests and reports.
- Whether everyone can take part in the meeting.

Four habits
- Write the criteria first.
- Keep a simple record.
- Ask your team the same questions, about the work.
- Look at the result and change the process.

Watch for
- "We handle that here" after a course or a program.
- An average that hides who was harmed.
- Paperwork filled in after you have decided.
- Asking one person to speak for a group.

Do next
- Write your four lines: decision, tool, date, check.
- Use the tool once this week.
- Look at it again in 90 days.
