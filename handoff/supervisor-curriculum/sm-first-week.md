# sm-first-week: Your First Week as a Supervisor: What You Now Answer For

**Subtitle:** The duties that start on day one, what to do when a report or request reaches you, and whom to call before you have learned everything else.
**Duration:** About 60 minutes across five lessons. Take them in short sittings.
**Content type:** foundation
**Status:** reviewed
**Last reviewed:** October 31, 2026
**Evidence date:** October 2026
**Scope note:** This is general information as of October 2026, not legal advice. This course is voluntary and does not count as the training Minn. Stat. 43A.21 requires; the state's own training does that.

**Course objectives:**
- Name the decisions and duties that became yours on the day you started supervising.
- Respond to a report of harassment or discrimination by reporting it immediately, without trying to settle it yourself.
- Recognize an accommodation request in plain words and move it forward promptly.
- Find the labor agreement that covers each person you supervise and check it before you change their work.
- Build a one-page list of the people to call, and set up one fair weekly habit.

**Applied next step:** Before the end of your first week, fill in the job aid at the end of this course with real names and contacts from your own division. Keep it where you can reach it during a hard conversation. Keep it out of this app, which is not a place for names.

## Lesson 1: The day the job changed

**Summary:** You may be doing much of the same work as last week, but your decisions now count as the employer's decisions. This lesson shows what that changes, and why equity shows up first in ordinary choices.
**Minutes:** 12
**Objectives:**
- Describe how your role changed when you became a supervisor.
- List the everyday decisions that now affect people's terms of employment.
- Explain why ordinary decisions need stated reasons.
- Handle a request from a former peer without giving up the relationship.

**Takeaways:**
- Small decisions are now employment decisions.
- The law applies the same standard to every employee, in every direction.
- Say your reason in terms of the work, and say it before you know whom it favors.

### Text: You now act for the employer

Last week you could notice a problem and talk it over with a coworker. This week, what you do about it counts. You decide who gets which case, when people work, what gets written about them, and whether a problem reaches the people who must handle it.

Look at the employer rights in Article 5 of the MAPE 2025-2027 contract. They include the right to "select, assign, direct, evaluate and promote employees." You may not hold each of those powers alone. But a good share of them now pass through your desk.

Most people feel this change as loss before they feel it as responsibility. You may no longer be in the group chat. A friend may go quiet when you walk in. That is normal. It does not mean you did anything wrong. It means the job changed.

### Text: Why equity starts here

In this course, equity means something specific you can do. Every person gets a fair shot under the same stated criteria. Barriers that shut people out without a work reason get removed. Harm gets reported and handled. Access needs get met. Minnesota's statute uses the words "inclusive work environments, disability awareness, cultural competence" and "equity and diversity" for what supervisors must be trained on. Those words become real in the decisions below.

Two U.S. Supreme Court decisions make the point about ordinary choices:

- *Muldrow v. City of St. Louis* (2024) held that a person challenging a transfer must show "some harm" to a term or condition of employment. The harm need not be significant. In that case, rank and pay stayed the same, but duties, schedule and perks changed.
- *Ames v. Ohio Department of Youth Services* (2025) held that majority-group employees do not face a higher bar under Title VII. One standard applies to everyone.

Read together, these cases mean an assignment or schedule change can matter legally, whoever the person is. The practical answer is simple: use consistent criteria and keep a short record. You will practice this in the course on fair assignments.

### Statement

You do not need to know every rule this week. You need to know which moments you cannot handle alone, and whom to call when they come.

### Text: What the evidence says about you, and about this course

Research on supervisors gives you a real reason to care about this. In one high-tech firm, designs using new hires and manager moves support a causal link between people-management skill and lower turnover. In one financial institution, more face-to-face time with a manager raised promotion rates, and that could explain a third of the firm's gender promotion gap. Neither study covers state government or disability services, so treat them as signals and not as forecasts for your team.

The evidence on training is more humbling. Courses reliably change what people know and say soon afterward. Evidence that they change job behavior is much weaker. So this course will not promise you a fairer team. It gives you tools tied to specific decisions, and the tools only work if you use them when the decision arrives.

### Leader move: Former peers

**Control:** You control whether old arrangements continue because they were fair or only because they were familiar.
**Failure:** Do not keep a friend's special arrangement to prove you have not changed. Do not cancel everything to prove you have.
**Next:** List any arrangements you inherited. For each, write the work reason in one line. Where you cannot, review it with the team's criteria in hand before you change anything.

### Scenario

**Context:** You were promoted this week to supervise the case consultants you used to work beside. An old teammate stops by. For two years the two of you informally let this person have first pick of the county visits closest to home. The teammate says, "You'll keep that going, right? You know my situation." The rest of the team does not know about the arrangement.
**Prompt:** What do you do next?

**Option:** Say yes for now. It is a small thing, and you need allies in your first week.
**Response:** This keeps an unstated preference and makes it yours. Visit assignments affect time, travel and schedule, so they are not too small to count. Others may read it as favoritism once they find out.

**Option:** End the arrangement today and tell the team you are ending favoritism.
**Response:** This overcorrects in public. It may embarrass a colleague, and you do not yet know whether a work reason, or an accommodation you were never told about, sits behind it.

**Option (recommended):** Say you will not decide on the spot. Ask if there is anything you should know about what the arrangement is for, then set out how visits will be assigned for everyone. If the person mentions a medical need, treat that as a possible accommodation request.
**Response:** This is the strongest move. It treats the colleague with respect, leaves room for a real need, and moves the decision to a method the whole team can see. A medical reason would go through the accommodation process and stay private.

**Option:** Ask the team to vote on whether the arrangement should continue.
**Response:** A vote exposes one person's arrangement, and any reason behind it, to coworkers. It also hands your decision to the group. Assignment criteria are your job to set.

### Knowledge check

**Question:** What does *Muldrow* mean for an everyday supervisor decision?
**Options:**
- A transfer only matters legally if pay or rank drops.
- A person challenging a transfer must show some harm to a term or condition of employment, and it need not be significant. (correct)
- Supervisors may no longer change anyone's duties without a court's approval.
- Only members of minority groups can challenge a transfer.

**Correct answer:** A person challenging a transfer must show some harm to a term or condition of employment, and it need not be significant.
**Feedback if correct:** Right. In *Muldrow*, pay and rank did not change, but duties, schedule and perks did. You can still change duties. Have a work reason and apply it consistently.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Look again. *Muldrow* set a low harm threshold. It did not bar changes, and *Ames* confirms that one standard applies to every employee.

### Transfer prompt

**Prompt:** Think of an arrangement or assignment you inherited this week. Complete this plan: "If someone asks me to keep [arrangement], then I will say [my sentence], and by [day] I will write down the work reason or review it."
**Plan starters:**
- "I want to understand what it is for before I decide anything."
- "I'll be setting out how this works for the whole team by Friday."
- "If there's a health reason, tell me only what you need changed, and I'll get it to the right person."

## Lesson 2: When someone tells you about harm

**Summary:** State policy gives supervisors a clear duty when they learn of harassment or discrimination: treat it seriously and report it immediately. This lesson teaches what to say and do in the first ten minutes.
**Minutes:** 13
**Objectives:**
- State the supervisor's reporting duty under Policies 1436 and 1329.
- Explain why you cannot hold a report in order to settle it informally.
- Respond to a request for secrecy honestly.
- Identify early actions that could look like retaliation.

**Takeaways:**
- Your duty starts when you become aware. It does not wait for a legal threshold.
- Promise privacy where you can. Do not promise secrecy.
- Pause any decision that could hurt the person until you have checked it.

### Text: The duty is short and clear

Minnesota Management and Budget's HR/LR Policy 1436 (harassment and discrimination) and Policy 1329 (sexual harassment) tell managers and supervisors to model appropriate behavior, treat reports seriously, respond when they become aware of a problem, and "immediately report" to Human Resources or the Affirmative Action Officer. Those who knowingly allow or tolerate this conduct are subject to discipline, up to discharge.

Three things follow.

First, your duty applies to you as well as to the people above you. Second, you do not need a formal complaint. Becoming aware is enough. Third, neither policy uses the phrase "severe or pervasive." That phrase comes from case law on legal liability. Your duty to report does not wait for conduct to reach that level.

If you do not yet know who your Affirmative Action Officer is, ask Human Resources today. Do not wait until you need the answer.

### List: The first ten minutes

- Listen. Let the person finish. Take brief notes of what they say, in their words.
- Thank them for telling you. You do not have to judge what happened.
- Be honest: "I have to report this to Human Resources. I'll keep it to the people who need to know."
- Ask if they feel safe at work today, and whether anything needs to change right away.
- Report it, the same day, to Human Resources or the Affirmative Action Officer.
- Tell the person what you did and who may contact them.
- Check your calendar for any decision about this person that is coming up. Pause it and check it.

### Accordion: Things that feel helpful and are not

**Item: "I'll talk to them quietly and handle it."**
Your conversation may tip off the person involved and shape what witnesses say later. It also skips the required report. Policy gives you a reporting job. It does not give you an investigating job.

**Item: "They asked me not to tell, so I can't."**
You can respect their fear and still report. Tell them so, plainly, before they say more if you can. Then tell the person receiving your report what the employee is worried about.

**Item: "I'll wait to see if it happens again."**
"Immediately" does not mean "after the second time." Waiting can also leave you among those who knowingly allowed it.

**Item: "Let me look through the team chat first."**
Gathering evidence is not your first move. If you already have something, such as a message you saw, tell Human Resources. Ask them before you go looking for more.

### Text: Retaliation can begin by accident

The EEOC lists these among protected activities: reporting discrimination or harassment to a supervisor, taking part in an investigation, and asking for a disability or religious accommodation. It gives examples of retaliation that include a lower evaluation than deserved, a transfer to a less desirable position, more scrutiny of someone's work, and a schedule change that deliberately conflicts with family responsibilities.

You can still manage someone who has made a report. You may correct real problems for real reasons. But you should know that a new supervisor can cause a retaliation problem by accident, simply by carrying out a plan that was already underway. So check the timing, and talk to Human Resources before you act.

Minnesota's data practices law also protects the identity of harassment complainants and witnesses in certain ways (Minn. Stat. 13.43, subd. 8). You do not need to master this. Just do not share who said what, and ask Human Resources if someone presses you.

### Leader move: The sentence you need ready

**Control:** You control what you say in the first moment, before you are ready.
**Failure:** Do not say "This stays between us." Do not say "Are you sure that's what they meant?"
**Next:** Write your own version of this sentence and practice it aloud: "Thank you for telling me. I have to take this to Human Resources today, and I'll tell you what happens next."

### Scenario

**Context:** A policy specialist on your team tells you that a senior coworker keeps making remarks about the specialist's religion and head covering, in the office and on calls. The specialist says, "Please don't make this a thing. I just wanted someone to know." You are also about to move the specialist's main project to the same senior coworker, a plan your manager approved last month.
**Prompt:** What do you do next?

**Option (recommended):** Thank the specialist, explain that you have to report this today and what will happen next, report it to Human Resources or the Affirmative Action Officer, and hold the project move until you have raised it with Human Resources.
**Response:** This meets the reporting duty and is honest about it. Holding the project move protects against a decision that could harm the person who spoke up, or put them under closer contact with the coworker.

**Option:** Respect the request. Note it privately and watch for more remarks. Go ahead with the project move, since it was approved before the report.
**Response:** Policy says to report immediately, and a request for silence does not remove that duty. The earlier approval does not settle the timing problem either. The move may look like a consequence of speaking up.

**Option:** Speak to the senior coworker today and tell them to stop. Tell the specialist it is handled.
**Response:** The instinct to protect is good, but this replaces the required route with your own. It can also compromise any later review. "It is handled" promises something you do not know.

**Option:** Ask the specialist to put it in writing first, so that you have something to report.
**Response:** A report does not need to be in writing before you act. Requiring it puts the burden on the person who is already worried, and it delays your duty.

### Knowledge check

**Question:** Under Policies 1436 and 1329, when must a supervisor report possible harassment or discrimination?
**Options:**
- After confirming the conduct is severe or pervasive.
- After the employee files a written complaint.
- Immediately, once aware. (correct)
- At the next regular meeting with Human Resources.

**Correct answer:** Immediately, once aware.
**Feedback if correct:** Yes. The policies say "immediately report" to Human Resources or the Affirmative Action Officer, and they do not use the "severe or pervasive" standard.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Not quite. The duty does not wait for a formal complaint or a legal threshold. Report immediately to Human Resources or the Affirmative Action Officer.

### Transfer prompt

**Prompt:** Finish this plan with real contacts from your division: "If someone tells me about harassment or discrimination, then I will say [my sentence], report it the same day to [role and contact], and check my calendar for decisions affecting that person."
**Plan starters:**
- Save the contact for your Human Resources partner.
- Ask Human Resources who your Affirmative Action Officer is.
- Check which decisions you are planning this month, so you could spot a conflict quickly.

## Lesson 3: When someone asks for a change for a medical reason

**Summary:** Accommodation requests rarely sound like requests. This lesson teaches you to recognize one, keep it moving and protect the person's privacy.
**Minutes:** 13
**Objectives:**
- Recognize a request made without legal words.
- Describe your first steps under HR/LR Policy 1433.
- Explain the risk of sitting on a request.
- Keep medical information out of your questions and records.

**Takeaways:**
- No special words are needed.
- Move it forward promptly, and tell the person who decides.
- Ask what would help. Do not ask for a diagnosis.

### Text: It will not sound like a request

Policy 1433, the state's ADA reasonable accommodation policy, defines a request as "any communication in which an individual asks or states that he or she needs the agency to provide or change something because of a medical condition." The person does not have to use any special words or mention the ADA, "reasonable accommodation" or "disability." The request can be oral, and a third party such as a family member or health professional can make it if the person is unable to.

So listen for sentences like these:

- "Since my treatment started, I can't do the 8 a.m. calls."
- "I can't read the new portal with my screen reader."
- "The office lights are giving me migraines. Could I sit somewhere else?"
- "My interpreter wasn't booked for the provider meeting again."

### List: What you do

- Acknowledge it. "Thanks for telling me. Let's get this moving."
- Ask what would help. "What needs to change for you to do this work?"
- Write down an oral request. Policy 1433 says oral requests must be documented in writing.
- Promptly forward it to the right decision maker, and tell the person who that is. Policy 1433 makes this the first step for whoever receives a request.
- Ask your ADA coordinator which requests you can approve yourself. The policy lets agencies decide what supervisors may approve, so ask about DHS's current practice.
- Follow up on timing. Tell the person if something is taking longer.

### Text: Why speed matters

EEOC guidance says unnecessary delay can violate the ADA, and that a supervisor who sits on a request can be treated as having effectively denied it. That guidance dates from 2002, before the ADA was amended, and it says it stays in effect until rescinded. As of October 2026, disability duties remain among the firmest ground a supervisor stands on.

Minnesota law also speaks to this. The Human Rights Act defines accommodation through an informal interactive process (Minn. Stat. 363A.08, subd. 6), and failing to engage in the process may be an unfair practice (363A.02, subd. 1(c)).

Policy 1433 requires the ADA coordinator to be consulted when questions or conflicts come up, and before a request is denied. So you do not decide alone to say no. The person may also ask to have the ADA coordinator, a union representative or a support person present. Article 31 of the MAPE contract also provides union representation at the employee's request.

### Tabs: Questions and privacy

**Tab: You may ask**
What is getting in the way of the work? What have you tried? What would help? How soon do you need it? Is it needed every time, or at certain times?

**Tab: Leave this to the process**
Do not ask for a diagnosis or a doctor's note. Under Policy 1433, where medical documentation is necessary, the ADA coordinator makes the request.

**Tab: What you may know**
Minnesota law keeps medical information in separate confidential files. Supervisors and managers may be told about necessary restrictions and accommodations (Minn. Stat. 363A.20, subd. 8). You need to know what changes. You rarely need to know why.

**Tab: What you tell the team**
Nothing about the reason. "I handle individual arrangements privately, for everyone." Under the MAPE contract, the appraisal form must not reference the ADA, FMLA or the Employee Assistance Program, either (Article 6, Section 3).

### Leader move: A standing need

**Control:** You control whether a regular need gets arranged once or has to be requested again and again.
**Failure:** Do not make someone ask every week for something that a repeating meeting will always need.
**Next:** Policy 1433 says a regular need, such as an interpreter for a weekly staff meeting, should be arranged without a request before each occasion. Look at your standing meetings this week, and fix any arrangement that depends on someone remembering.

### Scenario

**Context:** A case consultant says in passing, "My hands have been really bad since the summer. The new documentation system takes me twice as long. I'm behind, and I'm sorry." You had been planning to raise the backlog with them next week. You are not sure whether this is a real request or just a comment.
**Prompt:** What do you do next?

**Option:** Wait to see whether they raise it again. They did not actually ask for anything, and you do not want to pry.
**Response:** Policy 1433 says that when a supervisor learns an employee is having difficulty because of a medical condition, further inquiry may be required, and the supervisor should consult the ADA coordinator. Waiting can turn into sitting on a request.

**Option:** Ask what condition they have and whether a doctor has given them restrictions, so you can judge how serious it is.
**Response:** Those questions go beyond your role. If documentation is ever needed, the ADA coordinator asks for it through the process.

**Option:** Hold the backlog conversation as planned, so that the performance expectation stays clear, and suggest voice dictation if they think that would help.
**Response:** The expectation can stay clear later. Going first with the backlog ignores what you just heard, and a quick tip is not the process. The person may also experience this as a penalty for telling you.

**Option (recommended):** Say, "Thanks for telling me. Would a change in the system or how you do the work help? I'd like to get this to the right person." Write it down, contact the ADA coordinator promptly, and hold your backlog talk until you have spoken with them.
**Response:** This treats the comment as a possible request, stays on what would help, and gets advice before the performance talk. You can still address the work, with better information about what is causing the backlog.

### Knowledge check

**Question:** Who should ask an employee for medical documentation, if it is needed?
**Options:**
- The employee's immediate supervisor.
- The agency ADA coordinator. (correct)
- A coworker who works closely with the person.
- No one. Documentation can never be requested.

**Correct answer:** The agency ADA coordinator.
**Feedback if correct:** Correct. Policy 1433 says that where medical documentation is necessary, the agency ADA coordinator makes the request.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Not quite. Documentation can sometimes be requested when the need is not obvious, but under Policy 1433 the ADA coordinator does this. Your part is to forward the request and focus on what would help.

### Transfer prompt

**Prompt:** Plan for the next time you hear a hint. "If someone mentions a health or access problem affecting their work, then I will ask [my question], write it down, and contact [ADA coordinator's contact] within [time you commit to]."
**Plan starters:**
- Find out who your agency ADA coordinator is.
- Ask which requests DHS lets supervisors approve directly.
- List the standing meetings where a repeating access need should be arranged in advance.

## Lesson 4: Contracts, records and the people you call

**Summary:** Many good intentions go wrong because nobody checked the contract first. This lesson shows you how to find which agreement applies, what to check before changing someone's work, and how to think about what you write down.
**Minutes:** 12
**Objectives:**
- Find which labor agreement or plan covers each person you supervise.
- Name terms in the MAPE contract that affect first-week decisions.
- Check before making schedule changes or new work rules.
- Write records that are factual and limited to the work.

**Takeaways:**
- Know which agreement covers each person. It may differ across your team.
- Check first. Notice periods are easy to miss.
- What you write can become an official record.

### Text: One team, maybe several agreements

The people you supervise may be covered by different agreements. Under state labor law, general professional employees are in the MAPE unit. Other staff may be covered by AFSCME. Supervisors and managers have their own units or plans. This course draws on only one contract, MAPE 2025-2027, because that is the only one read for it. If your staff are covered by a different agreement, including AFSCME, MMA or the Managerial Plan, check that agreement itself. Do not assume the terms below carry over.

Ask Human Resources for a list of who is covered by what. Then look at the agreement yourself. Labor relations staff can tell you how it is applied here.

### Tabs: First-week terms in the MAPE 2025-2027 contract

**Tab: Schedules**
Article 27, Section 1(A): at least 14 calendar days' notice to the union and the employee before a permanent change in days, hours or length of the work day for full-time employees, unless the employee and the employer mutually agree to waive it.

**Tab: Telework**
Article 27, Section 1(I): if a request is denied, the employee can ask for the reasons. Before a supervisor or manager changes or denies an arrangement, the employer first meets with the employee. Give 21 days' notice of a change or cancellation when practicable. There is an appeal within 30 days, and it is not subject to the grievance procedure.

**Tab: Work rules**
Article 28: rules must be reasonable, not conflict with the contract and be applied without discrimination. New or amended rules are discussed with the union, and posted at least 10 working days ahead if practicable. A "team rule" you announce may be a work rule, so check.

**Tab: Questioning**
Article 8, Section 2: before questioning an employee during an investigation that may lead to their discipline, advise them of the nature of the investigation and offer union representation. See the course on corrective action.

**Tab: Position descriptions**
Article 6, Section 2: an employee can ask for a copy of their position description. Read each person's description this month. It helps you set fair expectations.

**Tab: Workload**
Article 27, Section 1(H): on the union's request, the employer meets and confers within 30 calendar days about concerns that employees cannot do their job duties because of increased workload.

### Text: What you write down

Keep short records of decisions that affect people. Stick to what happened, what you decided and the work reason. Avoid guesses about motive, personality, health or family.

Personnel data is covered by the Minnesota Government Data Practices Act. Some of it is public, such as the existence and status of complaints and the final disposition of discipline. "All other personnel data" is private (Minn. Stat. 13.43), and access within the agency is limited to people whose work assignments reasonably require it (Minn. R. 1205.0400). Ask Human Resources where notes should live and how long to keep them. Write each note as though the employee, a steward or a court might one day read it, because they might.

Do not put anyone's name or personnel details in this app or in its Ask feature. The app does not need them.

### Leader move: The call before the change

**Control:** You control whether labor relations hears about a change before or after you announce it.
**Failure:** Do not announce a schedule or rule change in a team meeting and then check the contract.
**Next:** Add one step to your habits: before any change to hours, duties or rules, ask "Who is covered by what, and what notice applies?" Then call.

### Scenario

**Context:** Your director asks you to have your team start phone coverage an hour earlier, permanently, starting next Monday. Today is Wednesday. Most of the team is covered by MAPE. Two people are covered by a different agreement. One person has an existing schedule arrangement. You do not know the reason for it.
**Prompt:** What do you do next?

**Option:** Email the team today so that they have as much notice as you can give, and deal with objections as they arise.
**Response:** Early notice is good, but this could miss the required notice for MAPE-covered staff and could conflict with terms you have not read. It also risks overriding an accommodation you do not know about.

**Option (recommended):** Call labor relations and Human Resources today. Ask what notice applies under each agreement, ask whether the arrangement affects anything, and go back to your director with a realistic start date.
**Response:** This is the strongest move. Under Article 27 of the MAPE contract a permanent hours change needs 14 calendar days' notice unless waived by mutual agreement. You give your director an accurate date and protect the existing arrangement until you know more.

**Option:** Ask for volunteers to start earlier and treat that as meeting the request.
**Response:** Volunteers might help in the short term, and some contracts may allow it. But this does not meet the permanent change requested, and it could shape who carries the early hours without any stated criteria. Check first.

### Knowledge check

**Question:** Under the MAPE 2025-2027 contract, how much notice is normally required for a permanent change in a full-time employee's hours of work?
**Options:**
- None, because scheduling is an employer right.
- At least 14 calendar days to the union and the affected employee, unless mutually waived. (correct)
- At least 30 working days to the whole department.
- Whatever notice the supervisor considers reasonable.

**Correct answer:** At least 14 calendar days to the union and the affected employee, unless mutually waived.
**Feedback if correct:** Correct, under Article 27, Section 1(A). Article 5 keeps scheduling as an employer right, and Article 27 sets the notice. Other agreements may differ, so check each one.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Check Article 27, Section 1(A). Employers can schedule working hours under Article 5, but a permanent change needs at least 14 calendar days' notice unless waived. Other agreements may differ.

### Transfer prompt

**Prompt:** Think of a change you expect to make this month. "If I need to change [hours, duties, a team rule], then before I announce it I will check [agreement] and call [labor relations contact]."
**Plan starters:**
- Ask Human Resources which agreement covers each direct report.
- Bookmark the articles on hours, discipline and work rules in each agreement.
- Ask where supervisors should keep notes, and how long.

## Lesson 5: Set up the first week

**Summary:** You cannot learn everything in a week. You can set one fair weekly habit, meet each person, understand the approvals that now reach you, and know where your required training lives.
**Minutes:** 10
**Objectives:**
- Set up one-to-ones with the same core agenda for everyone.
- Recognize when an equity analysis belongs with an action sent for your approval.
- Separate your required training from voluntary learning like this course.
- Know where to refer Tribal questions.

**Takeaways:**
- Give every person the same core time and questions.
- Ask whether an analysis exists before you approve a long-term action.
- This app is for private practice. The state's systems hold your requirements.

### Text: Meet everyone, the same way

Book a first one-to-one with every person you supervise. Use the same core questions for each:

1. What are your main priorities right now?
2. What is getting in the way?
3. What do you want to keep doing, or do more of?
4. Is there anything about how you work best that you would like me to know?
5. What should I know about this team that I probably do not?

Question 4 is an open door. Do not push anyone to walk through it. Do not ask anyone about a health condition, family plans, religion or immigration history. People can tell you what they choose to.

Then protect the time. If you find yourself cancelling one person's meeting more than others, treat it as a signal to look at. Face time matters. In the financial firm mentioned earlier, more of it with a manager raised promotion rates.

### Text: Approvals that now reach you

The DHS Equity Policy, in the undated copy reviewed for this course, requires a formal equity analysis for long-term actions going to a supervisor, manager, director or commissioner for approval. Ask your manager for the current version and how it is used in your division.

In practice, that means this question is now yours: "Where is the equity analysis for this?" If there is none, ask why before you sign. You may find the analysis is not needed for that action. Or you may find a service change that nobody has checked for its effect on people who use interpreters, live in greater Minnesota, or rely on a particular waiver service.

Use disparity data as a prompt to review a neutral process or access barrier. Do not use it to make a race-conscious decision about an individual.

### Accordion: What counts for what

**Item: Your required training**
Minn. Stat. 43A.21 requires all managers and supervisors to be trained on inclusive work environments, disability awareness, cultural competence and other equity and diversity areas, and it ties permanent status in a classified management or supervisory position to meeting training requirements. Minnesota Management and Budget's Supervisor Core is required for job certification under Administrative Procedure 21 and must be finished within the probationary period. Ask Human Resources or your manager where your requirements are tracked and when your deadlines fall.

**Item: This app**
It is voluntary. It records nothing for your employer. It does not assign courses or report scores, and your progress stays in your browser. It does not count toward the training above.

**Item: Tribal government relations**
This app does not teach this. Refer questions to ADSA and the Tribal liaison. Minn. Stat. 10.65 requires Tribal-state relations training for employees whose work is likely to include matters with Tribal implications, and it may cover you. Ask the Tribal liaison.

### Leader move: One fair habit

**Control:** You control one recurring habit that can start this week, with no one's permission.
**Failure:** Do not try to fix everything you inherited in your first week. Do not wait until you are settled to begin.
**Next:** Choose one: one-to-ones on the same agenda, or a simple assignment list. Start it this week and keep it for a month before adding another.

### Scenario

**Context:** On Thursday a policy analyst sends you a proposed long-term change to how providers submit a waiver form, with a note: "Director wants this by Friday. Just needs your sign-off." The packet has no equity analysis. You know the old form had a Hmong and a Somali translation. The new online form is English only. You do not know the policy details well yet.
**Prompt:** What do you do next?

**Option:** Sign it. You are new, the director wants it, and the analyst knows more than you.
**Response:** Your signature is what the approval step asks for. Deferring to urgency skips the check at the moment it exists for, and it sets the pattern for your team.

**Option:** Refuse to sign until the form is translated, and tell the analyst the change is discriminatory.
**Response:** You have spotted a real question, but you have jumped to a conclusion. You do not know yet what the analysis would show or what language options are planned. The label also raises the temperature without helping.

**Option (recommended):** Ask the analyst today whether an equity analysis exists or was decided against. Raise the language change as a specific question. Tell your manager what you found and what timing you can realistically offer.
**Response:** This uses the approval step as intended. It raises a concrete access question, gets the facts and gives your manager a chance to decide with information. Language access is best handled as a service-quality and program-access duty, so ask how DHS is meeting it for this form.

## Job aid

**Title:** First-week card: moments you do not handle alone
**Subtitle:** Fill in the contacts for your division. Keep this page out of the app.

**Purpose:** To tell you what to do in the first minutes of a moment you cannot handle alone, and whom to call.

**Remember:**
- Report possible harassment or discrimination immediately. No threshold, no written complaint needed.
- A request needs no special words. Move it forward promptly.
- Check the contract before you change someone's work.
- Pause decisions affecting someone who has just made a report or a request. Then check them.

**Section: Someone tells you about harassment or discrimination**
- Listen, take notes, and do not promise secrecy.
- Say: "I have to take this to Human Resources today, and I'll tell you what happens next."
- Report the same day to Human Resources or the Affirmative Action Officer: ____________
- Do not investigate or confront anyone yourself.

**Section: Someone mentions a medical or access need**
- Ask: "What would help?" Do not ask for a diagnosis or a note.
- Write down an oral request.
- Forward it promptly, and tell the person who decides. ADA coordinator: ____________
- Consult the ADA coordinator before any denial, or when questions arise.

**Section: You plan to change hours, duties or rules**
- Which agreement covers each person? MAPE, AFSCME, MMA, Managerial Plan, or other: ____________
- MAPE: 14 calendar days for permanent hour changes, unless mutually waived. Article 28 for work rules. Check other agreements directly.
- Call labor relations before you announce: ____________

**Section: Someone asks for sign-off on a long-term action**
- Ask: "Where is the equity analysis?"
- Ask which people are affected and what changes for them, including language and disability access.

**Section: Other contacts**
- Human Resources partner: ____________
- Tribal liaison, and ADSA for Tribal learning: ____________
- Where my required training is tracked: ____________

**Do next:** Hold the first round of one-to-ones, using the same core questions for everyone.
