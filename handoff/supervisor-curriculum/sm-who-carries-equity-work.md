# sm-who-carries-equity-work: Who Carries the Equity Work

**Subtitle:** On many teams, equity work settles on the same few people, often staff of color, disabled staff and bilingual staff, on top of a full job and without credit. This course shows you how to spot it, how to ask without assigning by identity, how to make it real work, and what to do when you are the one carrying it.
**Duration:** About 54 minutes
**Content type:** practice
**Status:** reviewed
**Last reviewed:** October 31, 2026
**Evidence date:** October 2026
**Scope:** For supervisors and managers, and anyone who assigns work. It is voluntary and asks no one to disclose an identity. This is general information, not legal advice, and it does not change any job description, pay rate or evaluation process.

**Course objectives:**
- Identify equity work on your team, including what no one calls by that name.
- Ask for this work by skill, interest and time, rather than by who someone is.
- Turn equity work into scoped, credited, shared work that takes the place of something else.
- Carry your own share of it as the supervisor.
- Set limits and get support if you are the person this work lands on.

**Applied next step:** List the equity and access tasks your team did in the past three months, and who did them. Choose one to re-scope, share or take on yourself.

## Lesson 1: What the work is and where it lands

**Summary:** This lesson names the work, describes the pattern, and is honest about how much we know.
**Minutes:** 13

**Objectives:**
- List the kinds of tasks that count as equity work on a DSD team.
- Explain cultural taxation and the minority tax, and the limits of that research.
- Separate a pattern in your team's work from a belief about any group.
- Run a simple check of your own team.

**Takeaways:**
- Equity work includes tasks no one calls equity work.
- The pattern is documented, mostly outside government.
- No one's identity tells you their availability, skill or wish to do this.
- You can see the pattern by looking at tasks. You don't need anyone's demographics.

### Text: What we mean by equity work
Equity work is anything done so that the team or the program works fairly for people it's currently failing. Some of it has a formal name: an equity analysis, a seat on the equity committee, a community engagement plan, leading an employee resource group. Much of it does not.

Here are some examples that may sound familiar. A bilingual consultant gets stopped in the hall to interpret a family's voicemail or read a letter. A Deaf employee is asked to explain Deaf culture to a new team, check captions on a video and advise on an interpreter contract, none of which is in the job. A disabled employee ends up checking documents for accessibility because they're the one who notices. A staff member of color gets invited to every interview panel, every workgroup that needs "a different perspective," and every community meeting about the place they grew up. A colleague gets asked to review a draft "to see if anything sounds off." Staff come to the one supervisor they trust with things they will not bring to anyone else, and that supervisor spends hours listening.

Every one of these tasks may be worth doing. The question is who does them, whether anyone chose that, what it costs and whether it counts.

### Statement
The work matters. That's why it shouldn't depend on the unpaid effort of whoever is most affected.

### Text: What the research says
Padilla (1994) used the term "cultural taxation" for extra, often unrewarded labor expected of underrepresented staff. Rodriguez, Campbell and Pololi (2015), writing about academic medicine, used the phrase "minority tax" for the extra responsibilities placed on faculty from underrepresented groups in the name of diversity efforts, work that often isn't recognized in promotion. Work on administrators points to outside, internal and social forces, including personal motivation, group expectation and institutional pressure. Qualitative research also describes "racialized feeling rules," in which colleagues of color are judged more harshly for not conforming to norms of positivity and carry emotional burdens with no organizational support. Babcock and colleagues (2017), in field and lab settings, found differences by gender in who was asked to take on low-promotability work and who accepted, with beliefs about who would agree contributing to the pattern.

Here's the limit. Most of this comes from universities and academic medicine. We found no research on public-sector supervisors of color carrying extra load, and none in disability services. We have no DSD data. So don't take this as a statement about your team. Take it as a question worth asking about your team.

### Accordion: Reasons this pattern is easy to miss
#### Item: People volunteer
Many people choose this work because they care about it. A person's offer is real, and it doesn't mean they have unlimited time. It may also reflect that they can see what will happen if no one does it. "Fair distribution of work" (dc-t25) calls it understanding an offer without assuming unlimited capacity.

#### Item: It looks like good performance
You may think, "They're so good with this." You may be right. That is also how skill gets taken for granted, and how a task stops being a request.

#### Item: It's scattered
A five-minute favor doesn't look like much. Twenty of them are a day's work, and they never show up in a tracker. "Recognizing hidden and invisible work" (dc-t26) teaches how to describe it.

#### Item: Saying no has a cost
A person may feel that saying no would hurt the community, make them look difficult or leave a colleague or a family with nothing. That's different from having a choice.

### Text: Pattern, not group
Be careful with what you do with this. Don't start thinking, "Staff of color don't want equity work," or "Disabled staff will be overloaded, so leave them off." Some want it badly and do it superbly, and leaving them off is its own exclusion. As "Recognizing hidden and invisible work" puts it, a recurring pattern can prompt an inquiry; it doesn't authorize predicting work from identity.

### List: A check of your own team
You can do this with task records alone.
1. List the equity, access, language and community tasks done in the past three months.
2. Next to each, write who did it and whether it was assigned, requested or just picked up.
3. Mark which were in the person's job description or work plan.
4. Mark which came with something taken off their plate.
5. Mark which showed up in any record of their work.
6. Look at who appears most often. Don't add anyone's identity to the list. If you know the pattern already, you'll see it.

### Leader move: Your check
**Control:** You control whether you look. It takes about half an hour, and it's the base for everything else in this course.
**Failure:** Do not survey staff about their identities, and do not ask the person who carries the most to do the check. That would just be more of the same work.
**Next:** Do the check before your next round of assignments.

### Scenario
**Context:** You supervise a mixed team of policy staff and consultants. You run the check and see that one consultant has done most of your team's work on community meetings, plain-language reviews and interpreter scheduling for the past six months. She was thanked in two meetings. None of it is in her work plan, and her caseload hasn't changed. She's also the person you'd think of first for the new workgroup on language access.
**Prompt:** What is your next step?

**Option 1:** Add her to the workgroup. She's the expert, and she's never said no.
**Response:** You've just seen the pattern, and this repeats it. Never saying no is not evidence of capacity, especially if saying no is hard. She may want it, and you need to ask her after looking at her load.

**Option 2:** Take all of it away from her so that she's protected.
**Response:** You'd be removing work she may value, without asking, and she might lose relationships and standing. Deciding on her behalf isn't the same as helping.

**Option 3:** Meet with her. Show her what you counted, ask what she wants to keep, and offer to put that in her work plan with something taken off her plate. Then look for others to build skill on the rest, and open up the workgroup to anyone.
**Response:** This is the best step. You've made the work visible, she gets a real choice, and you've started to share the load. Opening the workgroup means she can still join, by choice.
**Recommended:** yes

**Option 4:** Bring it up at the team meeting so that everyone appreciates how much she does.
**Response:** Public thanks may embarrass her, and it doesn't change anything. It can also further identify her as "the person who does that." Talk with her first.

### Knowledge check
**Question:** What should you conclude from the research on cultural taxation?
- All staff of color are overloaded with equity work at DSD.
- Staff from underrepresented groups shouldn't be asked to do equity work.
- A documented pattern, mostly from higher education and medicine, gives you a reason to check how this work is spread on your own team. (correct)
- It has been shown to apply equally in all state agencies.
**Correct answer:** A documented pattern, mostly from higher education and medicine, gives you a reason to check how this work is spread on your own team.
**Feedback if correct:** Yes. The research gives you a question. Your own task records give you an answer about your team. It tells you nothing about any one person.
**Feedback if incorrect:** The research is mostly from universities and medicine, and we have no DSD data. Don't turn it into a rule about a group. Use it as a reason to look at your own team's tasks.

### Transfer prompt
**Prompt:** Write one if-then plan: "If I need someone for an equity, access or community task, then before I think of a name I will ____."
**Options:**
- If one name comes to mind first, then I will check what that person has done in the last three months.
- If someone has been doing this work unassigned, then I will ask them what they want to keep.
- If I haven't run the check, then I will do it before the next round of assignments.

## Lesson 2: Asking without assigning by identity

**Summary:** How to invite people into this work using skill, interest and time. This lesson covers the law, the language, and the common hard cases: interpreting, panels and community work.
**Minutes:** 14

**Objectives:**
- Explain why assigning this work by protected trait is both unfair and legally risky.
- Write a request that describes the work rather than the person.
- Handle language skills, interview panels and community relationships.
- Make sure someone can say no at no cost.

**Takeaways:**
- Ask for the skill, not the identity.
- Language skill is a job matter. Interpreting needs a qualified interpreter.
- Nobody speaks for a group.
- Open the invitation to all, and let "no" be free.

### Text: What the law says, as of October 2026
You don't need to be a lawyer to do this right, and you do need a basic map. All of these are from federal or state sources as of October 2026.

Title VII protects everyone by the same standard (*Ames v. Ohio Department of Youth Services*, June 5, 2025). A change in duties can count as harm even when pay and rank stay the same (*Muldrow v. City of St. Louis*, April 17, 2024). So giving someone extra duties, or leaving them out of an opportunity, because of a protected trait can create a problem.

The EEOC's March 2025 technical assistance takes the position that there's no "diversity interest" exception, that client or customer preference is no defense to race or color discrimination, and that training, mentoring and leadership development are terms or privileges of employment. It's an agency position rather than law, and it is what federal enforcers have said. It bears directly on "let's send her because she's from that community."

None of this stops you from doing equity work. The Minnesota Human Rights Act, state policy and your own team's needs all still call for it. It tells you how: open to all, based on skill and interest.

### Statement
Ask for the skill you need. Don't ask for a person to stand in for a group.

### Tabs: Instead of this, try this
#### Tab: Equity review of a draft
**Instead of:** "Can you look at this, since you'd know how it would land?"
**Try:** "I need someone to review this for plain language and access. It takes about two hours. Who has the time and the skills? I can move your other work to fit it."
Then, if the question is how a community would receive it, ask the community through proper engagement. No colleague should stand in for that.

#### Tab: A community meeting
**Instead of:** "You should go to this one. They'll trust you."
**Try:** "This meeting needs someone who knows the program and has worked with this county or these families. Here's who has done that." A real working relationship is a work fact. A shared background is not a qualification.

#### Tab: A panel
**Instead of:** "We need someone diverse on the panel. Can you do it again?"
**Try:** Train a larger group of panelists from across the team, rotate them, and count the time. Multnomah County has trained interview panels and tracked representation. Ask Human Resources what applies here. See "Hiring Decisions: Criteria Before Candidates" (sm-hiring-decisions). Selecting a panelist by race or sex is a question to take to Human Resources.

#### Tab: Sharing an experience
**Instead of:** "Would you tell the team what it's like to be a Deaf employee?"
**Try:** Don't ask. Use a paid trainer or a course. If someone offers on their own, thank them and ask how they want it done. They should be able to change their mind. No one should be asked to disclose their disability, faith, immigration history or other identity to contribute.

### Accordion: Three hard cases
#### Item: Bilingual staff
Knowing a language is a skill, and it may really be part of a job. If speaking with families in their language is part of the work, say so in the position description, and ask Human Resources how that is handled, including any question of pay. Being bilingual doesn't make someone a qualified interpreter or translator. "Working with interpreters" (dc-s52) and "Language access as a leadership responsibility" (dc-l96) cover this. Don't pull staff off their own work to interpret for colleagues. Use a qualified interpreter, and budget for it. If language work has grown up informally, count it, then ask how it should be handled.

#### Item: Staff who are Deaf, DeafBlind or disabled
They aren't your accessibility department. If your team needs accessibility checks, assign them across the team and train people. Paying for expertise is fine, and so is hiring it. If a disabled staff member's job includes access work, it should be in the job. See "Supervising With Deaf, DeafBlind and Hard of Hearing Colleagues" (sm-deaf-deafblind-hoh).

#### Item: Working with communities
Community trust matters and is often built by particular people over years. Respect it as a working relationship, and give it time in the work plan. Don't assume someone has that relationship because of their background, or that they want it. Work with Tribal governments is separate: it's government-to-government, and it has its own duties. Refer those questions to the Tribal liaison and ADSA. Don't send a staff member because they're Native.

### List: A request that works
- What's the task, and what is it for?
- How much time, for how long?
- What skill is needed? Can someone learn it?
- What will come off the person's plate?
- Who decides, and what's the person's authority?
- How will the work be credited?
- Can they say no? Say that they can, and mean it.
- Send it to everyone who could do it, not to one person.

### Leader move: After someone says no
**Control:** You control what happens after a no. Say "thanks for telling me," and ask the next person. Don't ask for a reason.
**Failure:** Do not say "but you'd be so good at this" or "the community really needs you." Do not note it later as a lack of commitment. A no that costs someone isn't a real choice.
**Next:** Look at how you've responded to a no recently. Think about how someone on your team would describe it.

### Scenario
**Context:** Your unit is reviewing a waiver communication to families, and your manager wants it to work for Somali-speaking families. A consultant on your team is Somali and has often helped informally. Your manager says, "Just have her look it over. She'll know whether it works, and it'll be faster than getting a translator." You know she's already behind on her caseload.
**Prompt:** What do you do?

**Option 1:** Ask her to look it over. It'll only take an hour, and she has helped before.
**Response:** You'd be using her background as a stand-in for a translator and for community input. One person can't tell you what works for all Somali-speaking families. She's also behind, and asking her to do this adds to the problem.

**Option 2:** Tell your manager this needs qualified translation and a check with families, ask who pays for that and who owns the timeline, and offer to invite her in only as a program expert, if she wants, with time taken from elsewhere.
**Response:** This is the best step. You've separated the translation from community input and from her job. She can still choose to be involved, with her time counted.
**Recommended:** yes

**Option 3:** Tell your manager she can't do it because of the risk of a discrimination claim.
**Response:** It's not the best reason, and it may overstate the law. The stronger reasons are accuracy, her workload and the fact that one colleague doesn't stand in for a community. Lead with those.

**Option 4:** Leave her out of this, and of anything to do with Somali families from now on.
**Response:** That would remove work she may value and keep her from opportunities because of her background. Ask her what she wants, and don't decide for her.

### Knowledge check
**Question:** Which request fits this lesson?
- "You're from that community. Can you go to the meeting?"
- "We need a person of color on this panel, and you were great last time."
- "This review takes about two hours and needs plain-language skills. I'll move your other work. It's open to anyone, and it's fine to say no." (correct)
- "Could you share your experience as a disabled person at the all-staff meeting?"
**Correct answer:** "This review takes about two hours and needs plain-language skills. I'll move your other work. It's open to anyone, and it's fine to say no."
**Feedback if correct:** Yes. It describes the work, the time, the skill and the trade-off, and the no is free.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Each of the others asks a person to stand in for a group or asks them to disclose. Describe the work, the time and the skill instead, and invite anyone who could do it.

### Transfer prompt
**Prompt:** Think of the next time you'll need this kind of help. Write one if-then plan: "If I need ____, then I will send a request that says ____ to ____."
**Options:**
- If I need language help, then I will ask who provides qualified interpreters and translators, and budget for it.
- If I need a panelist, then I will draw from a trained, rotating group.
- If I need to know how a community will receive something, then I will ask through engagement and not through a colleague.

## Lesson 3: Make it real work

**Summary:** Equity work that isn't scoped, resourced and credited will fall back onto the same people. This lesson covers how to give it time, count it and share it, including your own part.
**Minutes:** 13

**Objectives:**
- Scope an equity task in time, authority and duration.
- Record the work fairly without building a file about anyone.
- Build skill across the team so the work doesn't rest on a few people.
- Name the equity work that belongs to you as the supervisor.

**Takeaways:**
- If it's real work, something else moves.
- Count it in the work plan and the review, in the person's own terms.
- Rotate and train, so that the same people aren't always the experts.
- The supervisor's share isn't delegable.

### Text: Five things that make it work
"Employee Resource Groups and Affinity Spaces" (gc-employee-resource-groups) and "Equity Committee and Unit Equity Team Roles" (gc-equity-committee-roles) teach the design for those two settings: paid time, a clear charter, set terms, rotation and shared membership. Those rules work for all equity tasks on your team. Here they are, in brief.

**Scope.** What is it, how long does it take, when does it end? "Help with equity stuff" is not a task.

**Time.** Paid work time, during the work day. Something moves to make room.

**Authority.** Can this person decide anything, or are they advising? Who owns the decision? Don't give someone responsibility for a result with no power to affect it.

**Credit.** It appears where the rest of their work appears. More below.

**Shared.** Set terms. Rotation. More than one person.

### Statement
If nothing moves to make room for it, you haven't assigned work. You've added a burden.

### Text: Counting it
One student affairs source recommends counting this work in formal evaluations, and Seattle's race and social justice goals directed departments to include implementation in manager and supervisor evaluations. This is thin evidence, drawn from elsewhere. It's also just reasonable: work that doesn't count will be done less, or will be done by people who pay for doing it.

Here's how to do that without creating a new problem. Ask Human Resources how to record it in your agency's work plan and review process. For MAPE-represented staff, Article 6 of the 2025-2027 contract covers appraisal, so check that. For others, check the contract or plan. Treat it as you'd treat any other work: what was done, for whom, what came of it.

### Accordion: Recording it fairly
#### Item: Describe the task
"Coordinated interpreters for six community meetings and rewrote two notices in plain language." That's useful. "Great at diversity" isn't.

#### Item: Don't collect stories
Don't record what someone's experience is or what they've told you about their life. "Recognizing hidden and invisible work" teaches this: more personal detail is not better evidence.

#### Item: Let them choose
Some people want public credit. Others want it quiet. Ask. Recognition can respect their choice, and the work should still appear in the record that affects their career.

#### Item: Don't make it a rating on inclusion
Count what people did. Don't score their commitment or attitude, and don't compare who cares most. Taking part in voluntary learning, including this app, is never a thing to evaluate.

#### Item: Don't penalize those who don't do it
If equity tasks are voluntary extras, then not doing them can't be held against someone. If they're part of a job, say so in the job. Make up your mind which it is.

### List: Sharing it
- Train more people. Plain-language review, accessible documents, interpreter booking and captions are learnable skills.
- Rotate: note-taking, booking, facilitation, panels and committee seats.
- Make standing tasks part of someone's job in writing, and rotate who's responsible.
- Pay for outside expertise when the skill is specialized, including people with lived experience.
- Ask those newer to the topic to do the explaining and the preparation, so the work of explaining inequity doesn't fall only on those who experience it.

### Tabs: The supervisor's share
#### Tab: What's yours
Some equity work is your job and can't be moved: fair assignments, consistent ratings, responding to accommodation requests, reporting under Policy 1436, booking access as a normal part of your meetings, an equity analysis where your approval requires one, raising patterns upward. Don't hand it to the staff member who cares most.

#### Tab: What you should learn yourself
If you don't know how to book an interpreter, or how to check a document's accessibility, learn. If you don't know what your team's language needs are, find out. If you're always asking one person how something will land, read the course on it instead.

#### Tab: Being the one in the room
When something goes wrong in a meeting, such as a comment or a joke or an assumption about someone, you are the one to respond. It should not be left to the one person it was aimed at. "Addressing microaggressions as a leader" (dc-l77) covers this.

### Leader move: Your own share
**Control:** You control what you stop passing on. Pick one task you've been handing to someone because they're better at it, and start doing it.
**Failure:** Do not say "I'm not the expert" as a way out of something that's part of supervising.
**Next:** This week, book the access for your own next meeting, or do the plain-language check on your own next notice.

### Scenario
**Context:** Your team has been asked to write an equity analysis for a change to a waiver process. You approve it when it's done. You'd normally ask the consultant who always does these, who is also the only person of color on the team. She does them very well. A newer policy specialist has said he'd like to learn, and he has time. The deadline is in three weeks, and it's a real deadline.
**Prompt:** What do you do?

**Option 1:** Ask her, as usual. The deadline's real and she's the best at it.
**Response:** It'll be done well. It also confirms that this work is hers and that no one else needs to learn it. And her other work takes the hit, as it did last time.

**Option 2:** Give it to the newer specialist alone. He wants to learn, and it takes it off her plate.
**Response:** You've spread the load, but you've set him up to struggle with no support, and you may be setting yourself up to send it back to her at the last minute.

**Option 3:** Ask the newer specialist to lead, ask her whether she'd like to coach him for a set number of hours with that time counted, and read the analysis closely yourself as the approver. If she'd rather not, find another source of coaching.
**Response:** This is the best step. You've built skill on the team, given her a real choice and credit for coaching, and kept your own responsibility as the person who approves it.
**Recommended:** yes

**Option 4:** Do it yourself to protect both of them.
**Response:** Doing your share is good. But this keeps the skill from growing on the team and adds to your own load. Reading it as the approver is your real share.

### Knowledge check
**Question:** You want to count equity work in someone's review. What's the best way to do that?
- Rate how committed they are to equity.
- Describe the specific tasks and results, and ask Human Resources how to record them. (correct)
- Write down what they've told you about their background and why it matters to them.
- Leave it out to avoid drawing attention to them.
**Correct answer:** Describe the specific tasks and results, and ask Human Resources how to record them.
**Feedback if correct:** Yes. Treat it as work: what was done, for whom, with what result. Ask how they would like it credited.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Ratings of commitment and personal stories are the wrong record, and leaving it out keeps the work invisible. Describe tasks and results, and ask Human Resources how your process records them.

### Transfer prompt
**Prompt:** Write one if-then plan: "If an equity task comes to my team, then I will set ____, move ____, and credit it by ____."
**Options:**
- If it's a recurring task, then I will write it into someone's work plan and set a rotation date.
- If only one person knows how, then I will have someone else learn it this quarter.
- If it's part of my own job as a supervisor, then I will stop passing it on.

## Lesson 4: When you are the one carrying it

**Summary:** For supervisors who get asked to do this work on top of their job, whether by their manager, their peers or their staff. This lesson is about setting limits, keeping your duties straight and finding support. It also speaks to those who assign this work to them.
**Minutes:** 14

**Objectives:**
- Recognize the extra requests that can land on a supervisor.
- Respond to a request with a scope, a trade or a no.
- Handle staff who bring concerns to you because they trust you, within your reporting duties.
- Find support that doesn't require you to speak for a group.

**Takeaways:**
- A no, or a trade, is a reasonable answer.
- Staff trust is valuable. It doesn't suspend your duty to report.
- You don't have to represent anyone.
- If you assign this work upward or across, the same rules apply to you.

### Text: How it can land on a supervisor
If you're a supervisor of color, a disabled or Deaf supervisor, a supervisor who is openly part of a particular community, or one of very few people like you in management, you may know this already. You might be asked to join every equity effort, to review your division's language, to speak at events, to mentor anyone who shares your background, to sit on every panel. Staff from other units may seek you out because they think you'll understand. Your manager may turn to you first when anything about race or disability comes up. You may be judged more harshly than a colleague if you seem less than positive about it, or if you decline.

We should be honest about how little is known. We found no research on public-sector supervisors in this position. What we have comes from higher education and is largely descriptive. People's experiences vary widely, and this lesson doesn't assume yours. You may not be carrying this at all, or you may have chosen it and be glad to. What follows is a set of practical options, for whoever needs them.

### Tabs: Answering a request
#### Tab: Scope it
"Tell me what this is, how much time it takes, what authority comes with it and when it ends." This alone will change some requests, and it treats the request as work.

#### Tab: Trade it
"I can do this if [other work] moves. Which would you like me to set aside?" See "Your Own Load" (sm-your-own-load). If you're on a committee because of who you are, you can still ask what it replaces.

#### Tab: Redirect it
"I'm not the right person to speak for that. Here's the office that handles it," or "I'd start with the community engagement process," or "You might read this course." You aren't obliged to be the first stop.

#### Tab: Decline it
"I can't take that on this quarter." You don't need to explain. If you're concerned that your no may count against you, you can state it in writing, keep a copy and say something to your manager about how the request was framed.

#### Tab: Say yes
Some of this work may be why you're in the job. You don't have to refuse it to be doing it right. Say yes on the terms in Lesson 3, including credit.

### Text: When staff come to you
Staff may bring you things because they trust you, especially people who feel no one else in management would understand. That trust is real, and worth a lot. It can also put you in a hard spot.

The state policies apply. As read in October 2026, Policies 1436 and 1329 say supervisors must immediately report alleged discrimination or harassment to Human Resources or the Affirmative Action Officer, and those who knowingly allow or tolerate it are subject to discipline up to discharge. Treat that as covering what someone tells you in confidence, and what people outside your unit tell you. If you are unsure, ask Human Resources the same day. Being trusted doesn't change the duty, and you can be at risk if you hold it.

So say this early: "I want to hear you. Before you go on, you should know that if this is about discrimination or harassment, I have to report it, and I can't keep it to myself. Do you want to go on?" Many people prefer knowing. Then, if you report, tell them what you can about what happens next. Check on them, and watch out for retaliation.

For a concern that isn't reportable, you can listen. You still don't have to be everyone's person. "I can talk for 20 minutes today" is an honest offer. Point people to their own supervisor, the Employee Assistance Program, their union or an employee resource group, depending on what they need.

### Statement
Trust is not a reason to hold a report. Being trusted is a reason to say up front what you have to do.

### Accordion: Finding your own footing
#### Item: You don't represent anyone
You can say what you think, or nothing at all. If someone asks what "your community" thinks, you can say, "I can only speak for myself, and you'd want to ask through the engagement process."

#### Item: Peer support
A peer who also supervises can be useful. Use the rules from "Your Own Load": no names and no details. You don't need them to share your background, and you may want some who do.

#### Item: Employee resource groups
State enterprise employee resource groups are described in their guidelines as open to all, including allies, with paid time for meetings within your schedule and supervisor discretion for workload. They aren't a complaint channel or a bargaining table. Messages on state systems are government data. If a group is useful to you, use it. You also don't owe it your leadership.

#### Item: Feeling rules
You may notice you're expected to be upbeat about equity efforts that aren't working, or calm about something that's aimed at you. You're allowed to disagree. You also have to decide how much of that you want to say at work, and to whom. You know your situation best.

#### Item: Harm directed at you
If you experience harassment or discrimination, you have the same routes as anyone else. Being a supervisor doesn't take that away, and you don't have to absorb it as part of the role. Go to Human Resources or the Affirmative Action Officer.

### Text: If you are the one who keeps asking them
This lesson is for you too. If there's a supervisor or peer you always turn to about race, disability or a community, ask yourself what you'd do if they weren't here. Then start doing some of it. When you need their real expertise, ask on the same terms as anyone: scope, time, credit, and a free no. If you're their manager, say out loud that they can say no. Then make sure their review shows what they've done and doesn't punish what they've declined. "Backing Your Supervisors" (sm-backing-your-supervisors) has more on this.

### Leader move: One request this month
**Control:** You control your answer to the next request. Have your scope question ready.
**Failure:** Do not automatically say yes because you're afraid no one else will do it. Do not feel you have to refuse everything, either. Choose based on what you want to do and what the work will cost.
**Next:** Look at everything you've been asked to do for equity in the past three months. Keep what you want. For the rest, draft one sentence you can say.

### Scenario
**Context:** You're one of very few supervisors of color in your division. Your director asks you to lead a new workgroup on staff retention "since you understand what people are going through." You also hear from a staff member in another unit, who stops you after a meeting and says, in confidence, that their supervisor keeps making comments about their accent and they don't want anyone to know. You're already behind on two projects, and you care about both of these things.
**Prompt:** What do you do about the two situations?

**Option 1:** Say yes to the director because this matters, and keep the staff member's confidence because they asked you to.
**Response:** Saying yes with no terms adds to the load and tells the director that this is yours. Keeping the confidence is the bigger problem: comments about someone's accent may involve national origin, and your duty to report does not depend on whether they're in your unit or what they asked for.

**Option 2:** Tell the staff member you have to report it, and tell them what will happen. Report it today. Then ask the director what the workgroup is, how much time it takes, what authority it has and what moves to make room, and what skills are needed from the person who leads it.
**Response:** This is the best step. You've met your duty and been honest with the person. You've treated the director's request as work, and you've moved the discussion from who you are to what the role needs. You can still say yes, on those terms.
**Recommended:** yes

**Option 3:** Decline the workgroup, and tell the staff member to go to Human Resources themselves.
**Response:** Declining may be right for you. But telling them to go on their own doesn't meet your duty once you've heard it, even though they can also go directly. You need to report it too.

**Option 4:** Talk to the other supervisor privately, as a peer, and let them know to watch their comments.
**Response:** It may feel like a kind and practical solution, but it skips the required route, it could expose the person who told you, and it's the kind of private handling that the reporting duty exists to prevent.

### Knowledge check
**Question:** A staff member from another unit starts telling you about harassment by their supervisor, and asks you to keep it between you. Which is the best response?
- Promise to keep it private, because trust matters most.
- Tell them up front that you have to report discrimination or harassment, and report it right away if that's what it is. (correct)
- Tell them it isn't your concern, since they aren't in your unit.
- Listen, then wait and see whether it happens again.
**Correct answer:** Tell them up front that you have to report discrimination or harassment, and report it right away if that's what it is.
**Feedback if correct:** Yes. Being honest about your duty lets them decide what to tell you, and it keeps you from making a promise you can't keep.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Under Policies 1436 and 1329 as read in October 2026, supervisors must immediately report. A promise of secrecy, waiting or saying it isn't your concern all fail that duty. Being honest at the start is also kinder.

### Transfer prompt
**Prompt:** Write one if-then plan: "If I'm asked to take on equity work because of who I am, then I will say ____."
**Options:**
- If it comes with no terms, then I will ask for the scope, the time, the authority and what moves.
- If someone begins telling me something that may be reportable, then I will tell them what I have to do before they go on.
- If I keep turning to the same colleague, then I will do the next one myself, or ask on the same terms as anyone.

## Job aid

**Title:** Who carries the equity work: a supervisor's check
**Subtitle:** Before you ask, assign or say yes.

**Purpose:** Help you see who is carrying this work, ask for it fairly, make it count and carry your own share.

**Remember:**
- The work matters, and it shouldn't run on unpaid effort.
- Ask for the skill. Don't ask someone to stand in for a group.
- No one's identity tells you their time, skill or interest.
- A free no is the test of a real ask.

### Section: Check your team every quarter
- What equity, access, language and community tasks got done?
- Who did them? Assigned, requested or picked up?
- Was it in their work plan? Did something come off their plate? Did it count?
- Use task records only. No identity data.

### Section: A fair ask
- What's the task, and how long does it take?
- What skill is needed?
- What moves to make room?
- Who decides?
- How is it credited?
- Open to anyone. Fine to say no.

### Section: Never
- Use a coworker as an interpreter.
- Ask someone to speak for a group or share their story.
- Send someone to a community because of their background.
- Send Tribal questions to a Native colleague. Go to the Tribal liaison and ADSA.
- Hold a no against anyone.

### Section: Your share
- Fair assignments, consistent ratings, accommodation responses.
- Reporting immediately under Policies 1436 and 1329.
- Booking access for your own meetings.
- Responding when something goes wrong in the room.

### Section: If it lands on you
- Scope it. Trade it. Redirect it. Decline it. Or say yes on terms.
- Tell people up front what you have to report.
- You speak for yourself.
- You have the same routes as anyone if you're harmed.

**Do next:** Run the quarterly check this week, then choose one task to re-scope, share or take back.
