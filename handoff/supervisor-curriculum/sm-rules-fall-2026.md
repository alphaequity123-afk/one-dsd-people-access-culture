# sm-rules-fall-2026: What a Supervisor Should Know About the Rules, Fall 2026

Subtitle: A dated, plain-language map of the state duties, civil rights law, federal enforcement positions and pending cases that touch a supervisor's everyday decisions, as of October 2026.
Duration: 80 minutes
Content type: foundation
Status: reviewed
Last reviewed: October 31, 2026
Evidence date: October 2026

This course is general information as of October 2026, not legal advice. For a real situation, ask Human Resources, labor relations or your ADA coordinator.

Course objectives:
- Separate a binding duty from a federal enforcement position and from a practical recommendation.
- Explain Minn. Stat. 43A.21 as the state's own training duty, separate from this voluntary app.
- Act on the immediate reporting duty and the accommodation process without delay.
- Apply Ames and Muldrow to routine decisions such as assignments, schedules and transfers.
- Describe accurately what has changed on disparate impact, Olmstead and digital accessibility, and what is still pending in United States v. Minnesota.

Applied next step: Pick one decision you expect to make in the next 30 days, such as a schedule change, a stretch assignment or a hire. Write the job-related reason before you decide, and name the person you would ask if a rule in this course seems to apply.

## Lesson 1: Three kinds of rules, and whose duty is whose

Summary: Not everything labeled a rule binds you in the same way. This lesson sorts law, enforcement positions and recommendations, and explains why the state's training duty under Minn. Stat. 43A.21 is separate from this app.
Minutes: 12
Objectives:
- Tell a requirement from an enforcement position and from a recommendation.
- Read the status labels used in this course.
- Explain what Minn. Stat. 43A.21 requires of the state and of supervisors.
- Explain why this voluntary app does not satisfy that requirement.
Takeaways:
- Ask of any rule: is this law, an agency's stated position, or advice?
- 43A.21 is the state's duty. The state's own training meets it. This app does not.
- A date matters. Much of this field moved in 2026 and will keep moving.

### Text
You will hear a lot of confident statements about equity and the law this year. Some say equity work is now illegal. Some say nothing has changed. Neither is accurate. The most useful habit you can build is to ask what kind of statement you are hearing.

This course uses three categories.

A requirement is law or binding policy. A statute, a court's holding, or a Minnesota Management and Budget (MMB) policy that applies to you.

An enforcement position is what a federal agency says it believes the law means and what it plans to pursue. It matters. It tells you where investigations may go. It is not itself law, and courts can disagree with it.

A recommendation is practical advice. It is a good idea for reasons this course explains, but no statute or policy requires it.

### List
How to read the status labels in this course. They come from the research for this course, dated October 31, 2026.
- Primary: the research read the statute, rule, opinion or policy text itself.
- Secondary, or "reported": the research saw only a law firm alert, an advocacy group, a case tracker or a news report. Treat these as probably right, and possibly incomplete.
- Not verified: the research looked and could not confirm it. This course says so instead of guessing.

### Text
Now the state's duty. Minn. Stat. 43A.21 (primary) was amended in 2023. Subdivision 3(b) says all managers and supervisors must be trained on inclusive work environments, disability awareness, cultural competence, and other equity and diversity areas. Subdivision 3(a) ties permanent status in a classified management or supervisory position to meeting training requirements. The research for this course found no amendment after 2023 and no repeal as of October 2026.

Subdivision 2(a) goes further. All state employees must receive annual training on statutes or policies related to ADA Title II, the state's affirmative action policy, equal opportunity employment, and digital accessibility standards. Subdivision 6 says training content and platforms must meet state accessibility standards and that accommodations must be timely.

Supervisor Core and Manager Core are MMB's programs. MMB's page, which is undated, says they are required for job certification under Administrative Procedure 21 and must be finished within the probationary period.

### Statement
43A.21 is the state's duty. This app is not how the state meets it.

### Text
This app is voluntary for everyone, from staff to commissioner, with one exception: the Equity Analysis Toolkit is required of all DHS staff, regardless of status. The app assigns nothing, tracks nothing for your manager, and sends nothing to your supervisor or employer. Your progress stays in your browser. The one exception is Ask: your questions go to outside answer and research services, and the app owner keeps a copy of them. Finishing every course here does not count as required training and does not replace Supervisor Core, Manager Core or your agency's annual training. If you are unsure which required training applies to you, ask Human Resources.

One more common mix-up. The Minnesota Human Rights Act, chapter 363A, does not require supervisor training. A full text search of the chapter (primary) found "training" only in a peace officer board reference and in remedies that can order admission to training programs. If someone tells you the Human Rights Act requires your training, they likely mean 43A.21.

Tribal-state relations training is a separate duty under Minn. Stat. 10.65 (primary) for a defined group of employees. This app refers Tribal learning to ADSA and the Tribal liaison.

### Tabs
Tab: Requirement
Body: 43A.21 training. Immediate reporting under MMB policies. The accommodation process. Title VII and the Human Rights Act.

Tab: Enforcement position
Body: The EEOC and DOJ documents on DEI from 2025. A Justice Department Office of Legal Counsel opinion. These shape federal action. They are not statutes.

Tab: Recommendation
Body: Writing your reason before a decision. Keeping an assignment log. Using one set of interview questions. Good practice with strong reasons behind it, and no statute names them.

### Leader move
Control: You control whether you repeat a claim about the law without checking what kind of claim it is.
Failure: You treat a headline as a rule and change how you treat staff, or you treat an enforcement position as nothing and miss a real risk.
Next: When a rule comes up on your team, ask two questions out loud: what is the source, and what is its date? Then take it to Human Resources if it affects a decision.

### Scenario
Context: A case consultant on your team has read an article and asks in a team meeting whether the state still has to do equity training at all, since the federal government opposes it. Others are watching for your answer.
Prompt: What do you do next?
Option A: Say that the federal government has ended all of it, so people should stop worrying about equity training.
Response A: This overstates the federal position and is wrong about state law. 43A.21 subdivision 3(b) still stands as far as the research for this course could find as of October 2026.
Option B: Say that you do not talk about politics at work and move to the next item.
Response B: The question is about a work rule, not politics. Closing it down leaves the rumor in place and teaches people not to ask you.
Option C: Say that the state's statute, 43A.21, still requires the training as of now, that federal agencies have taken positions about some kinds of content, and that you will bring Human Resources' current guidance back to the team.
Response C: This is accurate and proportionate. It separates state law from federal position, gives the date, and commits you to a next step you can keep.
Recommended: C

### Knowledge check
Question: Which statement about this app and Minn. Stat. 43A.21 is correct?
Option A: Finishing the supervisor courses in this app meets the 43A.21 training requirement.
Option B: 43A.21 is the state's own duty, and it is met through the state's own training. This app is voluntary and separate.
Option C: The Human Rights Act requires this training, so 43A.21 no longer matters.
Correct answer: B
Feedback correct: Right. The state's training meets the statutory duty. This app is a voluntary resource and keeps no record for anyone.
Feedback incorrect: This app does not satisfy the requirement, and the Human Rights Act contains no supervisor training duty. 43A.21 is the source, and the state's own training is how it is met.

### Transfer prompt
If someone on my team tells me a new rule changes how we work, then I will ask for the source and date, sort it as law, enforcement position or advice, and check with Human Resources before I change a decision.

## Lesson 2: The Human Rights Act, harassment and your immediate duty

Summary: Minnesota's harassment law changed in 2024 in a way many people missed, and MMB policy gives you a duty that does not depend on any legal threshold: report immediately.
Minutes: 13
Objectives:
- Describe what 2024 c. 105 changed in the Human Rights Act.
- Explain why "severe or pervasive" is not the test for your duty to act.
- State the immediate reporting duty under Policies 1436 and 1329.
- Choose the right next step when someone asks you to keep a concern quiet.
Takeaways:
- Since the 2024 change, "discriminate" in chapter 363A includes harassment generally, not only sexual harassment.
- You do not decide whether conduct meets a legal test before you report it.
- Immediately means now, not after you have tried to fix it yourself.

### Text
Start with what changed. Before 2024, the Human Rights Act's definition of "discriminate" in section 363A.03, subdivision 13, specifically covered sexual harassment in relation to sex discrimination. Laws 2024, chapter 105, section 3 (primary) changed it. "Discriminate" now includes "segregate, separate, or harassment." The practical point is that harassment based on any protected class falls more plainly within the definition.

Section 2 of the same law added episodic impairments and impairments in remission to the definition of disability (primary). Lesson 3 returns to that.

People also often say that the 2023 session changed the harassment standard. The research for this course checked. The 2023 act (Laws 2023, c. 52, primary) added a definition of gender identity and pay history protections, and it did not touch the sexual harassment definition.

### Accordion
Item: What about "severe or pervasive"?
Body: Those words appear nowhere in chapter 363A (primary text search). They come from case law. In Kenneh v. Homeward Bound (Minn. 2020), the court is reported to have kept the phrase while telling courts to apply it to current workplace norms rather than old precedent (secondary). The research for this course found no bill enacted to remove it, and no 2025 or 2026 Minnesota appellate decision applying the 2024 change. That question is open. It is also not your question to answer.

Item: Did the federal rules on harassment change?
Body: The EEOC is reported to have rescinded its 2024 harassment guidance on January 22, 2026, by a 2-1 vote (secondary). The same reports say the underlying law is unchanged. A withdrawn guidance document does not change Minnesota law or MMB policy.

Item: Which protected classes does Policy 1436 list?
Body: Read the policy for the current list. It includes gender expression and genetic information, among others (primary). The Human Rights Act separately protects gender identity (primary).

### Text
Here is the part that is yours. MMB's HR/LR Policy 1436, Harassment and Discrimination Prohibited, and Policy 1329, Sexual Harassment Prohibited (both primary), say that managers and supervisors must model appropriate behavior, treat reports seriously, respond when they become aware of a problem, and "immediately report" to Human Resources or the Affirmative Action Officer. Those who knowingly allow or tolerate prohibited conduct are subject to discipline, up to discharge. Neither policy uses the words "severe or pervasive."

So you do not need a formal complaint. You do not need the person's permission. You do not need to be sure it happened. If you know about it, you report it. Then you follow your agency's procedure, which is for Human Resources or the Affirmative Action Officer to explain.

### Statement
Your duty starts when you become aware. It does not wait for severity, proof or permission.

### List
What this means in practice:
- Listen, take notes, and say honestly what you have to do next.
- Do not promise secrecy. You can promise to share only with those who need to know.
- Do not run your own investigation or question witnesses first.
- Do not hold the concern while you try an informal fix.
- Report the same day you learn of it, whether it came from the person affected, a witness or your own eyes.
- Watch what happens next. Assignments, schedules and reviews for the person who raised it should not change without a clear work reason you have checked.

### Leader move
Control: You control how fast the report goes in and how you treat the person who raised the concern while it is being handled.
Failure: You try to protect the team by handling it quietly, and you delay the report while the conduct continues.
Next: Find out today exactly who your Human Resources contact and Affirmative Action Officer are, and save them where you can reach them quickly.

### Scenario
Context: A program consultant tells you at the end of a one-to-one that a senior colleague has made repeated comments about her religious head covering in the team chat. She adds, "I'm not filing anything. Please don't make it a thing. I just wanted you to know." You like both employees, and the team has had a hard quarter.
Prompt: What do you do next?
Option A: Thank her, explain plainly that you have to report this to Human Resources or the Affirmative Action Officer, tell her what you know about what happens next, and make the report today.
Response A: This meets the policy and treats her honestly. Her wish matters and you can tell Human Resources about it, but it does not remove your duty.
Option B: Respect her request, keep an eye on the chat for a few weeks, and report only if it happens again.
Response B: Policy 1436 says immediately report once you are aware. Waiting to see more is the delay the policy is written to prevent.
Option C: Speak privately to the senior colleague and ask them to stop, which keeps it out of the formal process as she asked.
Response C: An informal word can feel kind, but it skips the required report, may compromise a later review, and leaves her exposed if the colleague works out who spoke up.
Option D: Decide first whether the comments are serious enough to meet a legal standard, and report if they are.
Response D: You are not asked to apply a legal test. The policies do not use "severe or pervasive," and your duty does not depend on it.
Recommended: A

### Knowledge check
Question: As of October 2026, what did Laws 2024, chapter 105 change in the Minnesota Human Rights Act?
Option A: It added a requirement that employers train supervisors.
Option B: It extended the definition of "discriminate" to include harassment generally, and added episodic impairments and impairments in remission to the disability definition.
Option C: It wrote "severe or pervasive" into the statute.
Correct answer: B
Feedback correct: Right. That is section 3 and section 2 of the 2024 law. Chapter 363A still contains no supervisor training duty and does not use "severe or pervasive."
Feedback incorrect: The 2024 law changed the definition of "discriminate" and the disability definition. The training duty is in 43A.21, and "severe or pervasive" comes from case law, not the statute.

### Transfer prompt
If someone tells me about conduct that may involve a protected class and asks me to keep it between us, then I will tell them what I have to do, and I will report it to Human Resources or the Affirmative Action Officer the same day.

## Lesson 3: Accommodation: the firmest ground you have

Summary: Disability duties are among the most settled in this course. You do not need to decide the request. You need to recognize it, move it promptly and keep it private.
Minutes: 13
Objectives:
- Recognize a request made in ordinary words.
- Describe your role under MMB Policy 1433.
- Explain the employee's preference and the agency's choice among effective options.
- Identify what you may not ask and what you must not share.
Takeaways:
- No special words are needed. A request can be oral.
- Sitting on a request can be treated as denying it.
- Supervisors and managers must not ask for medical information.

### Text
Many people expect the rules on disability to have collapsed along with other federal positions. On employment accommodation, they have not. The ADA's employment title and the Human Rights Act both still apply.

The EEOC's enforcement guidance on reasonable accommodation (October 17, 2002, primary) is old and predates the 2008 ADA amendments. It says it stays in effect until rescinded or superseded, and it states that it lacks the force of law. It is still the plainest federal statement of the basics. No magic words are needed. The request can be oral or come through a representative. Unnecessary delay can itself violate the ADA, and a supervisor who sits on a request can be treated as having effectively denied it.

Minnesota adds its own text. Section 363A.08, subdivision 6 describes accommodation through an informal interactive process. Section 363A.02, subdivision 1(c) says failure to engage in that process may be an unfair discriminatory practice (both primary). Since 2024, an impairment that is episodic or in remission can count as a disability (primary). A person who seemed fine last month may have a real need this month.

### Text
MMB's HR/LR Policy 1433 (last revised July 27, 2017 on its face; read for this course) sets the state process. A request is any communication in which someone asks or states that they need the agency to provide or change something because of a medical condition. It can go to you, Human Resources, the ADA coordinator or the Affirmative Action Officer or designee. Oral requests must be documented in writing.

As the first step, the person who receives the request must promptly forward it to the appropriate decision maker, and at the same time tell the requester who that is. Your agency decides how much a supervisor may approve directly. The ADA coordinator decides requests outside your authority, must be consulted when issues or conflicts arise, and must be consulted before any denial. If an approved accommodation cannot be provided within a reasonable time, the decision maker tells the requester the status before the end of 30 days and, where feasible, uses temporary measures. Check your agency's own procedure for local steps.

### Accordion
Item: Whose choice is it?
Body: Under EEOC guidance, the employee's preference gets primary consideration, and the employer has ultimate discretion among effective options. Policy 1433 says the preference is always seriously considered, and the agency need not provide the employee's choice so long as what it offers is effective, or it determines the accommodation would cause undue hardship. The test is effectiveness, not convenience.

Item: What can I ask?
Body: You can ask what would help. You must not request medical information or documentation. Policy 1433 reserves that for the ADA coordinator, when appropriate, and says medical records are not appropriate documentation and cannot be accepted.

Item: What can I share?
Body: Medical information goes in separate confidential files. Supervisors and managers may be told necessary restrictions and accommodations (Minn. Stat. 363A.20, subd. 8, primary; Policy 1433). The fact that someone has an accommodation is itself confidential under Policy 1433. If a coworker asks, say you handle individual arrangements privately for everyone.

Item: Can the employee bring someone?
Body: Policy 1433 says an individual may ask that the ADA coordinator, a union representative or a support person be present. The MAPE 2025-2027 contract, Article 31, provides union representation in accommodation at the employee's request. Check the contract that covers your employee for other units.

Item: What about the new federal Section 504 rule on gender dysphoria?
Body: An HHS final rule announced October 30, 2026 (primary) excludes gender dysphoria not resulting from physical impairments from disability under Section 504, for HHS-funded programs. It is days old as this course is dated. The Human Rights Act separately protects gender identity (primary). Do not decide such a request yourself. Route it the same way and ask the ADA coordinator.

### Statement
You do not have to know the answer. You have to move the request.

### Leader move
Control: You control the first response, the speed of the handoff, and whether you ask a medical question.
Failure: You offer your own fix, wait to see whether the problem goes away, or ask for a doctor's note, and the request stalls on your desk.
Next: Look up who the decision maker is for each type of request in your unit, and what your agency lets supervisors approve.

### Scenario
Context: A policy specialist says in passing, "Since my treatment started I can't get through the afternoon in the open office. I don't know what to do. It comes and goes." You are about to go into a budget meeting. Your unit has a norm that everyone sits together on Tuesdays.
Prompt: What do you do next?
Option A: Ask what the treatment is, so you can judge how long this might last.
Response A: This asks for medical information, which Policy 1433 says supervisors and managers must not request. Ask what would help at work instead.
Option B: Tell them you hear it, ask what would help, write down what they said, and forward it promptly to the decision maker, telling them who that is.
Response B: This treats plain words as a possible request, documents an oral request as the policy requires, and starts the clock in the right place.
Option C: Suggest trying headphones for a month, and tell them to come back if that does not work.
Response C: Your own trial fix delays the process. A delay on your desk can be treated as a denial, however well meant.
Recommended: B

### Knowledge check
Question: Under Policy 1433, what is your first step when you receive a reasonable accommodation request you cannot decide yourself?
Option A: Ask the employee for documentation from a medical provider.
Option B: Wait until the employee submits the written form.
Option C: Promptly forward it to the appropriate decision maker and tell the employee who that is.
Correct answer: C
Feedback correct: Right. An oral request gets documented and moved. The ADA coordinator handles any documentation request, if one is appropriate.
Feedback incorrect: Supervisors must not request medical information, and a request does not depend on a form or special words. Forward it promptly and tell the person who is deciding.

### Transfer prompt
If someone on my team tells me they need something changed for a health reason, then I will ask what would help, write it down, forward it the same day, tell them who decides, and avoid any question about their diagnosis.

## Lesson 4: Ames, Muldrow and the enforcement positions

Summary: Two Supreme Court decisions widened what can be challenged in a supervisor's routine decisions, and for everyone. Separately, the EEOC and DOJ have published positions on DEI. Learn which is which.
Minutes: 14
Objectives:
- State what Ames and Muldrow held.
- Apply them to assignments, schedules, transfers, mentoring and training access.
- Describe the 2025 EEOC and DOJ documents accurately as enforcement positions.
- Choose a decision habit that holds up under both.
Takeaways:
- One standard applies to all employees, majority group or not.
- "Some harm" to a term or condition is enough. Same pay and title does not end the question.
- Written, job-related reasons applied the same way to everyone are your strongest protection and the fairest practice.

### Text
Muldrow v. City of St. Louis was decided April 17, 2024 (primary). A police sergeant was transferred. Her rank and pay stayed the same. Her duties, schedule and perks changed. The Court held that a transfer plaintiff under Title VII must show "some harm" to an identifiable term or condition of employment, and that the harm need not be significant. Retaliation claims keep their separate standard, which asks whether the action was materially adverse.

Ames v. Ohio Department of Youth Services was decided June 5, 2025, by a unanimous Court (primary). The plaintiff worked at a state youth-services agency and challenged a denied promotion and a demotion. Some lower courts had required majority-group plaintiffs to show extra "background circumstances." The Court said that rule cannot be squared with Title VII. The standard is the same for every employee.

Put them together. A lateral move, a schedule change or a reshuffled caseload can be challenged if a protected trait motivated it, and that is true whichever group the employee belongs to. Commentary also reads Muldrow as arguably reaching restrictions on training, leadership development, mentoring or sponsorship (secondary), and the EEOC treats those as covered (primary, enforcement position).

The research for this course did not verify how the Eighth Circuit, which includes Minnesota, has applied these two cases to public employers.

### Tabs
Tab: Law
Body: Title VII, Ames and Muldrow. The Human Rights Act. These bind.

Tab: EEOC position
Body: The technical assistance released March 19, 2025 remains posted with no withdrawal notice (primary). It still describes Ames as pending, so it has not been refreshed. It says DEI training can create a hostile environment depending on content, application or context. It treats mentoring, sponsorship, networking and training as terms or privileges of employment. It says limiting workplace groups or separating employees by protected characteristic can be unlawful, even with identical programming. It says there is no "diversity interest" exception, and that client or customer preference is no defense to race or color discrimination. It says opposing a training can be protected activity if there is a fact-specific basis.

Tab: DOJ position
Body: Guidance dated July 29, 2025, described as non-binding best practices (primary). OMB M-25-33 (September 12, 2025) tells federal agencies to follow it, which gives it practical force in grant administration (primary). It says training should not stereotype, exclude or disadvantage, should not require people to affirm ideological positions or confess to biases or privilege, and should not penalize dissent. Its footnote 6 acknowledges that harassment-prevention training aimed at unlawful discrimination is lawful if it does not single out groups as inherently racist or sexist.

Tab: Courts so far
Body: Courts are splitting on the facts. Young v. Colorado DOC (10th Cir., May 11, 2026) is reported to hold that a single mandatory training did not plausibly create a hostile environment, and a cert petition is reported (secondary). Diemert v. City of Seattle (9th Cir., Sept. 28, 2026) is reported to have sent a human-services employee's claim past summary judgment, on trainings plus about ten incidents over five years, including manager comments and denied advancement (secondary). The research for this course found no Eighth Circuit decision on DEI training. These cases do not bind Minnesota courts.

### Text
Read those carefully and two things stand out. First, these are positions, not statutes. You should know them, and you should not treat them as settled law. Second, what they describe going wrong is conduct: sorting people by identity, limiting access by protected class, demanding people affirm a belief, punishing disagreement. Nothing in them says you may not treat people fairly, remove barriers, or look hard at whether your process works. Diemert, as reported, also points to what supervisors do after a training, not only what a slide said.

For DSD, one item needs attention. Matching staff to clients by race, because a client or a family prefers it, runs into the EEOC's customer-preference position. A person's choice of their own provider in their own services is a different question from your assigning employees by race. If this comes up in your unit, take it to Human Resources. Do not decide it from this course.

### List
Decision habits that hold up:
- Set the job-related reason or criteria before you choose the person.
- Offer stretch work, mentoring, training nominations and leadership openings through a visible route anyone on the team can use.
- Apply the same test you would apply if the person belonged to a different group.
- Write a short note at the time of the decision. It will be more accurate than one you try to reconstruct later.
- Let people disagree with equity content without any consequence at work.
- Keep caring about who is being left out, and fix it by opening the route rather than by choosing individuals by identity.

### Statement
Same standard for everyone. Visible criteria for every opportunity.

### Leader move
Control: You control whether a routine decision has a reason you could show to anyone on your team.
Failure: You assume a same-pay move is too small to matter, or you think one group's complaint could not count.
Next: Before your next assignment or schedule change, write one sentence stating the work reason, then ask whether you would make the same call if the person were someone else.

### Scenario
Context: You supervise waiver policy staff. You plan to move an experienced consultant off a visible legislative project onto a backlog of desk reviews. Pay and title stay the same. Your draft note says the project "needs a different perspective" and names a replacement. The consultant you are moving belongs to a majority group, and the replacement does not. You did not think about that until you reread your note.
Prompt: What do you do next?
Option A: Go ahead. Same pay and title means no one could have a claim, and majority-group employees have a higher bar anyway.
Response A: Both reasons are wrong after Muldrow and Ames. "Some harm" need not be significant, and the standard is the same for every employee.
Option B: Drop the change, since any move may now be a legal risk.
Response B: The law does not stop you from managing work. If the move has a sound reason, freezing helps no one, including the replacement who may have earned the chance.
Option C: Stop and state the work reason plainly: which skills and which deadlines? Apply that test to everyone on the team who could do the project, record it, and ask Human Resources if the reason does not hold up.
Response C: This keeps your authority to assign work, replaces a vague phrase with a job-related reason, and would be just as fair if the two people were reversed.
Recommended: C

### Knowledge check
Question: Which statement is accurate as of October 2026?
Option A: The EEOC's March 2025 DEI document is a statute that makes all DEI training illegal.
Option B: Ames holds that majority-group employees must meet the same Title VII standard as anyone else, and the EEOC document is an enforcement position.
Option C: Muldrow holds that a transfer can only be challenged if pay or rank changes.
Correct answer: B
Feedback correct: Right. Ames and Muldrow are Supreme Court holdings. The EEOC document states an agency's position and has no force as a statute.
Feedback incorrect: The EEOC document is an enforcement position, not a statute, and it does not say all DEI training is illegal. Muldrow held that "some harm" to a term or condition is enough, even with pay and rank unchanged.

### Transfer prompt
If I am about to change someone's assignment, schedule or access to an opportunity, then I will write the job-related reason first, check that I would apply it to anyone on the team, and keep the note.

## Lesson 5: Disparate impact, Olmstead and accessibility dates

Summary: Federal enforcement has shifted sharply on disparate impact and on Olmstead. The statutes have not disappeared, and Minnesota's own duties continue. Accessibility dates moved, but the duty did not start in 2027.
Minutes: 14
Objectives:
- State accurately what has changed on disparate impact.
- State accurately what has changed on Olmstead and integration.
- Give the current federal accessibility dates and the Minnesota standard.
- Use disparity data as a prompt to review process, not as a basis for an individual decision.
Takeaways:
- Do not say disparate impact is dead. Do not say it is enforced as before.
- Do not say Olmstead is gone. Do not say federal enforcers are pressing it.
- Treat accessibility as a duty now, not a 2027 project.

### Accordion
Item: Disparate impact: what changed
Body: Executive Order 14281 (April 23, 2025) set a federal policy of eliminating disparate-impact liability to the maximum degree possible (primary, as quoted in the HHS rule). DOJ's rule rescinding its Title VI disparate-impact provisions is reported as published December 10, 2025 (secondary). The HHS rule effective July 24, 2026 removes its Title VI effects provisions, and HHS says it will not pursue Title VI disparate-impact liability against funding recipients (primary). A Justice Department Office of Legal Counsel opinion dated June 9, 2026 is reported to conclude that the EEOC's Title VII disparate-impact framework is unconstitutional. It binds federal agencies, not courts (secondary).

Item: Disparate impact: what has not changed
Body: Title VII's statutory disparate-impact provision still exists, and reports say private plaintiffs and states may continue under existing precedent (secondary). The Human Rights Act prohibits maintaining "a system of employment which unreasonably excludes" applicants (Minn. Stat. 363A.08, subd. 2, primary). The research for this course did not verify how Minnesota case law currently treats disparate-impact claims under that text.

Item: Disparate impact: what is pending
Body: The EEOC is reported to have sent a final rule rescinding its part of the Uniform Guidelines on Employee Selection Procedures to federal reviewers on October 16, 2026, with effect projected around January 2027. The rule text is not public (secondary). Do not act on this until Human Resources tells you what changes.

Item: Olmstead: what changed
Body: An Office of Legal Counsel opinion of June 18, 2026 says neither Section 504 nor Title II imposes an integration mandate and that Olmstead did not so hold (primary). It binds the executive branch, not courts (secondary). DOJ is reported to have withdrawn its 2011 Olmstead guidance on July 20, 2026 (secondary). On September 23, 2026, the HHS Section 504 integration provisions were reported vacated in Texas v. Kennedy, at the joint request of DOJ, HHS and the remaining states. The sources are advocacy groups. The research for this course did not read the order or confirm exactly which sections were vacated (secondary).

Item: Olmstead: what has not changed
Body: The ADA, Section 504 and the Supreme Court's Olmstead decision stand. DOJ's Title II integration rule is reported to remain (secondary). Minnesota's Olmstead Plan continues. A comprehensive update went to the Leadership Forum on August 14, and a September 2026 version and Subcabinet materials are posted, but formal adoption was not confirmed (secondary). Current status of the Jensen settlement was not verified.

### Statement
Olmstead and the statutes remain. The federal enforcement position has changed. Minnesota's commitments continue through its Plan and state law.

### Text
Why does this matter to you? Because people on your team work on waivers, community living and services every day, and they will ask. They may hear that community integration is no longer the law. That overstates it. They may also assume federal enforcers are behind them on integration. For now, that overstates it the other way.

Disparate impact raises a separate practical question. Your unit may look at disparities in who gets served, who gets hired, or who gets a waiver slot. Removing a federal effects test does not ban that analysis. What changes is that a disparity alone does not show federal Title VI liability. A practical rule follows from this, and it is a recommendation rather than a legal requirement. Use disparity data to prompt a review of neutral process and access barriers. Ask where people drop out, what the form asks, who gets told, and how long it takes. Never use it as a reason for a race-conscious decision about an individual person.

### List
Accessibility dates and standards, as of October 2026:
- DOJ Title II web rule, as extended by an interim final rule published April 20, 2026: public entities of 50,000 or more population by April 26, 2027; smaller entities and special districts by April 26, 2028. The standard stays WCAG 2.1 AA (primary). Existing Title II duties continue meanwhile (secondary).
- HHS Section 504 web rule, as extended by an interim final rule published May 11, 2026: recipients with 15 or more employees by May 11, 2027; fewer by May 10, 2028 (secondary).
- Minnesota: Minn. Stat. 16E.03, subd. 9 names Section 508 and WCAG 2.0 and lets the state's chief information officer incorporate later revisions (primary). MNIT's standard is reported to incorporate WCAG 2.1 AA, effective July 1, 2024 (secondary).
- 43A.21, subd. 6 applies the state accessibility standard to training content and platforms, and says accommodations must be timely (primary).

### Text
The federal dates moved. The state standard already applies, and the federal effective-communication duties did not pause. If your team publishes a document, a slide deck or a form, treat accessibility as today's work. Ask your ADA coordinator or accessibility lead which standard and date applies to your program.

Language access follows a similar pattern. Executive Order 13166 is reported revoked by Executive Order 14224, and federal guidance is being withdrawn (secondary). Title VI and Section 1557 remain statutes. The research for this course did not verify a Minnesota language-access statute specific to DHS. Treat language access as a matter of service quality and program access, and ask about specifics rather than cite the old guidance.

### Leader move
Control: You control how accurately you describe these changes to staff who work with people affected by them.
Failure: You tell staff a duty has ended because the federal enforcement posture changed, or you promise federal backing that has been withdrawn.
Next: Write two sentences you can use with your team on Olmstead, and put the date on them.

### Scenario
Context: A quarterly report shows that people in one racial group are approved for a service more slowly in your region. A consultant asks whether this is still something you are allowed to look at. Another suggests moving that group's files to the front of the queue.
Prompt: What do you do next?
Option A: Move those files forward, since the data shows a gap and that fixes it fastest.
Response A: This makes individual decisions turn on race. It is the use of disparity data this lesson warns against, and it does not fix whatever is causing the delay.
Option B: Drop the report, since disparate impact is no longer federally enforced.
Response B: This overstates the change. HHS's position concerns its own liability theory. Nothing in it stops you from finding out why your process is slower for some people.
Option C: Review the steps that produce the delay, such as intake, forms, language access, referral routes and handoffs, then change those steps for everyone, and tell your manager what you find.
Response C: This uses the data as a prompt to review neutral process and access barriers, which is sound on the law as it stands and more likely to help.
Recommended: C

### Knowledge check
Question: How should you describe Olmstead to your team as of October 2026?
Option A: The Olmstead decision and the statutes stand, the federal enforcement position has changed, and Minnesota's commitments continue through its Plan and state law.
Option B: Olmstead was overturned in 2026, so integration is no longer a legal question.
Option C: Federal enforcers are actively pressing an integration mandate, just as before.
Correct answer: A
Feedback correct: Right. An Office of Legal Counsel opinion binds the executive branch, not the courts, and it did not overturn a Supreme Court decision.
Feedback incorrect: No court overturned Olmstead in the sources reviewed, and federal enforcement has shifted away from an integration mandate. The accurate description holds both of those facts.

### Transfer prompt
If someone on my team says a federal change ended a duty, then I will say what changed, what remains and what date I am relying on, and I will check with the right office before we change how we serve people.

## Lesson 6: United States v. Minnesota and how to stay current

Summary: The federal government has sued Minnesota over its affirmative action system, and a DHS policy is an exhibit. No court has ruled. This lesson says what is known, what to do in the meantime, and how to keep this course from going stale in your head.
Minutes: 14
Objectives:
- Describe the status of United States v. Minnesota as of October 2026.
- Choose the right next step when a hiring step touches the pending case.
- Identify what stays steady regardless of the outcome.
- Plan how you will check for changes.
Takeaways:
- It is a pending case. As of the docket summary viewed October 31, 2026, no relief has been granted.
- Keep following the current written process, and take questions to Human Resources rather than improvising.
- Dated knowledge expires. Check before you rely on it.

### Text
United States v. Minnesota, No. 0:26-cv-00273 (D. Minn.), was filed January 14, 2026, according to DOJ's own release (primary). DOJ opened its investigation in July 2025, and DHS is reported to have said the requirement rests on state law (secondary).

The following comes from a public case tracker viewed October 31, 2026, and law firm and news summaries (secondary). The research for this course did not read the complaint, the amended complaint or the exhibit.
- A three-judge court is hearing the case.
- DHS's Hiring Justification Policy is listed as Exhibit B.
- An amended complaint was filed September 11, 2026, and it is reported to narrow the case to a facial challenge with forward-looking relief.
- Fact discovery runs to March 1, 2027, and dispositive motions are due by July 15, 2027.
- A protective and preservation order was entered in September 2026.
- No relief has been granted.

The challenged provisions are reported to include Minn. Stat. 43A.19, subd. 1(a), 43A.191, subd. 3(c), 43A.02, subd. 33, and several Minnesota rules. DOJ is reported to be asking the courts to overrule Weber and Johnson, the older Supreme Court decisions that permit some employer affirmative action (secondary). Those precedents appear to remain binding until overruled.

The state statute itself reads as follows. An agency missing its hiring goals "must justify its nonaffirmative action hires" (Minn. Stat. 43A.191, subd. 3(c), primary). Section 43A.19 states a goal of eliminating the effects of past and present discrimination (primary).

### Statement
A lawsuit is not a ruling. Follow the current process and ask. Do not improvise in either direction.

### Tabs
Tab: If you are on a hiring panel
Body: Set criteria before you see candidates. Score everyone on those criteria. The recommendation in the research for this course is not to weigh race or sex in choosing an individual, and to take any question about the state's process to Human Resources. Title VII applies to every applicant, and Ames confirms the standard is the same. Recruiting more widely and removing barriers to applying are different from choosing a person by race or sex.

Tab: If a justification step comes up
Body: Do not skip it on your own because of the suit. Do not reinterpret it on your own either. Ask Human Resources what the current process requires, and do it the way they tell you.

Tab: If you hold hiring records
Body: Do not delete or tidy hiring and selection records outside the normal retention process. A preservation order is reported in the case. Ask Human Resources whether it reaches your records.

Tab: If staff ask you about it
Body: Say it is a pending case, no court has ruled, and you will share what Human Resources tells you. Do not predict the result. Let people disagree with the state's policy or with the lawsuit without consequence.

### Accordion
Item: What about DHS's own plan?
Body: DHS's Affirmative Action Plan for July 1, 2024 to June 30, 2026 (primary) said training would be mandatory for managers and supervisors, including completing the Intercultural Development Inventory, and that completion would be tracked. That plan period has ended. The research for this course did not find a 2026-2028 plan and could not confirm current practice. Ask Human Resources what is in effect. This app is separate from that plan and gives no assessment or scores.

Item: What about the executive order?
Body: Executive Order 23-12 (September 2023, primary) rescinded Executive Orders 19-01 and 21-13. A Minnesota executive order expires 90 days after the issuing governor leaves office (Minn. Stat. 4.035, subd. 3, primary). The current governor's term is reported to end in January 2027 and the election is November 3, 2026 (secondary). The order could lapse around April 2027 unless renewed. This does not change 43A.21, which is a statute.

Item: What could change soon?
Body: The rule on the Uniform Guidelines. A cert decision in Young. A decision in De Piero (3d Cir.). Rulings in United States v. Minnesota. Any federal change to the Title II integration rule. Adoption of the Olmstead Plan. The 2027 legislative session. A new governor's orders. Any of these could make part of this course out of date.

### List
What stays steady whichever way these go:
- Decide assignments, schedules, ratings and hires on job-related reasons you set first.
- Report harassment and discrimination concerns immediately.
- Move accommodation requests promptly and keep medical details out of your hands.
- Do not retaliate against anyone who raises a concern, asks for an accommodation or disagrees.
- Keep work accessible.
- Check the contract before you act on a work rule. Only the MAPE 2025-2027 contract was read for this course. For AFSCME, MMA and the Managerial Plan, read your own.

### Leader move
Control: You control whether you act on the current written process and whether you ask before improvising.
Failure: You quietly drop a required step because of the news, or you predict how the case will end and treat staff accordingly.
Next: Put a recurring reminder on your calendar for January, April and July 2027 to ask Human Resources what has changed.

### Scenario
Context: You have finished interviews for a program consultant vacancy. Your top-scored candidate is ready for an offer. Human Resources' checklist has a hiring justification step for this job group. A fellow supervisor tells you to ignore it: "The feds are suing over that. Nobody's doing it anymore."
Prompt: What do you do next?
Option A: Skip the step, on your colleague's word that it no longer applies.
Response A: A pending lawsuit is not a ruling, and no relief had been granted as of October 31, 2026. Your colleague's claim is not a change in the process.
Option B: Reopen the decision and pick a different candidate, so that no justification is needed.
Response B: This changes who gets the job in order to avoid a form, and it makes the choice turn on something other than your job-related criteria. That is the kind of decision Title VII and Ames expose.
Option C: Keep your scoring record intact, ask Human Resources what the current process requires for this hire, and complete it as they direct.
Response C: This keeps a clean record built on job-related criteria and puts the question of what the policy now requires with the office responsible for it.
Recommended: C

### Knowledge check
Question: Which statement is the most accurate about United States v. Minnesota as of October 31, 2026?
Option A: The court has struck down Minnesota's affirmative action statutes.
Option B: The case is pending, with discovery and dispositive motions scheduled into 2027, and a case tracker shows no relief granted.
Option C: The case was dismissed after the amended complaint.
Correct answer: B
Feedback correct: Right. The filing date comes from DOJ. The schedule and status come from a case tracker and other secondary sources, so check for changes before relying on them.
Feedback incorrect: No court has struck down the statutes and the case has not been dismissed, according to the sources reviewed. It remains pending, with fact discovery to March 1, 2027 and dispositive motions by July 15, 2027.

### Transfer prompt
If a hiring step or a state policy is said to have changed because of a lawsuit or a headline, then I will keep my records intact, ask Human Resources what currently applies, and follow that until they tell me otherwise.

Purpose: A one-page reminder of what binds you, what is an enforcement position, and what to do while the rules move. Dated October 2026. Check before relying on it.

- "Reported" means the source was secondary. Verify it before you repeat it as fact.

Sections:

Binding on you now
- Immediately report harassment and discrimination concerns to Human Resources or the Affirmative Action Officer (Policies 1436 and 1329).
- Promptly forward accommodation requests and tell the person who decides. Never ask for medical information (Policy 1433).
- One Title VII standard for all employees (Ames). "Some harm" is enough (Muldrow). The Human Rights Act covers harassment under "discriminate" (2024 c. 105).
- Complete the state's required training (43A.21, Supervisor Core or Manager Core). Ask Human Resources what applies.

Enforcement positions to know
- EEOC technical assistance, March 19, 2025: training, mentoring and access can be terms of employment. No identity-sorted programs. Client preference is no defense to race or color discrimination.
- DOJ guidance, July 29, 2025: non-binding, with practical force through OMB M-25-33. No required affirmation or confession. No penalties for dissent.
- Federal agencies have stepped back from disparate impact and an integration mandate. Courts, states and private plaintiffs are not bound by those opinions.

Say it this way
- Disparate impact: "Federal enforcement has largely moved away from it. The Title VII provision and Minnesota law still exist. We use data to review our process, not to decide about an individual."

- United States v. Minnesota: "It is pending. No court has ruled. I will share what Human Resources tells me."

Dates to hold

- May 11, 2027: HHS Section 504 web rule, recipients with 15 or more employees (reported).
- March 1, 2027 and July 15, 2027: discovery and dispositive motion dates in United States v. Minnesota (reported).
- Accessibility under state standards applies now.

Do next
