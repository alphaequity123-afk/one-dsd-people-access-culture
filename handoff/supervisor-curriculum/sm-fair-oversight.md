# sm-fair-oversight: Fair Oversight in Program Integrity

**Subtitle:** Practical habits for supervisors and managers of review, audit and program staff: consistent criteria, records someone else can check, real language access, and pattern review.
**Duration:** 62 minutes across 5 lessons
**Content type:** practice
**Status:** reviewed
**Last reviewed:** October 31, 2026
**Evidence date:** October 2026

**Course objectives:**
- Hold two duties at once: stopping fraud, and not harming honest providers or the people they serve.
- Build review criteria around conduct rather than a community or a language.
- Keep records that let someone else check a decision.
- Plan language and communication access in reviews and notices.
- Use pattern review to test whether the method is accurate and consistent.

**Applied next step:** Pick one review or flag your team uses. Write down each criterion. Mark each one as conduct or as a possible stand-in for who someone is. Bring your list to your own manager.

## Lesson 1: Two duties, and what a supervisor controls

**Summary:** Fraud harms people, and so does careless oversight. This lesson sets out what is happening and which parts are yours.
**Minutes:** 12

**Objectives:**
- Describe both kinds of harm.
- Use precise language about errors, findings and fraud.
- Separate what a supervisor controls from what other offices decide.

**Takeaways:**
- Take fraud seriously and take accuracy seriously.
- A flag is a lead, and a documentation gap is not fraud.
- You control criteria in use, the records your team keeps, how your team talks and who reviews.

### Text: What is going on, as of October 2026

Program integrity is a live and tense subject in Minnesota. DHS reports identifying high-risk services, freezing new enrollment in some services, suspending payments to some providers and revalidating providers, including with unannounced site visits. Minnesota's federal Medicaid funds have also been reported as deferred on program-integrity grounds. Those reports come mainly through summaries, and figures change, so check DHS's own pages for current numbers. Some Rochester providers have said publicly that they lost funding despite meeting requirements. That is the providers' account, and it is only one side.

This is the part to keep in mind: no published data were found on how these actions fall by race, language or community. So this course doesn't claim that enforcement is unfair, and it doesn't claim that it is fair. It teaches habits that let you find out, and that keep your own team's work defensible.

### Tabs: Two kinds of harm

#### Tab: Fraud

When someone bills for services that were never delivered, the person who needed them goes without and public money is lost. Trust in the program falls, including for honest providers.

#### Tab: Careless oversight

An action against a provider can end services for people who did nothing wrong. Paperwork demands can sink a small provider who could have fixed an error. Loose talk about who commits fraud can stigmatize people who get services lawfully, and can discourage eligible families from seeking help.

#### Tab: The job

Neither harm cancels the other. You are not choosing between being tough and being fair. A review that's accurate, consistent and explainable serves both duties.

### List: Words that matter

- Error: a mistake, such as a wrong code or a missing signature.
- Documentation gap: the record doesn't show what the rule requires. It may or may not mean that the service was not delivered.
- Improper payment: a payment that should not have been made, for any reason.
- Flag: a data result that needs a person to look at it. A flag is a lead, not a finding.
- Fraud: intentional deception. Only use this word when the responsible office has reason to.

### Accordion: What's yours and what isn't

#### Item: Yours as a supervisor

The criteria your team actually uses. The records they keep. A second reader on hard calls. How your staff write and talk about providers. Telling the right office about access risks. Whether your staff feel able to tell you that a method is not working.

#### Item: Not yours alone

Decisions on payment suspensions, sanctions, referrals and appeals belong to the offices responsible for them. Ask them. This course doesn't state the rules for notices or hearings. Equitable Oversight Design for Program Integrity Leaders, another course in this app, goes into those and into the fraud risk frameworks.

#### Item: Not for this app

Specific providers, specific people or open cases. Do not type those details here.

### Statement

This course is general information as of October 2026, not legal advice. Legal conclusions belong to the responsible DHS offices and legal staff.

### Leader move: Fix the word first

**Control:** You control what words go into your team's memos and emails.
**Failure:** "Fraud" shows up in a draft to describe missing paperwork. It gets forwarded, and now people treat it as a finding.
**Next:** Read your team's last three summaries. Replace "fraud" wherever the evidence shows only an error, a gap or a flag.

### Scenario

**Context:** You supervise reviewers. A reviewer's draft says a group of providers in one service have "a fraud problem" because their claims lack required documentation. You know that the documentation rule changed recently, and that some providers asked for guidance and never got it. The reviewer feels strongly, and leadership wants fast action.
**Prompt:** What do you do next?

**Option:** Forward the draft. Strong words show that the division is taking this seriously.
**Response:** The evidence so far is about documentation, not intent. The label could set off responses that hurt providers and the people they serve. It would also be hard to defend later.

**Option (recommended):** Ask the reviewer to separate what the evidence shows into possible errors, gaps and indicators that need referral. Tell the responsible office about the guidance problem. Send on anything credible through the proper path.
**Response:** This keeps a path open for real fraud to be looked at, and it fixes a likely cause of errors. You're not slowing down. You're being accurate about what you have.

**Option:** Drop the review because the rule change means nobody can be held responsible.
**Response:** A rule change doesn't rule out fraud, and errors may still need correction. Dropping it walks away from one of your two duties.

### Knowledge check

**Question:** A data query flags a provider for unusual billing. What is the flag?
**Options:**
- A finding of fraud.
- A lead that a person needs to check against records.
- Proof that the provider's documents are false.
- A reason to tell other providers to be careful of this one.

**Correct answer:** A lead that a person needs to check against records.
**Feedback if correct:** Right. Two providers can share an address for innocent reasons. A data result can be wrong. A person checks before anything else happens.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Look for the answer that treats the flag as a start, not an end.

### Transfer prompt

**Prompt:** Think of the next summary or memo your team will send upward. Finish this plan: "If a draft says 'fraud' or 'problem provider', then I'll ask ____ before it goes out."

## Lesson 2: Consistent criteria: conduct, not community

**Summary:** Why review criteria should describe what someone did, and how to spot a stand-in for who someone is.
**Minutes:** 13

**Objectives:**
- Distinguish conduct-based criteria from identity stand-ins.
- Question past-case patterns.
- Assign review work without using a reviewer's or a provider's identity.

**Takeaways:**
- Criteria should describe conduct and billing patterns.
- Language, neighborhood, faith or ethnicity of a provider's staff or clients are not risk indicators.
- Past cases show where people looked, as well as what was there.

### Text: Why this matters

A criterion chosen because of who people are raises a direct concern under Title VI, which bars intentional discrimination on the basis of race, color or national origin in programs receiving federal funds. As of October 2026, HHS has removed its disparate-impact provisions from its Title VI regulations, effective July 24, 2026, and says it will not pursue that kind of liability. That doesn't make a poor criterion a good one. A review method that follows a community instead of conduct is likely to miss fraud elsewhere and burden honest providers. The practical test remains: does this criterion predict real problems, and does it describe what someone did?

### Tabs: Conduct or stand-in?

#### Tab: Conduct and patterns

Hours billed that one worker couldn't have worked. Services billed while a person was hospitalized. Claims that don't match authorizations. Sudden unexplained spikes. Separate providers sharing a bank account with no clear reason.

#### Tab: Stand-ins

The languages staff speak. Where the owner was born. A neighborhood. A faith community. The ethnicity of people served. A foreign-sounding business name. A provider being small or new, without any conduct attached.

#### Tab: Not always obvious

Some criteria look neutral but may act as stand-ins. A shared address may reflect a shared office building in a community with few affordable spaces. Heavy use of one type of service may reflect a family's real needs. Don't throw these out automatically. Test what they predict, and ask for an innocent explanation before acting.

### Accordion: Questions to ask about any criterion

#### Item: Where did it come from?

Past cases reflect where people previously looked. If you looked only in one place, that's where you found things.

#### Item: What does it predict?

Of the cases it flagged, how many were confirmed after review? Compared with what?

#### Item: Who does it fall on?

Where you can, compare who it flags by provider size and region, and, where the data are sound, by the communities served. If a group gets flagged often and confirmed rarely, the method is creating burden without benefit.

#### Item: Is there a better one?

Could a criterion based on the conduct in past cases do the same job?

#### Item: Would you say it out loud?

Could you explain this criterion to a provider, a reporter or a judge?

### Text: Assigning your own reviewers

Your staff assignment decisions matter as well. Do not assign reviewers to providers based on the reviewer's race or national origin, and do not bar them from a case for that reason. The EEOC's position is that client or customer preference is no defense to race or color discrimination. Assign on job needs, such as a language skill the work actually requires, experience with a service type and a genuine conflict of interest. A real conflict, such as a family tie to a provider, should be handled the same way for everyone. If a review needs a language, use a qualified interpreter or a qualified language resource rather than assuming a colleague can do it.

### Leader move: Ask for the conduct behind the rule

**Control:** You can ask what conduct a criterion stands for, every time.
**Failure:** A rule goes live because it matched past cases, and nobody asks what in those cases actually looked wrong.
**Next:** For each rule your team uses, ask, "What did people do that this catches?" If nobody can answer, it needs another look.

### Scenario

**Context:** An analyst proposes a new rule to flag providers for review. One part of it is that a provider's staff mainly speak a language other than English. The analyst says that several past fraud cases involved providers like that, so it seems predictive. The rule goes live next week, and it has not been tested.
**Prompt:** What do you do next?

**Option:** Keep it, because it comes from real cases.
**Response:** Past cases show who was looked at, as well as who did something wrong. This criterion targets language, which can stand in for national origin. It is not about conduct at all.

**Option:** Keep it, but leave it out of the written description so that it won't be controversial.
**Response:** Hiding it makes things worse. It removes any chance of review, and it would be very hard to defend. It also tells your staff that concealment is acceptable.

**Option (recommended):** Remove the language criterion. Ask the analyst what the conduct was in those cases and build the rule on that. Test how it performs, including how many flags are confirmed and whom they fall on, before use. Delay the launch if needed.
**Response:** This keeps what was useful about those cases, removes a stand-in for national origin and tests the rule. Tell your manager why the date moved.

**Option:** Cancel the rule altogether, so nobody can object.
**Response:** Real conduct patterns in those cases may be worth finding. Canceling the rule abandons that part of the duty. Fix it instead.

### Knowledge check

**Question:** Which of these is a conduct-based criterion?
**Options:**
- The provider's staff mainly speak a language other than English.
- The provider is located in a particular neighborhood.
- Services were billed for a person on days that person was in the hospital.
- The owner immigrated within the past ten years.

**Correct answer:** Services were billed for a person on days that person was in the hospital.
**Feedback if correct:** Right. It describes something that was done and can be checked against records. There may still be an innocent explanation, so a person checks it.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Look for the criterion that describes what happened, not who someone is or where they are.

### Transfer prompt

**Prompt:** Choose one rule, checklist or flag your team uses. Finish this plan: "If I go through ____, then for each criterion I'll ask what conduct it catches, and I'll ask ____ how many of its flags were confirmed."

## Lesson 3: Records someone else can check

**Summary:** How your team can keep records that show why a decision was made, and how a second reader catches problems early.
**Minutes:** 12

**Objectives:**
- List what a useful review record contains.
- Set up a second reader for hard calls.
- Keep similar cases consistent over time.

**Takeaways:**
- Another person should be able to read the record and see why you did what you did.
- Second readers catch errors and drift.
- A record of what was checked and found innocent matters as much as a record of what was found.

### List: A useful record has

- Why the case was picked, in terms of the criterion used.
- What was checked, and when.
- Innocent explanations considered, and what became of them.
- What the reviewer concluded, in precise words.
- What step came next, and under which written criteria.
- Who reviewed the decision.
- Any access arrangements made, such as an interpreter, and any problems reaching the provider.

### Text: Tiered responses

Your team may not decide what happens next, but you can make sure similar situations go the same way. Ask the responsible office whether written criteria exist for each level of response. These range from technical help for common errors, up through targeted reviews, corrective plans and referral. If there are no written criteria, tell your manager. Without them, two reviewers can look at the same facts and send them down very different paths.

### Accordion: Keeping reviewers consistent

#### Item: Second reader

For hard calls, and for a sample of ordinary ones, have a colleague read the record without knowing the first reader's conclusion. Talk through any differences.

#### Item: Regular case talk

Once a month, have your team look at a made-up or fully anonymized case and say what they would do. Differences here are useful. They tell you where the guidance is unclear.

#### Item: Drift check

Standards can tighten under pressure and loosen when things calm down. Compare how a similar case was handled six months ago.

#### Item: Reversals

Find out what happened after your team's work, including appeals and reversals where you may know them. If you never hear, ask how you can. If reversals cluster in one kind of case, the method may need another look.

### Text: Data and privacy

Records in this work may include private data and investigative information. Follow your office's rules on what is kept and who sees it. If you are unsure, ask your data practices contact. Avoid personal notes on the side. Do not paste anything from a case into this app or into Ask.

### Leader move: Read a clean case

**Control:** You choose what you read. Most managers read only the hard cases.
**Failure:** Files closed with no finding get no attention, so no one learns how much burden those reviews created or whether the flag was any good.
**Next:** Each month, read one closed file with no finding. Ask what could have told you sooner, and what it cost the provider.

### Scenario

**Context:** Two of your reviewers looked at similar documentation problems. One recommended technical assistance. The other recommended referral for further action. Neither record says why. Both reviewers are experienced and each believes they were right. One provider is large. The other is small and serves mostly families who speak a language other than English.
**Prompt:** What do you do next?

**Option:** Back the reviewer you trust more.
**Response:** That might be right in the case at hand, but you would be deciding on trust. There's no record that anyone else could check, and the same gap would appear in the next case.

**Option:** Assume the different result reflects bias, and overturn the stricter one.
**Response:** You don't know that yet. Maybe the facts differ. Assuming bias without looking isn't fair to the reviewer, and it doesn't fix the process either.

**Option (recommended):** Have each reviewer write down what they saw and which criterion they used. Compare the facts, side by side. If facts match, align the response according to the written criteria. If there are no written criteria, raise that with your manager and the responsible office.
**Response:** This tests for real differences, keeps the discussion on evidence and treats both providers by the same standard. It also shows you whether the guidance needs work.

### Knowledge check

**Question:** What is the main test of a good review record?
**Options:**
- It is long.
- It uses strong language.
- Someone who was not involved can read it and see why each step happened.
- It contains every email about the case.

**Correct answer:** Someone who was not involved can read it and see why each step happened.
**Feedback if correct:** Right. That's what makes it possible to check for consistency and fix mistakes.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Think about who a record is for. A good record lets someone else follow the reasoning.

### Transfer prompt

**Prompt:** Think of the next hard call in your team. Finish this plan: "If a reviewer brings me a case that could go either way, then I will ask ____ to read the record first, without seeing the conclusion, and I'll ask ____."

## Lesson 4: Language and communication access in oversight

**Summary:** Reviews, requests and notices have to be understood before they can be fair. This lesson covers practical access.
**Minutes:** 12

**Objectives:**
- Describe the status of language access duties as of October 2026.
- Plan access for site visits, record requests and notices.
- Write about conduct and process rather than communities.

**Takeaways:**
- Federal language-access guidance has weakened. Title VI and Section 1557 remain statutes.
- A provider who can't understand a request can't fix a problem.
- Tell people what's happening, what it means for their services and whom to contact.

### Text: Where language access stands

As of October 2026, Executive Order 13166 on language access has been revoked by Executive Order 14224, and federal guidance has been pulled back. DOJ's July 2025 guidance is reported to tell federal agencies to minimize nonessential multilingual services and to state that Title VI violations require intent. Replacement guidance is reported as not yet issued as of January 2026. Title VI and Section 1557 remain statutes. Whether DSD's activities fall under Section 1557, and any Minnesota-specific duties, were not verified for this course, so ask your legal staff.

Do not rest your practice on the revoked order or on old guidance. Rest it on what the work needs. Oversight depends on accurate information. A misread notice or a badly interpreted site visit produces wrong findings. Communication access for Deaf, DeafBlind and hard of hearing people, and accessible formats for blind people and others, are disability access matters. Ask your ADA coordinator.

### Tabs: Where access comes up

#### Tab: Site visits

Find out ahead of time, where visits are announced, what language or communication support might be needed. For unannounced visits, have a plan to get a qualified interpreter by phone or video. Don't rely on the provider's staff or a family member to interpret anything that matters. Note in the record who interpreted.

#### Tab: Requests for records

Say exactly what you need, by when, and why. Use plain words. Give a named contact and a way to ask questions. If a request is a surprise and it's complicated, a small provider may fail to respond for reasons unrelated to the conduct you are looking at.

#### Tab: Notices

Use the responsible office's templates, and check that readers can tell what is happening, why, what they can do and by when. If a notice can't be read by the person it's for, tell the office that owns it. Ask how notices reach people who use other languages or formats.

#### Tab: Talking with people who get services

Keep it practical. What's happening? Are my services affected? How do I find another provider? Whom do I call? Point to official resources. Do not guess.

### List: How to write and talk about this work

- Describe conduct and process. Do not describe communities.
- Don't link fraud to a language group, a faith or a national origin, even as background.
- Don't confirm or discuss an open case outside your role.
- Send press questions to your communications office.
- Check how a statement might sound to a family who gets services lawfully from a provider in the same community.

### Leader move: Test the notice on a real reader

**Control:** You can ask whether the people who get your team's letters can understand them.
**Failure:** A letter is legally complete and practically unreadable, and the missed deadline gets recorded as non-cooperation.
**Next:** Have someone outside your team read one letter you send. Ask them what it wants them to do, and by when. Tell the template owner what you learn.

### Scenario

**Context:** A reviewer returns from a site visit, reporting that the provider's owner "seemed evasive" and gave answers that didn't match the records. The owner's first language is not English. The owner's teenage child interpreted for part of the visit, because no interpreter had been arranged.
**Prompt:** What do you do next?

**Option:** Record the owner as evasive, because the answers did not match.
**Response:** You can't tell from this whether the owner was evasive or whether the interpretation failed. A record that says "evasive" may do real damage, and it might be wrong.

**Option:** Discard the whole visit and close the review.
**Response:** The records may still raise questions that need answers. Ending the review because of an access problem isn't the right fix.

**Option (recommended):** Treat the answers as unreliable, and note who interpreted and why. Arrange a follow-up with a qualified interpreter. Compare records against what you learn then. Change your visit plan so this doesn't repeat.
**Response:** This protects accuracy, which serves both duties. If there's a real problem, a clean follow-up will make the record much stronger.

### Knowledge check

**Question:** Which statement fits this course, as of October 2026?
**Options:**
- Language access duties ended when EO 13166 was revoked.
- EO 13166 is revoked and federal guidance is pulling back, while Title VI and Section 1557 remain statutes.
- All DSD activities are confirmed as covered by Section 1557.
- Language access only matters if a provider files a complaint.

**Correct answer:** EO 13166 is revoked and federal guidance is pulling back, while Title VI and Section 1557 remain statutes.
**Feedback if correct:** Right. Whether specific activities are covered is a question for your legal staff, and accuracy is a practical reason to provide access either way.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Look for the answer that notes both the federal change and what remains, without claiming more than what's been verified.

### Transfer prompt

**Prompt:** Look at the next site visit, records request or notice from your team. Finish this plan: "If ____ goes out next, then I will first check how someone who doesn't read English well, or who uses another format, would get and understand it, and I will ask ____."

## Lesson 5: Pattern review and supervising under pressure

**Summary:** How to check whether your method works and falls evenly, and how to help your staff speak up when it does not.
**Minutes:** 13

**Objectives:**
- Track confirmation rates and burden across provider types.
- Avoid both quotas and conclusions about communities.
- Make it safe for staff to say that a method isn't working.

**Takeaways:**
- Track how many flags are confirmed, not just how many are raised.
- Use patterns to fix the method. Don't use them to make claims about communities.
- Pressure for speed makes criteria drift. Protect your people's ability to say so.

### Text: Questions a pattern review asks

Ask how many cases were flagged and how many were confirmed after review. Compare that by provider size, region and type where data allow. Where the numbers are sound and the data appropriate, also compare by the communities served. Look at time to resolve, the number of people whose services were disrupted, how quickly they were reconnected, and appeal outcomes. If a method flags one kind of provider often and rarely confirms anything, it's creating burden without benefit. Revise it.

Report fraud results and access effects together. A count of findings by itself tells leaders little. A count with no account of who lost services hides half the job.

### Accordion: Traps

#### Item: Quotas

Don't set a number of reviews for each group or region. This repeats the original mistake and doesn't test anything about accuracy.

#### Item: Conclusions about communities

A confirmed rate is about the method and the cases reviewed. It doesn't tell you about a community. If you looked only in one place, your data can only speak to that place.

#### Item: Small numbers

A few cases in a small region can look dramatic. Check the count before you draw any conclusion. Reading Patterns and Reviewing Process gives more detail, including a floor of 10 respondents for staff survey results and how to think about other small numbers.

#### Item: Treating a difference as proof

A difference tells you where to look. It doesn't prove that a method is unfair, and it doesn't prove that it's fair. Follow up with a review of cases and criteria.

### Tabs: Supervising under pressure

#### Tab: What pressure does

When leaders want fast results, people cut corners. Second reads go away. "Possible" becomes "fraud" in a summary. Staff who raise concerns may fear looking soft. There isn't much research on this in program integrity, so use your own judgment here.

#### Tab: What you can do

Ask, in regular meetings, "What's a flag or a method that's not working?" Thank people who say so. Take what they tell you upward. When you get pressure to skip a step, tell your manager what will be lost, and let them decide with that knowledge.

#### Tab: Protecting staff

A staff member who raises a discrimination concern may be protected against retaliation, so ask Human Resources. Either way, do not treat them worse for raising it. Concerns of discrimination against staff go to Human Resources or the Affirmative Action Officer. Concerns about how providers are treated can go to your manager and the responsible office. Ask where to take them if you don't know.

#### Tab: Your staff's own experience

Reviewers may carry heavy work, angry contacts and painful cases about harm to people. Staff from the communities under scrutiny may hear things that are hard to hear. Do not ask them to speak for anyone. Ask each person what they need. See Your Own Load.

### Leader move: Report both columns

**Control:** You control what goes in your team's summary.
**Failure:** A monthly report lists actions taken and dollars identified. Nobody knows how many flags were wrong or how many people lost a provider.
**Next:** Add one line to your next report: how many flagged cases were confirmed. If you don't have that number, write down who would.

### Scenario

**Context:** Your team's numbers for the last six months show that small providers in one metro area were flagged much more often than others, and few of those flags were confirmed. A colleague says that the high flag count shows that the area has a fraud problem. A reporter has asked your communications office about the review, and you've been asked for talking points.
**Prompt:** What do you do next?

**Option:** Provide talking points that describe a growing problem in that area, to show that DHS is acting.
**Response:** Few flags were confirmed, so that statement isn't supported. It could also stigmatize families and honest providers in that area.

**Option (recommended):** Provide accurate points on the process and the conduct it looks for, with no reference to a community, and no claim of fraud where none has been confirmed. Separately, take the low confirmation rate to your manager and the owner of the method, and ask to revise it.
**Response:** This is truthful and it addresses the real finding, which is about the method. Reviewing can continue while the flag gets fixed.

**Option:** Stop all reviews in that area until attention fades.
**Response:** This abandons real review, and it may be read as treating an area differently because of who lives there. Fix the method. Don't start and stop by location.

**Option:** Tell staff to spread flags across other areas, so the numbers look more even.
**Response:** This would be a quota in disguise. It makes the chart look better while lowering accuracy everywhere.

### Knowledge check

**Question:** A flag fires often for one kind of provider, and few of those flags are confirmed. What does this most directly suggest?
**Options:**
- That kind of provider is hiding fraud better.
- The method may be creating burden without benefit, and should be reviewed.
- More reviews should be assigned to other kinds of providers, to even things out.
- Nothing, since flags don't need to be confirmed.

**Correct answer:** The method may be creating burden without benefit, and should be reviewed.
**Feedback if correct:** Right. A low confirmation rate is a finding about the method.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Look for the answer that looks at the method. Evening out the numbers, or assuming hidden fraud, doesn't test anything.

### Transfer prompt

**Prompt:** Find out whether your team can say how many of its flags get confirmed. Finish this plan: "If I ask ____ for the confirmation rate on ____, and it's low or unknown, then I'll take it to ____ by ____."

## Job aid

**Title:** Fair oversight checklist for supervisors
**Subtitle:** Accurate, consistent, explainable

**Purpose:** Help supervisors of review and program staff keep oversight firm and fair, using habits they control.

**Remember:**
- Fraud harms people. Careless oversight harms people.
- A flag is a lead. A gap is not fraud.
- Criteria describe conduct, not community.
- Decisions on suspensions, sanctions, referrals and appeals belong to the responsible offices.

### Criteria

- What conduct does this catch?
- Where did it come from, and what did past reviewers look at?
- How many of its flags were confirmed?
- Whom does it fall on, by provider size and region, and where sound, by communities served?

### Records

- Why picked, what checked, what innocent explanations were considered, what concluded, what next, who reviewed.
- Second reader on hard calls and on a sample.
- Note who interpreted, and any access problems.

### Access

- Qualified interpreter, not family or provider staff, for anything that matters.
- Plain requests with a date, a reason and a named contact.
- Ask the ADA coordinator about disability access, and your legal staff about language-access duties.

### Words

- Describe conduct and process, never communities.
- Use "fraud" only where the responsible office has reason to.
- Tell people what's happening, whether services are affected, and whom to call.

### Patterns

- Track confirmed cases as well as flags.
- Report fraud results and access effects together.
- No quotas. No claims about communities.

**Do next:** Choose one rule your team uses. Ask what conduct it catches and how many flags were confirmed. Take the answer to your manager.
