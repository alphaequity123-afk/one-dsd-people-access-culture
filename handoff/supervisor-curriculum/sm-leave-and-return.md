# sm-leave-and-return: Leave and Return to Work

**Subtitle:** What a supervisor does when someone needs time away, how to cover the work fairly while they are gone, and how to bring them back without losing their accommodation, their assignments or their standing.
**Duration:** About 54 minutes
**Content type:** formal-support
**Status:** reviewed
**Last reviewed:** October 31, 2026
**Evidence date:** October 2026
**Scope:** For supervisors and managers of any kind of team. This is general information, not legal advice. It is voluntary. It does not decide who qualifies for leave or what any contract provides.

**Course objectives:**
- Respond well when someone first tells you they need time away, and route it the same day.
- Build a coverage plan that does not quietly harm the people who stay.
- Plan a return that keeps the person's work, standing and any accommodation.
- Keep time away from counting against someone in ratings, assignments and opportunities.

**Applied next step:** Ask your Human Resources contact for the current leave process that applies to your team. Write down who you call, what you are expected to do, and what you are not supposed to ask.

## Lesson 1: When someone says they need time away

**Summary:** Your first response shapes how safe it feels to use leave. This lesson covers what to say, what to ask, what not to ask and who decides.
**Minutes:** 13

**Objectives:**
- Recognize a leave or accommodation need in ordinary words.
- Give a first response that is warm, brief and useful.
- Separate what you need to know from what you do not.
- Route the matter promptly and apply the same response to everyone.

**Takeaways:**
- People rarely use the formal words.
- You do not decide eligibility. Human Resources does.
- You need dates and coverage facts, not a diagnosis or a story.
- Your reaction will be remembered, and the rest of the team will hear about it.

### Text: What this course will not tell you
Leave has many sources. Federal law, state law, the labor agreement or plan covering the person, and state policy may all apply, and several may apply at once. They differ on who qualifies, how long leave lasts, whether it is paid, what notice is required and what paperwork is allowed. Those details change, and they depend on the person.

So this course does not teach eligibility. If you learn one rule from it, make it this: you do not decide whether someone qualifies, and you do not tell anyone they do not. You respond, you route, and you make the work function. Your contact in Human Resources holds the rules.

### Statement
You do not need to know the leave rules. You need to know whom to call, and call them today.

### Text: It rarely comes in formal words
"My mother's surgery is next month and I will have to be there for a while." "We are expecting a baby in the spring." "My doctor wants me to slow down." "I need to go home for a funeral, and it will be more than a few days." "I keep having flare-ups, and I'm not sure I can keep this schedule."

Any of these may raise leave, accommodation or both. For accommodation, EEOC guidance dated October 17, 2002 says no magic words are needed, and a request may be oral or may come from someone else on the employee's behalf. The same guidance warns that unnecessary delay can violate the ADA, and that a supervisor who sits on a request can be treated as having effectively denied it. It is agency guidance and it states that it lacks the force of law, but it remains in effect until rescinded or superseded, and it is the practical standard to work to.

Minnesota adds its own text. The Minnesota Human Rights Act describes accommodation through an informal interactive process (section 363A.08, subdivision 6), and says failure to engage in that process may be an unfair practice (section 363A.02, subdivision 1(c)). In 2024, the Legislature also added episodic or in-remission impairments to the definition of disability. So someone whose condition comes and goes is not outside your concern because they were fine last month.

### List: A first response that works
1. "Thank you for telling me. I'm glad you told me early." Or, if it's hard news, simply, "I'm sorry. Thank you for telling me."
2. "You don't need to tell me more about the reason than you want to."
3. "Human Resources handles the leave process. I'll let them know today, or you can contact them directly. Which do you prefer?"
4. "What dates do you know now? We can sort out the work once you know more."
5. "Your job right now is to take care of this. I'll work out the coverage."

### Tabs: What you may need, and what you do not
#### Tab: You may need
The dates or the likely dates. Whether they can be reached, and how, if they want to be. What is in progress and who should know. Anything that has to be handled before they leave. Any restrictions or accommodations, as Human Resources tells you. Minnesota Statutes section 363A.20, subdivision 8 says supervisors and managers may be told necessary restrictions and accommodations.

#### Tab: You do not need
The diagnosis. The treatment. The prognosis. Who else in the family is involved or how sick they are. Whether they "really" need all that time. Whether they plan to come back, if they haven't said. Medical information belongs in separate confidential files, under section 363A.20, and does not go in your supervisor file or your email.

#### Tab: If you were told anyway
People often share more than they have to. Listen, thank them and keep it private. Do not pass it on to your manager, your team or a colleague. Do not write it down beyond what the process requires. If you are not sure what to do with something you were told, ask Human Resources in general terms.

### Accordion: Responses that cost people
#### Item: "How long will you be out?" as the first words
It tells the person that the work is what you heard. Coverage matters, and it can wait for the second minute.

#### Item: "Can you push it to after the session?"
Even as a light question, it can discourage someone from using leave they are entitled to. Do not ask anyone to delay. If a timing question is genuine, ask Human Resources how to raise it.

#### Item: "We really need you right now"
This is a compliment that turns into pressure. People remember it.

#### Item: Defining family or a proper reason yourself
Who counts as family and what is owed at a death or a birth differ from one household to the next. Do not decide by your own picture of what is normal. Ask Human Resources what applies, and treat each person's request with the same seriousness.

### Leader move: Same response for everyone
**Control:** You control whether your first response is the same for a new parent, a person with a chronic illness, someone caring for a parent, and someone going home for a funeral.
**Failure:** Do not be warmer about the reasons you understand from your own life and cooler about the ones you do not. Teams notice who gets which reaction.
**Next:** Write your own version of the five lines above, in your own words, and keep them handy.

### Scenario
**Context:** A policy specialist on your team tells you, near the end of a hard week, "I've been having health issues and my doctor wants me to take some time. I don't really know how long. I know the legislative work is coming up." You are short-staffed, and the legislative work is due in three weeks.
**Prompt:** What is your best first response?

**Option 1:** "Oh no, what's going on? Is it serious?"
**Response:** It comes from care, but it asks for medical detail you do not need. The person may feel they have to explain to earn your support.

**Option 2:** "Thank you for telling me. You don't owe me details. Human Resources handles this, and I'll contact them today unless you'd rather do it. Let me worry about the legislative work."
**Response:** This is the best response. You thank them, take the pressure off, route it the same day and take ownership of the work problem.
**Recommended:** yes

**Option 3:** "Can you get me a doctor's note with the dates, so I can plan?"
**Response:** Documentation is the process's job, and it is limited to what is needed. Asking for a note yourself pushes medical information to the wrong person.

**Option 4:** "Would it be possible to wait until after the legislative work, three weeks from now?"
**Response:** This puts your staffing problem on them, and you have no idea what waiting would cost. It can also discourage them from using what they are entitled to.

### Knowledge check
**Question:** An employee says they keep having flare-ups of a condition that were not a problem last year, and asks if they can change their hours. What should you do?
- Tell them to wait until there's a doctor's note, since the condition isn't constant.
- Treat it as a possible accommodation request or leave need, and contact Human Resources or the ADA coordinator promptly. (correct)
- Decline it, since a condition that comes and goes doesn't count.
- Ask what the diagnosis is, so you can tell whether it counts.
**Correct answer:** Treat it as a possible accommodation request or leave need, and contact Human Resources or the ADA coordinator promptly.
**Feedback if correct:** Yes. No special words are needed, and Minnesota's 2024 change added episodic or in-remission impairments to the definition of disability. Delay can itself be a problem.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Look again. No special words are needed, and a condition that comes and goes can still count. You don't need the diagnosis. You need to route it promptly.

### Transfer prompt
**Prompt:** Write one if-then plan: "If someone tells me they need time away, then I will first say ____, and I will contact ____ the same day."
**Options:**
- If they share a health reason, then I will thank them and not ask for more.
- If they ask me to delay telling anyone, then I will ask Human Resources how to handle it, without details.
- If I feel my staffing panic rising, then I will take it to my manager and not to them.

## Lesson 2: Covering the work without harming the people who stay

**Summary:** Coverage is where leave turns into a fairness problem, for the person away and for those who pick up the work. This lesson gives you a method.
**Minutes:** 13

**Objectives:**
- Separate the work that must continue from the work that can wait or stop.
- Distribute coverage using stated reasons rather than who is willing.
- Use leave coverage as an open opportunity.
- Explain the coverage to the team without revealing the reason.

**Takeaways:**
- Coverage that lands on the same willing person is not a plan.
- Some work should stop. Say so out loud.
- Acting and stretch work created by leave should be offered by criteria.
- The team needs to know what changes, not why someone is away.

### Text: Why coverage is a fairness decision
When someone goes on leave, the usual move is to ask who can pick it up. The most willing person says yes. They are often the same person each time, and it may be the person with the least standing to say no. They get thanked. Three months later they are worn out, their own work has slipped and no one has written it down.

There is some evidence that this deserves care. In a study by Hammer and colleagues (2011), grocery supervisors received family-supportive training. Outcomes improved for employees with high family-to-work conflict and got worse for those with low conflict. That study was about training, not coverage, and it was in a very different kind of workplace. It is still a useful warning: an average can hide people who gain and people who lose. Support for one employee that is paid for by another is not support at the team level.

### List: A coverage plan in five questions
1. What must continue, because people or deadlines depend on it? In DSD work, that includes the people on someone's caseload, providers waiting for answers, and legal or legislative dates.
2. What can wait, and until when?
3. What will stop? Name it, and tell your manager in writing.
4. Who covers what, and for what stated reason (skill, capacity, development)?
5. What comes off the plate of each person who takes something on?

### Statement
If nothing comes off anyone's plate, you do not have a coverage plan. You have a request for unpaid effort.

### Tabs: Three kinds of work to look at
#### Tab: Work with people who depend on it
People receiving services, families, counties and providers should not have to absorb the gap. Tell them who to contact. If the person on leave used an interpreter, a particular format or a particular way of working with someone, make sure the handoff says so. Do not pass along any reason for the absence.

#### Tab: Visible and valuable work
A briefing, a stakeholder group or a high-profile project may open up. That is a real opportunity for someone. Offer it using stated criteria, and tell the team what those are. EEOC technical assistance from March 2025 treats access to training, mentoring and leadership development as terms or privileges of employment. That is an agency position rather than law, but it matches what fairness requires anyway. Also write down that this is temporary, and what will happen on return.

#### Tab: Hidden work
Who books the interpreter? Who answers the new person's questions? Who catches errors in the shared tracker? Hidden work is often what falls first after someone leaves, and no one notices until something breaks. Ask the person, if they are available before leaving, what no one else knows they do. "Recognizing hidden and invisible work" (dc-t26) can help you see it.

### Text: What to tell the team
Tell them what is changing. Do not tell them why. Something like: "One of our colleagues will be out for a while. Here's who is covering what, and here's what is on hold. I'll update you on [date]." If someone asks why, say: "I keep individual arrangements private for everyone, including you."

Do not confirm a guess, even a kind one. Do not let the person's absence become the topic of a team card or a collection unless they have said they want that. Ask them, if you can do so through a channel they've agreed to.

### Leader move: Telling your manager the truth
**Control:** You control whether the cost of the absence is visible. Write down what will stop or slip and who is taking on what.
**Failure:** Do not absorb the work yourself, and do not report that everything is fine. If you hide the gap, your manager has no reason to help.
**Next:** Send your manager the plan: what continues, what waits, what stops, who covers, and what you need from them. Leave out the reason for the absence.

### Scenario
**Context:** A case consultant goes on leave for an unknown length of time. Your most reliable consultant immediately offers to take the whole caseload. That person has done this twice before and has a heavy load already. Another consultant has less experience, and has asked for more complex work. Two cases involve people who communicate through an interpreter, and one of the two is mid-way through a reassessment.
**Prompt:** What do you do next?

**Option 1:** Accept the offer. They volunteered, they know the work, and the cases will be in good hands.
**Response:** This solves today's problem. It also repeats a pattern, hides the cost and passes up a real development chance for someone else. An offer is not the same as capacity.

**Option 2:** List the cases by urgency and complexity. Split them using stated reasons, with the newer consultant taking some with support. Name what comes off each person's plate, and make sure the handoff records each person's communication needs.
**Response:** This is the best step. People get continuity, the work is spread by reasons you can explain, someone develops, and interpreter arrangements don't get lost.
**Recommended:** yes

**Option 3:** Divide the caseload equally among everyone, so it is fair.
**Response:** Equal counts are not equal demands. A reassessment with an interpreter does not weigh the same as a routine renewal. Compare the actual work.

**Option 4:** Tell the team that you'll handle the cases yourself until the consultant is back.
**Response:** You may need to take some of it for a few days. For an unknown period, this hides the problem from your manager and takes you away from supervising. See the course "Your Own Load" (sm-your-own-load).

### Knowledge check
**Question:** A team member asks why a colleague has been out for three weeks. What fits this lesson?
- Share the general reason, since the team cares and needs to understand.
- Say you keep individual arrangements private for everyone, and tell them what the work plan is. (correct)
- Tell them to ask the colleague.
- Confirm their guess if it's a kind one.
**Correct answer:** Say you keep individual arrangements private for everyone, and tell them what the work plan is.
**Feedback if correct:** Yes. The team needs to know what is changing for them. The reason belongs to the person who is away.
**Feedback if incorrect:** The reason belongs to the person who is away, and sending someone to ask them is no better. The team needs the work plan, and a clear statement that privacy applies to everyone.

### Transfer prompt
**Prompt:** Think of the next likely absence on your team. Write one if-then plan: "If ____ is away, then the first thing I will list is ____, and I will take ____ off ____'s plate."
**Options:**
- If one person keeps volunteering, then I will thank them and ask what they will drop.
- If visible work opens up, then I will state the criteria before I offer it.
- If someone works with an interpreter or a specific format, then I will make that part of the handoff.

## Lesson 3: Contact and the return

**Summary:** How much to stay in touch, how to prepare for someone's return, and how to bring a person back into their job, including any restrictions or accommodation.
**Minutes:** 14

**Objectives:**
- Agree on contact during leave without creating pressure.
- Plan a return conversation that focuses on the work.
- Respond properly to restrictions or new accommodation needs at return.
- Restore assignments and carry accommodations forward.

**Takeaways:**
- Ask how they want to hear from you. Then do that, and no more.
- A return is a plan, not a date.
- Restrictions come through the process. You need the what, not the why.
- Do not quietly keep the good work with the person who covered it.

### Text: Contact during leave
Some people want updates. Some want silence. Few want to be asked questions about work. Before leave starts, or through Human Resources if it started suddenly, ask: "How would you like to hear from me, if at all? What do you want to know about?"

Ask Human Resources whether there's anything you need to be careful about. Do not ask someone on leave to do work, even small things like "just one quick question." If something truly requires them, go through Human Resources. Do not take their silence personally, and do not read their social media.

### Text: Planning the return
Ask Human Resources what happens at return, what they need from you, and what you may be told. Then think of the return as something to prepare, as you would for a new team member, except that this person already belongs.

### List: Before the first day back
- Confirm the date and any restrictions or arrangements with Human Resources.
- Check that their access still works: accounts, equipment, any assistive technology, and any interpreter or captioning arrangement.
- Prepare a short written update: what changed, what decisions were made, what is waiting for them.
- Decide what comes back to them and when. Tell those covering what will happen.
- Hold the first one-to-one on day one or day two, and put it on the calendar now.

### Statement
A person coming back should find their job, not a smaller one.

### Accordion: The return conversation
#### Item: How to open
"Welcome back. It's good to see you. I don't need you to tell me anything about your time away. Here's what's been happening with the work, and here's what I thought we'd do in the first couple of weeks. What would help?"

#### Item: What to ask
What would make the first few weeks work? Is there anything in how the work reaches you that you want to change? Do you want a say in how we hand things back?

#### Item: What not to ask
"Are you all better?" "Are you sure you're ready?" "Is it going to happen again?" "Are you planning on more time off?" Each one of those can come from care, and each asks for private information or suggests doubt.

#### Item: What the team may do
Colleagues may ask them questions. If they want you to say something to the team, ask them what. If they want it left alone, leave it alone and quietly address any comments you hear.

### Tabs: Restrictions and accommodation at return
#### Tab: Restrictions come through the process
If Human Resources tells you about restrictions, ask what they mean for the work. You may be told necessary restrictions and accommodations under section 363A.20, subdivision 8. You should not get the medical file, and you should not request it yourself.

#### Tab: They ask for a change
Any request at return is a possible accommodation request. It doesn't need special words. Respond promptly and bring in Human Resources or the ADA coordinator. Talk through what would work, in the informal interactive process that Minnesota law describes. The employee's preference gets primary consideration, while the employer has ultimate discretion among effective options, under the EEOC guidance. For MAPE-represented staff, Article 31 of the 2025-2027 contract provides union representation in accommodation at the employee's request. For others, check their contract or plan.

#### Tab: There was an accommodation before
It should not disappear because someone was away or because you are new. Keep what works. Do not ask for new medical information. "Accommodations Across Transitions" (gc-accommodation-transitions) calls this the accommodation cliff and shows how to carry them forward.

#### Tab: You are worried about safety
You may worry that someone isn't ready, especially in work with safety stakes. Do not decide this yourself, do not ask them to prove it, and do not send them home on your own judgment unless there's an immediate danger, in which case follow your emergency procedures. Describe what you actually saw to Human Resources and ask what to do. A diagnosis alone does not establish danger.

### Text: Handing back the work
Here is the fairness question that people skip. While someone was away, another person took on a visible project and is doing well. The easy thing is to leave it with them. The person who returns then finds that they have less visible work, with no decision ever having been made.

*Muldrow v. City of St. Louis* (April 17, 2024) is about transfers, but it makes the point: rank and pay can stay the same while duties, schedule and perks change, and that can count as harm if a protected trait motivated it. You may have good reasons to make a change. If so, make it as a decision: state your reason, check it with Human Resources and tell the person directly. Check what the contract says too. Ask Human Resources what the person's rights on return are before you change anything.

Credit the person who covered. Put it in their record for development. Find them their next opportunity some other way.

### Leader move: Do not make them earn it back
**Control:** You control whether the return begins with their normal work, normal trust and normal access.
**Failure:** Do not start with lighter work "to ease them in" unless they've asked for it or the process says so. A protective choice can look a lot like a demotion.
**Next:** Ask what they would like, tell them what you've planned, and adjust together in the first month.

### Scenario
**Context:** A senior consultant returns after three months away. While they were gone, a colleague took over their lead role on a stakeholder group, and the group likes the colleague. Human Resources has told you there are no restrictions. You think it might be simpler to keep the colleague in the lead and give the returning consultant a lighter set of cases "so they aren't overwhelmed."
**Prompt:** What do you do?

**Option 1:** Keep the colleague in the lead and tell the returning consultant it's temporary, to help them settle in.
**Response:** You've made a decision about their job based on an assumption about their capacity, right after protected time away. "Temporary" often isn't. This is the kind of change that can do harm, even with pay unchanged.

**Option 2:** Ask Human Resources what applies on return, plan to hand the lead back, and talk with the returning consultant about timing. Then credit the colleague and find them a next opportunity.
**Response:** This is the best step. The default is that the person gets their job back. Any change becomes an open decision, and the colleague's good work is recognized.
**Recommended:** yes

**Option 3:** Ask the returning consultant whether they are really up to the lead role yet.
**Response:** This invites them to prove their health. There are no restrictions, so the question isn't yours to raise this way. You can ask what would help them in the first few weeks.

**Option 4:** Let the two of them work it out.
**Response:** This hands a management decision to two colleagues with unequal information, and it's likely to create tension. It's your decision to make clearly.

### Knowledge check
**Question:** An employee returns with work restrictions from Human Resources. What are you entitled to under Minnesota Statutes section 363A.20, subdivision 8?
- The full medical file, so you can plan.
- The necessary restrictions and accommodations. (correct)
- The diagnosis, as long as you keep it confidential.
- Nothing at all, so you must guess.
**Correct answer:** The necessary restrictions and accommodations.
**Feedback if correct:** Right. Medical information goes in separate confidential files. Supervisors and managers may be told necessary restrictions and accommodations, which is what you need to organize the work.
**Feedback if incorrect:** The law strikes a balance here. Medical information stays in separate confidential files, and you may be told the necessary restrictions and accommodations.

### Transfer prompt
**Prompt:** Write one if-then plan: "If someone on my team returns from leave, then by the first day back I will have ____, and in the first week I will ____."
**Options:**
- If someone has covered visible work, then I will decide openly what goes back, with Human Resources.
- If there was an accommodation before, then I will confirm it is in place before they arrive.
- If they use assistive technology or an interpreter, then I will check it the week before they return.

## Lesson 4: After the return: no penalty, and fair questions

**Summary:** Time away can quietly cost people in ratings, opportunities and reputation. This lesson shows how to prevent that and how to handle the hard cases, including repeated absences and real performance problems.
**Minutes:** 14

**Objectives:**
- Rate and assign someone fairly after time away.
- Respond to team comments about absence and workload.
- Address a real performance concern without guessing at the cause.
- Know what to route when leave runs out or absences recur.

**Takeaways:**
- Rate the work they did in the time they were there.
- Availability is often a proxy. Check whether the work actually requires it.
- A performance concern is about the work, and it goes through Human Resources.
- Never tell someone their leave is running out, or what happens next, on your own.

### Text: Where the cost shows up
Here are the common places: a rating that compares a person's output across a shortened year to others' across a full one; a comment in a review that says "was not always available"; a stretch assignment not offered because "they've had a lot going on"; not being nominated because you assume they would not want it; colleagues who begin working around them.

Asking for accommodation is protected, and the courses on retaliation and accommodation explain it. In practice, keep this question in front of you: if the person had not been away, would I be making this same decision? If the answer is no, you need a very good work reason, and you should look at it with Human Resources.

### List: At rating time
- Rate what they did while they were at work, against the same standard.
- Do not count approved time away against them. Ask Human Resources how your process handles a shortened rating period.
- Check for proxies such as "presence," "availability," "reliability" or "commitment." Are those about the work, or about being around?
- Read your draft next to one for a colleague who wasn't away. Would you use these words for them?
- Do not mention the reason for absence in any review.

### Accordion: When the team has feelings
#### Item: "It's not fair that I had to cover"
It may not have been handled fairly. Listen first. Talk about the plan, what you can change, and what you'll do next time. Do not defend the absence or explain it. "You're right that this has been a lot, and I should have taken more off your plate. Here's what I'm changing."

#### Item: "They always seem to be out"
Answer plainly: "I don't discuss anyone's arrangements, and I wouldn't discuss yours. If your workload is the problem, let's talk about that." If comments continue or take a turn toward someone's disability, pregnancy or other protected trait, treat it as a conduct issue. You may have to report it under Policy 1436.

#### Item: Gossip about what's really going on
Do not join in, and do not correct it with facts. Ask people to stop. Make it clear to everyone that their own private matters would be treated the same way.

### Text: If there's a real performance problem
Someone back from leave can have a real performance problem. It may be related to the reason they were away, or it may not, and you shouldn't guess. The course "Inclusive Supervision and Team Culture" (di-inclusive-supervision-and-team-culture) and the course "Mental health, disability, and workplace support" (dc-l70) cover how to talk about the work. Here's the short version.

Talk about what you've observed in the work and what is expected. Mention that the accommodation process and other supports are open to anyone. Do not name your suspicion. Before anything formal, call Human Resources. See the course "Corrective Action and Labor Agreements" (sm-corrective-action).

### Tabs: Hard cases to route, not decide
#### Tab: Absences that keep recurring
Intermittent or repeated absences can be protected. Do not start counting them or apply an attendance rule on your own. Call Human Resources with the dates and what's happening with the work.

#### Tab: Leave is running out
If the person asks "what happens when my leave runs out?", do not answer from memory. Say, "That's a question for Human Resources, and it matters to get it right. I can help you reach them today." Do not suggest resigning, and do not suggest a part-time arrangement on your own.

#### Tab: They ask for more time
A request for more time may also be an accommodation request. Route it the same day. Do not say no. Do not say yes on your own authority either.

#### Tab: They do not come back
If someone doesn't return when expected, don't assume they've quit. Call Human Resources before you do anything or tell the team anything.

### Text: What to watch for over time
Evidence on leave and supervision specifically is thin. Research on supervisors and retention more broadly is stronger. Hoffman and Tadelis (2021) found that survey-measured people management skills strongly predict lower turnover at one high-tech firm, with designs that support a causal reading. Kelly and colleagues (2014), in a randomized study at an IT workplace, combined supervisor support training with changes to work practices and found modest improvements in work-family conflict and larger changes in schedule control and supervisor support. Neither study is about Minnesota state government or about leave as such. Both point to what you control: how you treat people when they have lives outside work.

### Leader move: Six months later
**Control:** You control whether you check. Look at who has received stretch work, nominations and visible roles since the return.
**Failure:** Do not assume that because the return went well, the cost did not arrive later.
**Next:** Put a reminder on your calendar six months after any long leave, and compare the person's assignments with what they had before.

### Scenario
**Context:** You're preparing mid-year reviews. A program consultant was out for ten weeks on approved leave. Their work, while they were at work, was good. Your draft says: "Output was lower than peers this period and availability was a challenge at times. Should demonstrate more consistent presence next year." A stretch assignment is also coming up, and you were going to skip them "so they can get their feet back under them."
**Prompt:** What do you do with the draft and the assignment?

**Option 1:** Keep the draft. It's accurate, and output really was lower.
**Response:** It's accurate in a misleading way. You'd be counting approved time away against them, and "presence" and "availability" have no tie to a work result here. This is a place where a review can turn into a penalty for leave.

**Option 2:** Rewrite the review around the work they did in the time they were there, ask Human Resources how a shortened period is handled, and offer the stretch assignment on the same criteria as everyone else.
**Response:** This is the best step. The rating is fair, you've checked the process, and they get to decide about the assignment themselves.
**Recommended:** yes

**Option 3:** Remove the comments, and give them a slightly higher rating to make up for it.
**Response:** Inflating a rating isn't fair to them or their colleagues, and it doesn't reflect the record. Rate the work against the same standard.

**Option 4:** Ask them privately if they feel their health will let them take on the stretch assignment.
**Response:** Ask anyone whether they have capacity and interest, in the same words. Asking about their health specifically isn't your question to ask. Let them decide.

### Knowledge check
**Question:** An employee asks you, "What happens to my job when my leave runs out?" What should you do?
- Tell them what you remember from the last time this happened.
- Suggest they consider resigning, as that can look better on their record.
- Say it matters to get it right, and help them reach Human Resources today. (correct)
- Say not to worry, as their job is safe.

## Job aid

**Title:** Leave and return: what you do, and what you route
**Subtitle:** For the first call and the first day back.

**Purpose:** Help you respond well, keep the work running and bring people back to their own job, while Human Resources handles the rules.

**Remember:**
- You do not decide eligibility. Route it the same day.
- No special words are needed.
- You need dates and restrictions. You do not need the diagnosis.
- Time away does not count against someone.

### Section: When someone says they need time away
- Thank them. Do not ask why.
- Offer to contact Human Resources today, or let them do it.
- Ask what dates they know, then let them go.
- Do not ask them to delay.

### Section: Coverage
- What must continue? What can wait? What stops?
- Who covers what, and for what stated reason?
- What comes off each person's plate?
- Hand off interpreter, format and access needs. Leave out the reason for absence.
- Tell your manager the real cost.

### Section: Tell the team
- What's changing and who covers.
- "I keep individual arrangements private for everyone."

### Section: Before they return
- Confirm date and any restrictions with Human Resources.
- Check access, equipment and any accommodation.
- Write a short update.
- Decide openly what work goes back.
- Book a one-to-one for day one or two.

### Section: After the return
- Rate the work they did while there.
- Tie "availability" and "presence" to a work result, or remove them.
- Offer opportunities on the same criteria. Let them decide.
- Check their assignments at six months.

### Section: Always route to Human Resources
- Repeated absences.
- Leave running out, or a request for more.
- Not returning when expected.
- A safety worry about someone's return.
- Any performance action.

**Do next:** Ask Human Resources today for the current leave process for your team, and save it with this page.
