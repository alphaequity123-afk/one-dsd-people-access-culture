# sm-deaf-deafblind-hoh: Supervising With Deaf, DeafBlind and Hard of Hearing Colleagues

Subtitle: Practical habits for supervisors who work with Deaf, DeafBlind and hard of hearing staff, peers and managers: ask, route requests, run meetings people can take part in, and keep opportunity fair.
Duration: 65 minutes
Content type: practice
Status: reviewed
Last reviewed: October 31, 2026
Evidence date: October 2026

This course is general information as of October 2026, not legal advice. Ask your ADA coordinator or Human Resources about a real request.

Course objectives:
- Ask each colleague how they communicate, without guessing from a label, device or file.
- Move an employment accommodation request correctly under MMB Policy 1433, including standing arrangements for regular meetings.
- Run one-to-ones and meetings so that a colleague can follow and contribute, using interpreters and captions correctly.
- Plan with a DeafBlind colleague individually, keep support roles straight, and respect consent around touch.
- Keep assignments, ratings, informal information and safety decisions fair.

Applied next step: Choose the next meeting you run. Before it happens, ask every participant the same question: "What do you need to follow and take part in this meeting?" Then find out how your agency books and pays for interpreters and CART.

## Lesson 1: Start with the person

Summary: Deaf, DeafBlind, hard of hearing and late-deafened colleagues communicate in many ways. A label tells you little. A plain question tells you what you need, and you can ask it without asking about anyone's life or body.
Minutes: 12
Objectives:
- Distinguish Deaf, deaf, DeafBlind, hard of hearing and late-deafened as people commonly use them.
- Explain why ASL is not English on the hands, and what that means at work.
- Ask a useful question about communication.
- Name the questions you do not need to ask.
Takeaways:
- Ask how the person communicates in this setting, and use the term they use for themselves.
- ASL is a language of its own. Written English may be a second language.
- The answer may differ by setting, and it may change.

### Text
The National Association of the Deaf (NAD) draws a distinction that many people find useful. Lowercase deaf describes not hearing. Capital-D Deaf describes people who share a language, American Sign Language, and a culture passed down across generations. Many Deaf people think of themselves first as members of a language community. The NAD also notes that most people in the community reject the term "hearing impaired." Deaf, DeafBlind and hard of hearing are widely accepted. Its core advice is simple: when you are unsure, ask the person how they identify.

ASL is a complete language with its own grammar, distinct from English, according to the National Institute on Deafness and Other Communication Disorders. It is not universal. This has a practical consequence for a supervisor. For a colleague whose first language is ASL, English may be a second language. A written memo may be fine for a schedule change and not fine for a complicated performance conversation. That says nothing about the colleague's intelligence or ability. You would not say it about someone who works in their second language in any other setting.

The range is wide. DHS Deaf, DeafBlind and Hard of Hearing State Services says one in five Minnesotans has a hearing loss. Many hard of hearing people use hearing aids, cochlear implants, captions, assistive listening or speech reading, and many do not use ASL. People who became deaf as adults may depend on real-time captions and written communication. Some deaf people do not sign at all. DeafBlind colleagues communicate in many ways, covered in Lesson 4.

### Statement
A file that says "deaf" does not tell you how to run the meeting. The person does.

### List
Questions that help:
- How do you prefer to communicate with me day to day?
- What works best for one-to-ones? For team meetings? For calls?
- What makes it harder? Lighting, background noise, where people sit, more than one person talking?
- Is there anything you would like me to do differently?
- Would you like me to set this up the same way each time, or check with you?

### List
Questions you do not need:
- How much can you hear?
- Were you born that way? What happened?
- Can't you just read lips?
- Is your whole family deaf?
- Can you teach the team some signs? (They may offer. It is not part of their job unless it is.)

### Tabs
Tab: ASL user
Body: May work through a qualified interpreter, a signing colleague fluent enough for the conversation, or video. Written English may or may not be a good substitute for a given task. Ask.

Tab: Hard of hearing
Body: May use devices, captions or assistive listening, and may not use ASL. Clear speech in a quiet room can work well, and the same person may miss most of a noisy group meeting. A good one-to-one does not prove the staff meeting works.

Tab: Late-deafened
Body: May rely on real-time captions and writing, and may not know ASL. The change may be recent. Do not book an interpreter on the strength of the word "deaf."

Tab: DeafBlind
Body: Combined hearing and vision loss, with a very wide range of methods. Ask, and plan individually. See Lesson 4.

### Text
One more point. The person you work with may not report to you. They may be your peer, your manager or your director. Everything here applies in all directions. If your supervisor is Deaf and uses an interpreter, the interpreter is there for both of you, because you do not sign. That interpreter is not a special favor to your supervisor. It is how the two of you do your work. Nobody on your team is the access problem. The gap sits between two people, and the agency's job is to bridge it.

And do not ask anyone to speak for all Deaf people. One person's preference is that person's preference. For wider questions, DHS State Services offers consultation and referral, and the Minnesota Commission of the Deaf, DeafBlind and Hard of Hearing and the Minnesota Association of Deaf Citizens are other places to learn. Your colleague is not your training department.

### Leader move
Control: You control whether you ask, and whether you write down the answer so that the person does not have to repeat it.
Failure: You act on a file, an assumption or what worked for someone else, and the person has to correct you in front of others.
Next: Ask every direct report the same communication question at your next one-to-one, so that no one is singled out.

### Scenario
Context: A new case consultant joins your unit next Monday. Human Resources mentions that the person is "deaf." You want the first day to go well. You are about to book an ASL interpreter for orientation and send an email to the team asking them to face the new hire when they talk.
Prompt: What do you do next?
Option A: Book the interpreter and send the team email. Being prepared shows you care.
Response A: You may book the wrong service and you would be telling the team about a colleague's disability before that person has any say. Under EEOC guidance, medical information is confidential, and telling coworkers can amount to disclosure.
Option B: Contact the new hire before Monday, say you ask all new staff what they need for orientation, ask how they would like to communicate, and set it up with the right office.
Response B: This gets the right answer from the right person, gives them a say in what others are told, and treats the question as ordinary.
Option C: Wait until Monday and see what they need when they arrive.
Response C: The person arrives to an orientation that may not work, and starts their job having to fix it in public. A short question ahead of time avoids that.
Recommended: B

### Knowledge check
Question: Which statement is accurate?
Option A: ASL is a form of English, so a written memo is always an equal substitute.
Option B: All deaf people use ASL, so an interpreter is always the right booking.
Option C: ASL is its own language, and some deaf and hard of hearing people do not use it, so you ask each person what works.
Correct answer: C
Feedback correct: Right. The label does not tell you the method. Ask, and record the answer.
Feedback incorrect: ASL has its own grammar, so English may be a second language for some colleagues. Others do not use ASL at all. Neither assumption is safe, so ask.

### Transfer prompt
If I learn that someone I will work with is Deaf, DeafBlind or hard of hearing, then I will ask them how they want to communicate in the specific setting coming up, and I will not tell others until they say what they want shared.

## Lesson 2: Requests, standing arrangements and who pays

Summary: Inside employment, the accommodation process applies, and it differs from what many staff learned about serving the public. Know your first step, the rule for regular meetings, and how the request stays private.
Minutes: 13
Objectives:
- Explain how employment accommodation differs from effective communication with the public.
- Recognize a request and move it under Policy 1433.
- Set up standing access for regular meetings.
- Find out how your agency pays.
Takeaways:
- For your employee, it is Title I and Policy 1433. For the public, it is Title II.
- A regular need gets a standing arrangement, not a new request each week.
- A budget line in your unit is not a reason to say no.

### Tabs
Tab: Employment (your staff)
Body: ADA Title I, the Human Rights Act and MMB Policy 1433. The employee's preference is always seriously considered. The agency is not obligated to provide the preferred choice so long as what it offers is effective, or it determines the accommodation would be an undue hardship.

Tab: Public service (people DSD serves)
Body: ADA Title II effective communication. The agency gives primary consideration to what the person asks for, under different rules. Other courses in this app cover this, including the course on Deaf Minnesota.

Tab: Why it matters
Body: Do not apply one set of rules to the other. In both, a decision that an option will not do goes through the right office, and it is not made by you alone.

### Text
MMB's Policy 1433 (last revised July 27, 2017 on its face) gives you a clear process. A request is any communication in which someone asks or states they need the agency to provide or change something because of a medical condition. No special words are needed. "I can't follow the staff meeting" may be enough. Some Deaf colleagues do not think of being Deaf as a medical condition at all, and that does not change the access they are owed or the fact that this is the route. Take the request on its terms. You do not need to debate how someone describes themselves.

The request can come to you, Human Resources, the ADA coordinator or the Affirmative Action Officer or designee. Oral requests must be documented in writing. As the first step, you promptly forward the request to the appropriate decision maker and tell the employee who that is. Your agency sets what supervisors may approve. The ADA coordinator must be consulted before any denial. If an approved accommodation cannot be provided within a reasonable time, the decision maker tells the requester the status before the end of 30 days and, where feasible, puts temporary measures in place. You must not request medical information. Policy 1433 says that where a disability and need are already known or obvious, the agency will not seek further documentation. Questions about that go to the ADA coordinator.

Here is the part many supervisors miss. Policy 1433 uses sign language interpreters as its own example. If someone needs the same accommodation repeatedly, a written request is required only the first time. They give appropriate advance notice for later occasions. And if it is needed on a regular basis, such as a weekly staff meeting, the agency must make arrangements without requiring a request each time.

### Statement
A weekly staff meeting should have standing access. No one should have to ask every week.

### Accordion
Item: Who pays?
Body: Policy 1433 says each agency must specify how it will pay for reasonable accommodations. It does not set a single method. Find out now how DHS handles interpreter and CART costs, before you need it. Do not tell an employee your unit lacks the budget. Before denying a request for lack of financial resources, the policy requires the agency to consult the State ADA Coordinator at MMB. That decision is not yours.

Item: Who decides among options?
Body: Consult the employee. The NAD advises employers to ask deaf and hard of hearing employees what they need, and notes that what works for one person or activity may not work for another. If the agency offers something other than what the employee asked for, it needs to be effective, and any denial must be explained in plain language in writing.

Item: Can a coworker who signs help?
Body: For a quick, casual exchange, if the employee wants that, it may be fine. It is not a substitute for a qualified interpreter in a meeting that matters. Knowing some sign is not the same as interpreting. And it takes that coworker away from their job. See Lesson 3.

Item: Can the employee bring someone to the discussion?
Body: Yes. Policy 1433 says an individual may ask that the ADA coordinator, a union representative or a support person be present. The MAPE 2025-2027 contract, Article 31, also provides for union representation in accommodation at the employee's request. Check your employee's own contract if they are in another unit.

Item: Who may know?
Body: The fact that someone has an accommodation is confidential under Policy 1433. Others may learn what they need to carry it out. An interpreter in a meeting is visible, of course. That does not make the reason for it a topic. Let your colleague decide what they say about themselves.

### List
Set up standing access by finding out:
- Who books interpreters and CART in your agency, and how far ahead.
- What the agency's payment route is.
- Whether the employee has preferred interpreters, and how that can be honored.
- What happens when a booked interpreter cancels.
- Who covers when you are out, so that the arrangement does not depend on you.
- How to add unplanned meetings, and how the employee can reach someone directly when it fails.

### Leader move
Control: You control how fast you forward the request and whether access becomes routine.
Failure: The employee has to request an interpreter every week, and any missed week becomes their problem to raise.
Next: List your unit's recurring meetings. For each one, ask whether access is standing or whether it depends on someone remembering.

### Scenario
Context: A hard of hearing policy specialist emails you: "I've been missing a lot in the Thursday meeting. The automatic captions get names and program terms wrong and I can't keep up with the cross-talk. Is there something better?" You have a small budget and the meeting has run this way for a year.
Prompt: What do you do next?
Option A: Ask whether they have had their hearing checked lately, or whether new hearing aids would help.
Response A: This is a medical question that Policy 1433 says you must not ask, and it puts the fix on the employee's body rather than on the meeting.
Option B: Tell them to read the minutes afterward, since the automatic captions are free and already available.
Response B: Minutes do not let someone ask questions or take part as the discussion happens. The EEOC makes that point about notes and summaries, and it is your employee who is saying the captions are not working.
Option C: Treat the email as a request, thank them, forward it promptly to the decision maker, tell them who that is, ask what would help, and start fixing the cross-talk in the next meeting.
Response C: This moves the request properly and makes an immediate change you control. Better meeting discipline helps everyone, and it does not replace whatever access the process decides.
Recommended: C

### Knowledge check
Question: Your employee who uses an ASL interpreter attends a weekly staff meeting. What does Policy 1433 say?
Option A: The agency must make appropriate arrangements without requiring a request before each occasion.
Option B: The employee must file a new written request every week.
Option C: The employee should bring their own interpreter.
Correct answer: A
Feedback correct: Right. A written request is required only the first time for a repeated need. Regular needs get regular arrangements.
Feedback incorrect: Policy 1433 uses this very example. A regular need does not require a new request each time, and arranging access is the agency's responsibility.

### Transfer prompt
If a colleague tells me they cannot follow a meeting or a conversation, then I will treat it as a possible request, forward it the same day, tell them who decides, and fix what I can in my own meetings before the next one.

## Lesson 3: One-to-ones, meetings and captions that work

Summary: The conversation is yours to run. This lesson covers how to work with an interpreter, tell CART from automatic captions, plan a meeting, and decide what to do when access falls through before an important conversation.
Minutes: 14
Objectives:
- Work directly with your colleague through an interpreter.
- Tell the difference between CART and automatic captions.
- Run a meeting so that people can follow and contribute.
- Decide what to do when access is missing before a high-stakes conversation.
Takeaways:
- Talk to your colleague, not to the interpreter.
- Automatic captions are not CART. Notes afterward are not participation.
- Do not hold a review or discipline meeting without access that works.

### Text
Effective communication at work covers more than people assume. The NAD names meetings, training, job evaluations, communication about work, discipline and benefits, and regular work-related communication. The EEOC's technical assistance on hearing disabilities (January 24, 2023; no force of law) lists interpreters in person or remote, CART and captioning. It also says note-taking and meeting summaries may not be enough, because they "do not allow the deaf employee to ask questions and participate in discussions."

Those are not abstract points. In a lawsuit announced June 25, 2021, the EEOC alleged that a Hawaii employer refused interpreters for deaf employees at staff meetings about safety, protocols and assignments, gave out written notes and handouts instead, and asked one deaf employee to interpret for the others. These were allegations, and the announcement does not say how the case ended. It is still a useful picture of what a supervisor should not let happen by default.

Not every exchange needs an interpreter. The NAD notes that occasional, as-needed services may be appropriate. A quick question about a file may work well in chat. Base it on what the conversation is and what your colleague says works, not on what is easiest to arrange. The harder and more consequential the exchange, the more it matters.

### Tabs
Tab: Working with an interpreter
Body: Look at and speak to your colleague. Say "What do you think?" and not "Ask them what they think." Everything said is interpreted, so do not say anything you would not want interpreted. Allow time, because interpretation takes a moment and your colleague needs a chance to respond. Do not ask the interpreter for opinions or to take part. The interpreter does not decide or advise, and DHS training materials describe the role as impartial and confidential. Share the agenda, names, acronyms and materials ahead of time. Terms like waiver names and program acronyms need preparing.

Tab: CART and captions
Body: CART is real-time text from a trained human captioner. Automatic captions come from software. DHS's guidance on virtual meetings says to understand the difference. New York State's Deaf, DeafBlind and Hard of Hearing Office goes further: when CART is requested, auto captions should never be used instead. Your colleague may be fine with automatic captions in a short, low-stakes call. That is their call to make for that setting. The captioner needs clean audio and advance materials too.

Tab: Video and remote
Body: Remote interpreting can work well or badly. A poor connection, a small screen or a shared-screen layout that hides the interpreter can all break access. Test it, and ask. New York State's guidance says the interpreter and the captions should both be visible at the same time, and Minnesota State Services for the Blind suggests giving interpreters co-host rights so they can be pinned and use spotlight.

Tab: Team interpreting
Body: Long or complex meetings may need two interpreters. Ask the person booking. Do not treat that as excess. It protects quality.

### List
Run the meeting so that it works. Most of this comes from DHS's accessible virtual meeting guidance and helps everyone.
- Name one person to handle access requests, and put their contact on the invitation.
- Contact providers about two weeks ahead, where you can. That is guidance, not a reason to refuse a late request. Ask the booking contact what can be done.
- State your name before you speak.
- One person at a time. Pause between speakers.
- Leave real time before you ask for objections, because interpretation lags behind speech.
- Do not rely on chat during the discussion, since it can compete with interpreters, captions and screen readers. Read out what matters.
- Describe what you share on screen. Avoid "this one here."
- Do not hold side conversations in the room or talk over the interpreter's voice.
- Check how it went afterward, by asking, and not only by whether anyone complained.
- Make recordings and transcripts accessible, and remember they do not replace taking part.

### Statement
A meeting a person can only read about afterward is a meeting they did not attend.

### Accordion
Item: What if the interpreter does not show up?
Body: Ask your colleague what they want to do. Depending on what the meeting is for, you may go on with a workable substitute, shorten it or move it. Do not simply carry on and promise to catch them up. Report the failure to whoever books the service.

Item: What about performance reviews and discipline?
Body: Plan access first. Discipline meetings may have added rules, including union representation. Consult Human Resources and labor relations, and read the contract that covers the employee. If you need a conversation and access has fallen through, reschedule when you can. A rushed conversation with poor access will not be useful, and it may not hold up.

Item: What about unplanned conversations?
Body: Agree on a default ahead of time, such as chat, a shared document or an on-demand remote interpreter if your agency has one. Work this out at a calm moment, and do not wait for the first crisis.

Item: What about an interpreter I do not think is working well?
Body: Ask your colleague privately. Interpreter fit matters. Tell the booking contact if there is a problem. Your colleague's view counts first, because you may have no way to judge the interpreting.

### Leader move
Control: You control how you run the conversation, whether you plan ahead, and whether you move a meeting when access has fallen through.
Failure: You hold the important talk anyway because it is on the calendar, and your colleague nods through a conversation they could not fully follow.
Next: Before your next meeting with an interpreter or captioner, send the agenda, names and acronyms ahead of time.

### Scenario
Context: You have a performance conversation scheduled with a Deaf program consultant at 2 p.m. At noon, the interpreter cancels and the booking office cannot replace them until next week. A colleague down the hall who took ASL classes offers to help. Your manager wants the review done this week. Nothing about it is urgent for safety or pay.
Prompt: What do you do next?
Option A: Accept the colleague's offer so that the review stays on schedule.
Response A: Knowing some sign is not qualified interpreting, and you would be exposing a private performance conversation to a coworker. The NAD points to EEOC decisions on qualified interpreters as opposed to "signers" for important communications, in the federal sector.
Option B: Hold the meeting by typing back and forth, without checking with your colleague, since you can both read.
Response B: Your colleague may or may not find this workable for a detailed and consequential talk. It is their communication, so ask first.
Option C: Tell your colleague what happened, ask what they would prefer, offer to reschedule for when a qualified interpreter is available, and tell your manager the reason for the delay without sharing private details.
Response C: This gives your colleague a real say and protects the conversation. A one-week delay is a small cost against a review that did not really take place.
Recommended: C

### Knowledge check
Question: What is the difference between CART and automatic captions?
Option A: There is none. Both are captions.
Option B: CART is real-time text from a trained human captioner. Automatic captions are produced by software, and they should not be swapped in when CART has been requested.
Option C: CART is only for prerecorded video.
Correct answer: B
Feedback correct: Right. They differ in how they are made and in how they handle names, terms and cross-talk. Your colleague may accept automatic captions for some settings, and that is their call.
Feedback incorrect: CART uses a trained human captioner in real time. Automatic speech recognition is a different method. Do not assume one can stand in for the other.

### Transfer prompt
If access falls through before a conversation that affects someone's job, then I will tell them right away, ask what they prefer, and move the meeting if that is what it takes.

## Lesson 4: Working with DeafBlind colleagues

Summary: DeafBlind colleagues use many methods, and a label does not tell you which one. This lesson covers how to plan with the person, keep support roles clear, and respect consent. It does not teach tactile communication or guiding.
Minutes: 13
Objectives:
- Explain why you cannot pick a method from the word DeafBlind.
- Distinguish a sign language interpreter from a Support Service Provider or CoNavigator.
- Adjust how a meeting runs when a member is DeafBlind.
- Respect consent and skill limits around touch.
Takeaways:
- Ask, and plan early. Plans here take more lead time, and that is normal.
- An SSP is not an interpreter. An interpreter is not an SSP.
- Do not touch someone or improvise a technique because you saw it once.

### Text
This lesson is deliberately limited. It does not train you in tactile communication, Protactile or guiding. Those take real learning from qualified people, and this course cannot substitute for them. It also contains no simulation of any kind. It gives you what a supervisor needs for the next decision: how to plan, whom to ask, and what not to assume.

DeafBlind means combined hearing and vision loss, and it does not mean a person has no hearing or no vision. According to DHS State Services, experiences and methods vary with the person, the setting and the task. A guide from New Jersey's Division of the Deaf and Hard of Hearing (2026) describes several. In close vision, an interpreter signs in a compact space within the person's preferred visual field, taking light and glare into account. In tracking, the person uses remaining vision and lightly holds the interpreter's wrists or hands. In tactile sign language, the person's hands rest on the interpreter's hands. Haptics are touch signals that convey environmental and social information. Others use braille, large print, or amplification with magnification.

Protactile deserves its own mention. Helen Keller National Center describes it as a touch-based language developed by DeafBlind people, which emerged in Seattle's DeafBlind community through the work of aj granda and Jelica Nuccio. For people who use it, touch is the main channel, and it does not rely on vision or hearing. It is not ASL with hands placed on top. If a colleague uses it, they are not asking you to learn it overnight. They are telling you what kind of interpreter and planning you need.

### Tabs
Tab: Sign language interpreter
Body: Provides language access, usually in formal communication, and works accurately and impartially. Some have specific experience working with DeafBlind people, and it is best practice to ask for that, according to the New Jersey guide. The guide notes that tactile interpreting is physically and cognitively demanding, so interpreters typically work in teams and rotate about every 20 minutes. That is one state's guidance. Ask your booking contact and your colleague what applies here.

Tab: Support Service Provider (SSP) or CoNavigator
Body: A trained person who gives environmental and visual information, human guide services, and help with brief casual communication, so that the DeafBlind person can make their own decisions. The New Jersey guide says SSPs do not interpret in formal or complex situations. DHS training materials also describe an SSP as supporting independence. Helen Keller National Center materials report that the terms SSP and CoNavigator are used interchangeably. The research for this course could not confirm how SSP services apply in a state workplace in Minnesota. Ask your ADA coordinator and DHS State Services.

Tab: Deaf interpreter
Body: A Deaf interpreter, including a Certified Deaf Interpreter, may team with a hearing interpreter and may help in DeafBlind settings. The Minnesota Commission of the Deaf, DeafBlind and Hard of Hearing says Minnesota has a small number, so plan early.

Tab: Why it matters
Body: Asking an SSP to interpret your budget meeting, or expecting an interpreter to also guide someone through the building, may not work and may not be appropriate. Keep the roles clear with your colleague and the people providing services. Both may be needed at once.

### List
Adjustments for a meeting with a DeafBlind member. Most come from guidance by Minnesota State Services for the Blind for its own council, seen only as a search summary, so check them with your colleague.
- Assign a communication facilitator who keeps everyone to the agreed practices.
- Have people raise a hand and wait to be called on, giving the interpreter time to find the speaker.
- Speakers state their names. One at a time, with pauses.
- Send documents to your colleague and interpreters ahead of time, in the format your colleague asked for.
- Do not say "this" or "that" about a document. Give the page and the line.
- Do not rely on a show of hands. For a vote, plan how each vote is named and conveyed. It takes longer. Allow for it.
- Leave enough time. Check how the room, the seating and the lighting work.
- Build in breaks that suit the interpreters and your colleague.
- For virtual and hybrid meetings, order services in advance and make the interpreter a co-host.

### Accordion
Item: What about touch?
Body: Do not touch a colleague, move their hands or take their arm unless you have agreed on it with them. A kind intention does not give you consent or skill. At the same time, touch is a normal part of communication for many DeafBlind people, and some colleagues may ask you to be open to it. If someone explains how they would like you to get their attention, do it that way. People on your team who are uncomfortable can also say so, and the answer is to plan other arrangements, such as interpreters, and not to treat your colleague as the problem. Ask your colleague or DHS State Services.

Item: What about getting around the building and emergencies?
Body: Ask your colleague what they want. Policy 1433 allows first aid and safety staff to be informed, when appropriate, if someone may need help in an emergency evacuation. The EEOC lists accessible alerts such as strobes and vibrating pagers. Work out the plan with your colleague, and do it before you need it. See Lesson 5.

Item: What about reading materials?
Body: Ask in what format, such as braille or large print, and how far in advance. Policy 1433 names both as possible accommodations. Remember that a document can be accessible for a screen reader and still not be usable for a person who reads it in braille unless it is sent in time. Ask.

Item: What if my unit has never done this?
Body: That is common. DHS State Services offers advice and referrals, and its regional offices provide DeafBlind services. Your ADA coordinator can help with the process. Do not ask your colleague to figure it all out for you. Do ask what has worked for them elsewhere, if they wish to share.

### Statement
You do not need to know the method. You need to ask, plan early and keep the roles straight.

### Leader move
Control: You control the time you allow, how the meeting runs and whether you plan with your colleague.
Failure: You treat one technique you saw somewhere as the answer, or you assume whoever accompanies your colleague can cover everything.
Next: If you chair a workgroup, ask now how the format would work for a DeafBlind member, before you have one. Find out who at your agency arranges this.

### Scenario
Context: You chair a cross-division workgroup on waiver rule changes. A DeafBlind colleague from another unit joins next month. A planner suggests that the SSP who comes with your colleague can interpret the discussion, and that the group can vote by a show of hands as usual.
Prompt: What do you do next?
Option A: Accept the plan. The SSP knows your colleague best and it saves an interpreter booking.
Response A: SSPs and interpreters are distinct roles. A detailed policy discussion calls for interpreting, and the SSP may not be able or expected to do it. A show of hands also leaves out your colleague.
Option B: Contact your colleague now, ask what communication and materials they need and what has worked, involve your ADA coordinator or access contact to arrange it, assign a communication facilitator, and change how votes are taken.
Response B: This plans with the person, keeps roles clear, gives the lead time this needs, and changes the group's process instead of expecting your colleague to adapt to it.
Option C: Ask your colleague to demonstrate their method for the workgroup at the first meeting so that everyone understands.
Response C: Access does not depend on a demonstration, and your colleague is there to work on waiver rules. They may offer information, but you should not require this.
Recommended: B

### Knowledge check
Question: Which statement about DeafBlind colleagues is accurate?
Option A: DeafBlind means no hearing and no vision, and everyone uses tactile sign.
Option B: Any person who accompanies a DeafBlind colleague can interpret formal meetings.
Option C: Methods vary widely, and SSPs and interpreters do different work, so plan with the person and keep the roles clear.
Correct answer: C
Feedback correct: Right. Ask what the person uses in this setting, plan early, and make sure the right role is covering the right need.
Feedback incorrect: DeafBlind covers a wide range of hearing and vision, and many methods. SSPs generally provide environmental information, guiding and casual communication, and they do not interpret in formal or complex situations.

### Transfer prompt
If a DeafBlind colleague is joining something I run, then I will contact them well ahead, ask what they need, get my ADA coordinator or access contact involved, and change how the group works.

## Lesson 5: Keeping opportunity fair, not only access

Summary: A person can have an interpreter and still be left out of what matters. Watch informal information, stretch work, ratings and assumptions about safety or phones. These are everyday supervisor decisions.
Minutes: 13
Objectives:
- Spot where information moves outside formal meetings.
- Offer assignments on the same criteria without assuming limits.
- Keep ratings on the work, not communication style.
- Handle safety questions without myths, and respond to mockery correctly.
Takeaways:
- Access to the meeting does not mean access to the work.
- Do not decide for someone what they cannot do.
- Being Deaf does not make someone your Deaf-services expert.

### Text
Getting the interpreter booked can feel like the finish line. It is the start. Plenty of work information moves outside meetings: the hallway update, the chat after a call, the remark on the way to lunch. Practical employer guidance from DeafTEC and others points out that informal information is easy for deaf staff to miss, and that relationships and advancement can depend on it. This evidence is practical guidance and first-person accounts, not controlled study. It also matches what Lesson 2 of the course on why this is supervisor work says about face time: time with a manager can shape who gets promoted.

So make a habit of checking what happened outside the room. If a decision came out of a hallway talk, put it in writing where everyone can find it, or do not decide in hallways. If the team's planning happens at lunch, move the planning. Include your colleague in social events by asking what would make them work, and do not assume they would rather not come.

### Tabs
Tab: Assignments
Body: Offer stretch work on the same criteria as everyone else. Do not rule someone out because the work includes phone calls, a legislative hearing or a community meeting. Ask what they would need and route it. Relay services, interpreters and captions exist, and whether a function is essential is a question for the ADA coordinator. Under Muldrow, narrowing someone's duties or visibility because of a protected trait can be challenged, even with pay unchanged.

Tab: Deaf-related work
Body: A Deaf consultant may have real expertise in Deaf community work and may want it. They may not. Do not assign all Deaf clients or every Deaf-related project to one person by default, and do not make them the unit's accessibility help desk. Ask about interests like anyone else's, and make sure that extra work counts. EEOC's position that client preference is no defense concerns race and color, but the same habit is sound here: assign on the job and the person's interests, not their identity.

Tab: Ratings
Body: Rate the work. Watch for words like "not a team player," "doesn't speak up" or "seems disengaged" when the meeting format may be the cause. Did they have access to what you are rating? Did they have the information in time? Do not hold it against someone that interpreted communication takes more time, or that they asked for access. A person who requests an accommodation is protected from retaliation.

Tab: Coworkers who sign
Body: A signing coworker can be a good thing for a team. Do not make them the unofficial interpreter. It pulls them from their own work, it is not their job, and it may put private matters in front of a peer. A bilingual Deaf staff member should not be asked to interpret for other Deaf staff, which was among the EEOC's allegations in the 2021 case.

### Accordion
Item: Safety
Body: The EEOC warns employers not to act on myths, fears or stereotypes about hearing conditions. Excluding someone for safety reasons requires an individualized assessment under the direct threat standard. Policy 1433 says that this assessment is about the person's present ability and cannot rest on fears, misconceptions or stereotypes, and it goes through the ADA coordinator. Do not decide on your own that a Deaf colleague cannot do home visits or drive to a county meeting.

Item: Alarms and emergencies
Body: The EEOC lists options such as strobe lights on fire alarms and vibrating pagers. Check your building and your remote setup with your colleague, and talk to the ADA coordinator and your safety lead. Do this for every site they work in, and do it ahead of time.

Item: Hiring
Body: Before a job offer, an employer generally cannot ask about medical conditions, according to EEOC guidance. It can ask about the ability to do the job with or without accommodation. Policy 1433 requires notice about accommodation on job announcements and accepts requests at any point in the process. If a Deaf candidate needs an interpreter for an interview, that request goes through the same process promptly. Score them on the same criteria. Watch for "communication skills" as a criterion when you mean spoken English and the job does not require that. Talk to Human Resources about it.

Item: Mockery or exclusion
Body: A coworker mimics signing, jokes about a colleague's speech, or keeps a colleague out of conversations on purpose. Disability is a protected class. Under Policy 1436, you immediately report to Human Resources or the Affirmative Action Officer. A word in private is not a substitute. Set the expectation for how your team behaves, and do it in your own conduct first.

Item: If you are Deaf, DeafBlind or hard of hearing yourself
Body: The same process and contacts apply to you. If you supervise hearing staff, access needs to work in both directions, and interpreted communication is part of how your team works. If someone on your team treats it as an inconvenience or goes around you, that is a performance and conduct matter for you and your manager to handle. It is not something you should have to absorb.

### Statement
If you would have offered it to them before you knew, offer it now. Then figure out access.

### Leader move
Control: You control what you offer, what you put in writing, and what you write down in a review.
Failure: You protect a colleague from a challenge they never asked to avoid, and their career narrows while you feel considerate.
Next: Look at the last three stretch assignments on your team. Who was considered? Was anyone ruled out by an assumption about communication?

### Scenario
Context: A hard of hearing case consultant has strong results and has told you they want to move up. A statewide assignment opens that includes monthly calls with county staff and a yearly legislative hearing. A senior colleague suggests offering it to someone else: "It's all phones and hearings. It would be unfair to set them up to struggle."
Prompt: What do you do next?
Option A: Offer the assignment to someone else and explain to the consultant that you did not want to overload them.
Response A: You have decided for them, based on an assumption rather than on the work. That can narrow their career, and it may be a discriminatory decision however kind it feels.
Option B: Offer it only to the consultant, so that the team can see you are inclusive.
Response B: Picking them because of their disability has the same flaw in another direction. Use the same criteria for everyone who could do the work.
Option C: Open it to the team on written criteria. If the consultant is selected or wants to be considered, ask what they would need for the calls and hearings, and take access questions to the ADA coordinator.
Response C: This treats them as a candidate on the merits and treats access as a problem to solve, not a reason to rule someone out.
Option D: Ask the consultant first to prove that they can handle phone calls by doing a test call.
Response D: Other candidates are not being asked to do this. A test built around an assumption about their disability is not a fair criterion. Ask the ADA coordinator if there is a real question about a job function.
Recommended: C

### Knowledge check
Question: A team member suggests that a Deaf colleague should not do community visits for safety reasons. What should you do?
Option A: Agree. Being careful is part of your job.
Option B: Ask what the actual concern is. Do not act on assumption, and take any genuine question to the ADA coordinator for an individualized assessment.
Option C: Require the colleague to bring a doctor's note saying they are safe.
Correct answer: B
Feedback correct: Right. Under EEOC guidance and Policy 1433, a safety exclusion has to rest on an individualized assessment, not on myths or fears, and the ADA coordinator is involved.
Feedback incorrect: Assumptions about safety do not meet the standard, and supervisors must not ask for medical information. An individualized review through the ADA coordinator is the route if there is a real question.

### Transfer prompt
If I catch myself thinking that some piece of work would be too hard for a colleague because of how they communicate, then I will offer it on the same criteria as everyone else, ask what they would need, and take any real question to the ADA coordinator.

## Job aid

Purpose: A one-page reminder for working with Deaf, DeafBlind and hard of hearing staff, peers and managers.

Remember:
- General information as of October 2026. Local steps come from your ADA coordinator and Human Resources.
- Draft, written without Deaf, DeafBlind or hard of hearing reviewers. Your colleague's own account comes first.
- No one speaks for a whole community. Ask the person in front of you.

Sections:

Ask
- "How do you prefer to communicate in this setting? What makes it harder?"
- Use the term they use for themselves.
- Skip questions about how much they hear or why.
- Record the answer so that they do not have to repeat it.

Route (Policy 1433)
- No special words are needed. Document oral requests.
- Forward promptly. Tell them who decides.
- Do not ask for medical information.
- Regular meetings get a standing arrangement, with no new request each time.
- The ADA coordinator is consulted before any denial. Budget is not your call to make.
- Keep the accommodation confidential.

Run the meeting
- Talk to your colleague, not to the interpreter.
- Send agenda, names and acronyms ahead of time. Book about two weeks out where you can.
- State your name. One at a time. Pause.
- CART and automatic captions are different. Do not swap them without asking.
- Notes afterward are not participation.
- Do not hold a review or discipline talk without working access.

DeafBlind colleagues
- Ask, and plan early. Methods vary.
- SSP and interpreter are different roles.
- Assign a communication facilitator. Change how votes work.
- No touch or technique without agreement and skill.

Keep it fair
- Put hallway decisions in writing.
- Same criteria for stretch work. Do not decide for them.
- Do not make them the unit's Deaf expert, and do not make signing coworkers the interpreter.
- Safety questions get an individualized review, not an assumption.
- Mockery goes to Human Resources or the Affirmative Action Officer immediately.

Do next
- Ask all your direct reports the same communication question.
- Find out who books and how the agency pays.
- Check your recurring meetings for standing access.
- Check alarms and emergency plans with your colleague and safety lead.
- Save your ADA coordinator's contact, and find DHS State Services' regional office for your area.
