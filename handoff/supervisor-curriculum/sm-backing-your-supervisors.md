# sm-backing-your-supervisors: Backing Your Supervisors: Conversation Guides

**Subtitle:** Short conversation guides a manager can use with a supervisor before each everyday decision, without assigning anything, checking anything or keeping score.
**Duration:** 80 minutes across 6 lessons
**Content type:** practice
**Status:** reviewed
**Last reviewed:** October 31, 2026
**Evidence date:** October 2026

**Course objectives:**
- Use a short conversation to support a supervisor just before a real decision.
- Ask about the decision and the process, never about whether someone took a course.
- Tell which matters are coaching and which go straight to Human Resources, the ADA coordinator or labor relations.
- Remove obstacles only you can remove, such as a missing tool, unclear authority or too much work.
- Follow up on the work in a way that keeps the supervisor's learning private.

**Applied next step:** Look at your calendar for the next two weeks. Find one decision a supervisor who reports to you will face. Pick the matching guide in this course and use it at your next regular meeting.

## Lesson 1: Your part, and the first week

**Summary:** What a manager adds, the rules that keep these conversations from becoming monitoring, and the guide for a supervisor's first week.
**Minutes:** 13

**Objectives:**
- Explain why a manager's follow-up matters more than a course.
- Apply the four rules for using these guides.
- Use the first-week guide with a new or acting supervisor.

**Takeaways:**
- Talk about the next decision, not the course.
- Nothing from these conversations goes into a review or a tracking sheet.
- In the first week, a new supervisor needs names, numbers and permission to ask.

### Text: Why this falls to you

Training tends to change what people know more than what they do. The research on equity training, and on training in general, keeps landing there. A review of behavior modeling training found that skills carried over to the job best when learners set specific goals and when their own superiors were trained too. The evaluators of a large New York City police training found little evidence that disparities fell, and they named immediate supervisors as perhaps the most important support for any training effect. A field experiment at a retail chain found that turnover fell when a chief executive simply asked store managers to work on it. None of these studies tested the guides in this course, and none shows that a conversation guide reduces disparities. They do point the same way: what the next person up asks about tends to get attention.

So your job here is small and specific. Before a supervisor makes a decision, you ask a few good questions. You make sure they know whom to call. You fix what only you can fix. Then you come back later and ask how it went.

### Statement

These guides are about a decision the supervisor is about to make. They are not a check on whether the supervisor has learned anything.

### List: Four rules for using these guides

- Do not ask whether anyone took a course. This app is voluntary from staff to commissioner. It has no assigned courses, no scores and no team view, and it sends nothing to supervisors or managers. Ask about the work instead.
- Keep it out of reviews. A conversation guide is support. Do not write down how a supervisor answered and do not use it in an appraisal.
- Do not type real details into this app. No names, no case facts, no active complaints. Those belong in Human Resources systems and in conversations with the right office.
- Stop coaching when a duty applies. Some topics in these guides, such as a report of harassment, are not coaching topics. The guide tells you when to stop asking and start routing.

### Statement

This course is general information as of October 2026, not legal advice. Union contracts, Human Resources procedures and state policies control the details. Only the MAPE 2025-2027 contract was read for this course, so check the AFSCME, MMA or Managerial Plan terms that apply to your people.

### Accordion: Conversation guide: the first week

#### Item: When to use it

On the supervisor's first or second day, including an acting supervisor. Fifteen minutes.

#### Item: Open with

"You're now answering for some things you didn't answer for last week. I'd like to make sure you know who to call before you need them."

#### Item: Ask

- "Do you know who your Human Resources contact, your ADA coordinator and your labor relations contact are?"
- "Which contract or plan covers each person on your team?"
- "If someone tells you tomorrow about harassment, what would you do that same day?"
- "What are you most worried about getting wrong this month?"

#### Item: Listen for

A plan to handle hard reports privately or to look into them alone. Uncertainty about who they can call. Friends on the team who now report to them.

#### Item: Offer

Introductions, not just a list of names. Clear limits on what they can decide alone. Time to complete Supervisor Core, which Minnesota Management and Budget describes as required within the probationary period.

#### Item: Avoid

"You'll figure it out." Also avoid suggesting this app covers the state's supervisor training. It does not. Training under Minn. Stat. 43A.21 is met through the state's own programs.

#### Item: Come back

One week later: "What came up that you didn't expect? Who did you end up calling?"

### Leader move: Remove one obstacle

**Control:** You control whether a new supervisor has working contacts, a copy of the contract and an hour a week to learn the job.
**Failure:** The usual failure is a handoff that covers projects and deadlines and leaves out duties. A new supervisor then learns about the reporting duty only after they have missed it.
**Next:** Introduce the new supervisor to their Human Resources contact this week, in person or by a short message from you.

### Scenario

**Context:** You manage four supervisors. One of them was promoted from within the team and started Monday. Your director asks you to confirm that every new supervisor has finished the app's supervisor courses within 30 days, and suggests you ask each person for a screenshot.
**Prompt:** What do you do next?

**Option:** Ask the new supervisor for the screenshot, since it comes from your director.
**Response:** This turns a voluntary tool into a monitored one. It also breaks what the app promises: no assigned courses and nothing reported to supervisors. A screenshot would not tell you whether the person can handle a real report anyway.

**Option (recommended):** Tell your director the app is voluntary and keeps no such records. Offer what you can confirm instead: the new supervisor knows their contacts, has their contract and is scheduled for Supervisor Core through the state's process.
**Response:** This answers the real concern, which is readiness. It keeps the promise, and it points to the state's own system for required training.

**Option:** Recommend the courses informally and say nothing to your director.
**Response:** Recommending something is fine. Leaving the director's request unanswered means the same idea will return, possibly aimed at someone else. Say plainly what the app does and does not do.

### Knowledge check

**Question:** A manager wants to follow up after a supervisor used this curriculum. Which question fits these guides?
**Options:**
- "Which of the courses have you finished?"
- "What score did you get on the accommodation lesson?"
- "You have a schedule change coming up. How will you decide who moves, and what will you write down?"
- "Can you send me your if-then plan?"

**Correct answer:** "You have a schedule change coming up. How will you decide who moves, and what will you write down?"
**Feedback if correct:** Right. It is about a real decision and it leaves the supervisor's learning private.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Look for the question about upcoming work. The others ask about the course, about a score that does not exist or about private notes.

### Transfer prompt

**Prompt:** Think of the next person who will step into a supervisor or acting role under you. Finish this plan for yourself: "If someone starts supervising under me, then by their second day I will introduce them to ____ and ask them ____."

## Lesson 2: Duties that cannot wait: accommodation and reports of harm

**Summary:** Guides for the two conversations where delay itself does damage, and how to tell when you should stop coaching and route the matter.
**Minutes:** 14

**Objectives:**
- Use the accommodation guide to keep a request moving.
- Use the reports-of-harm guide without slowing the reporting duty.
- Protect people from retaliation after either kind of request.

**Takeaways:**
- A supervisor sitting on an accommodation request can be treated as having denied it.
- Under state policy, a supervisor reports harassment and discrimination concerns immediately. Your conversation comes with or after the report, not before it.
- Your best question is usually "Who have you called, and what happens next?"

### Text: What the rules say, as of October 2026

Accommodation first. EEOC guidance from 2002, still in place and stating that it does not have the force of law, says a request needs no special words, can be spoken and can come through someone else. It says unnecessary delay can violate the ADA and that a supervisor who sits on a request can be treated as having effectively denied it. Minnesota's Human Rights Act defines accommodation through an informal interactive process, and failure to engage in that process may be an unfair practice. Medical information goes in separate confidential files, although supervisors and managers may be told about necessary restrictions and accommodations. Under the MAPE contract, Article 31, an employee can ask for union representation in the accommodation process.

Now reports of harm. State policies 1436 and 1329 say managers and supervisors must treat reports seriously, respond when they become aware of a problem and "immediately report" to Human Resources or the Affirmative Action Officer. People who knowingly allow or tolerate the conduct can be disciplined, up to discharge. Neither policy waits for the conduct to reach a legal threshold. So the bar for reporting is awareness, not proof.

### Accordion: Conversation guide: an accommodation request

#### Item: When to use it

As soon as you hear a supervisor has a request or a possible request. Ten minutes, the same day if you can.

#### Item: Open with

"Tell me what the person asked for, without the medical details. I don't need those, and you may not either."

#### Item: Ask

- "What did they say they need in order to do the work?"
- "Have you contacted the ADA coordinator or Human Resources yet? What's the next step, and when?"
- "When will you next update the employee?"
- "Is anything in your unit, such as a seating rule, a schedule or a piece of software, getting in the way?"

#### Item: Listen for

"I told them to bring a doctor's note." "Let's see how it goes for a month." "It's not really a disability." Or a supervisor who thinks they must decide alone.

#### Item: Offer

Your authority over the thing in the way, such as budget, schedule rules or a call to another unit. Keep the supervisor focused on a practical question: what would help you do the job?

#### Item: Avoid

Asking for a diagnosis. Telling the team why someone has a new arrangement. Treating the request as a performance topic.

#### Item: Come back

In a few days: "Where is it now? Has the employee heard from anyone?" Later, in general terms: "Did anything change in how you assign or review work since then?"

### Accordion: Conversation guide: a report of harm or discrimination

#### Item: When to use it

The moment a supervisor tells you someone has raised a concern, or that they saw something. If it has not been reported yet, the first step is the report.

#### Item: Open with

"Have you reported it to Human Resources or the Affirmative Action Officer? If not, let's do that now, and then talk."

#### Item: Ask

- "What did you tell the person would happen next? Did you promise to keep it secret?"
- "Are any decisions coming up that affect the person who raised it, or the people named? A schedule, an assignment, a review?"
- "How are you doing? This is hard."

#### Item: Listen for

"They asked me not to tell anyone." "I talked to the coworker, and it's handled." "It wasn't that serious." Any of these means the duty may be slipping. A request for secrecy does not remove it.

#### Item: Offer

To make the call together. To check that upcoming decisions are tied to work reasons before they go ahead. If you are also a supervisor or manager and now know about the concern, the duty applies to you too.

#### Item: Avoid

Looking into it yourself. Asking who else knows. Guessing at motives. Treating the person who raised it differently, or letting the team do so.

#### Item: Come back

Over the following weeks, ask about the work, not the case: "Is everyone getting normal assignments and normal time with you?" Retaliation often looks ordinary. It can be a worse shift, closer scrutiny or being left out.

### Leader move: Stop coaching when the duty starts

**Control:** You control whether a supervisor hears "call now" or "let me think about it" from you.
**Failure:** A kind manager talks through a report for days, helping the supervisor weigh it. Meanwhile the immediate-report duty has been missed by both of them.
**Next:** Practice saying this aloud: "This goes to Human Resources today. I'll stay with you while you do it."

### Scenario

**Context:** A supervisor tells you, in a regular meeting, that a staff member said a senior colleague keeps making comments about their religion. The staff member asked the supervisor to keep it quiet. The supervisor wants your advice on how to speak with the senior colleague. It happened last week.
**Prompt:** What do you do next?

**Option:** Coach the supervisor on how to give the senior colleague direct, respectful feedback.
**Response:** Feedback skills matter, but not here, and not first. State policy calls for an immediate report. An informal talk could also alert the colleague and put the staff member at risk.

**Option:** Respect the staff member's wish for now and ask the supervisor to watch for more comments.
**Response:** Waiting for more does not fit the duty. Both of you now know about the concern. A request for secrecy is a reason to explain the process carefully, not a reason to hold the report.

**Option (recommended):** Tell the supervisor this goes to Human Resources or the Affirmative Action Officer today, and offer to stay while they make the report. Then ask what the supervisor told the staff member, and what decisions are coming up that affect either person.
**Response:** This meets the duty for both of you, supports the supervisor and looks ahead to the retaliation risk. It also gives the supervisor a way to go back and be honest with the staff member about what must happen.

**Option:** Report it yourself and tell the supervisor to stay out of it from here.
**Response:** Reporting is right. Shutting the supervisor out is not. They still have to supervise everyone involved and tell the staff member what happens next, and they need to know how to do that without retaliation.

### Knowledge check

**Question:** A supervisor says, "I told them to get a doctor's note before I look at this." What is the most useful next thing for a manager to say?
**Options:**
- "Good. Get the documentation first, and then decide."
- "Have you contacted the ADA coordinator or Human Resources? What did the person say they need to do the work?"
- "What's the diagnosis? I may have seen this before."
- "Give it a month and see whether the problem continues."

**Correct answer:** "Have you contacted the ADA coordinator or Human Resources? What did the person say they need to do the work?"
**Feedback if correct:** Right. It moves the request to the people who run the process and brings the supervisor back to the practical question. Documentation, when needed, is limited and goes through that process.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Look for the answer that keeps the request moving. A demand for a note, a question about diagnosis or a month's wait can all work as delay, and delay can count as denial.

### Transfer prompt

**Prompt:** Think of the supervisor under you who is most likely to try to handle things quietly. Finish this plan: "If that supervisor brings me a concern about harassment or discrimination, then my first sentence will be ____, and before the meeting ends I will ask ____."

## Lesson 3: Everyday fairness: assignments, one-to-ones and feedback, meetings and access

**Summary:** Guides for the small, repeated decisions that shape a person's work and visibility.
**Minutes:** 14

**Objectives:**
- Help a supervisor state criteria before making an assignment.
- Help a supervisor make feedback specific and consistent.
- Help a supervisor plan communication access before a meeting, not after a problem.

**Takeaways:**
- Ask "How will you decide?" before you ask "Who will you pick?"
- Good feedback names what happened and why it matters to the work.
- Access is planned ahead and is the planner's job, not the job of the person who needs it.

### Text: Why small decisions deserve your attention

As of October 2026, two Supreme Court decisions make routine choices matter more. *Muldrow* (2024) says an employee challenging a transfer must show only "some harm" to a term or condition of work. It need not be significant. *Ames* (2025) says all employees meet the same standard, whichever group they belong to. Together they mean assignments, schedules, stretch work and nominations deserve clear criteria and a short record, whoever is affected. There is also a practical reason. One study using quasi-random manager rotation at a financial firm found that more face-to-face time with a manager raised promotion rates. Who gets your supervisors' time and attention is not a small thing.

### Accordion: Conversation guide: assignments, schedules and visibility

#### Item: When to use it

Before a significant assignment, a schedule change, a move in duties, or the choice of who briefs leadership.

#### Item: Open with

"Before you decide who, tell me how you'll decide."

#### Item: Ask

- "What does this work need? What would make someone a fit?"
- "Who else could do it if we looked past the first names that came to mind?"
- "Who's carrying the routine work that doesn't get seen, such as notes, onboarding and coverage?"
- "Would you use the same reasoning if the person affected were someone else on the team?"
- "What will you write down?"

#### Item: Listen for

"They always step up." "They wouldn't want it." "It's just easier." Assumptions about what someone wants, without asking them. Note also any change affecting someone who recently raised a concern or asked for an accommodation.

#### Item: Offer

Backing when the fair choice is slower or less popular. Cover for the person who is moved to a stretch role.

#### Item: Avoid

Picking or rotating people because of who they are, in either direction. Evening out a count without a reason tied to the work.

#### Item: Come back

Once a quarter: "Look back at your last few big assignments. Anything you'd do differently?"

### Accordion: Conversation guide: one-to-ones and feedback

#### Item: When to use it

When a supervisor is setting up regular one-to-ones, is preparing hard feedback or has been cancelling meetings.

#### Item: Open with

"What do you want this person to do differently, and what did you actually see?"

#### Item: Ask

- "What happened, and what did it mean for the work?"
- "Would you say this, in these words, to anyone on your team who did the same thing?"
- "Do all your people get a regular one-to-one? Who has been cancelled most?"
- "When did you last ask this person what they need from you?"

#### Item: Listen for

Words about style with no work attached, such as "presence", "attitude", "too quiet" or "not a fit". Saying nothing at all to one person because feedback feels awkward. Being vague is not a kindness. It keeps people from knowing where they stand.

#### Item: Offer

To rehearse the hard sentence together. To model it: ask the supervisor how you could give them better feedback, and act on what you hear.

#### Item: Avoid

Rewriting the supervisor's feedback for them. Asking them to report what staff said in private one-to-ones.

#### Item: Come back

After the conversation: "How did it go? What did they say they'd need?"

### Accordion: Conversation guide: meetings and communication access

#### Item: When to use it

Before a new standing meeting, an all-team meeting, a community or provider session, or a team change that adds someone who uses interpreters, CART or other access supports.

#### Item: Open with

"How will everyone get what they need from this meeting and be able to contribute to it?"

#### Item: Ask

- "Do materials go out ahead of time in a form everyone can use?"
- "Who books interpreters or CART, and how far ahead? Do you know how that gets paid for?"
- "Have you asked the person what works for them, rather than guessing?"
- "How can someone contribute if they don't speak first, or at all?"
- "Is there anything about the room or the platform that gets in the way?"

#### Item: Listen for

"We'll sort it out if someone asks." A colleague being used as the interpreter or the note taker because they are available. The same people always speaking first.

#### Item: Offer

Budget and a clear answer on who pays. Your support if meetings run longer or need to move. Ask the ADA coordinator if you do not know the answer.

#### Item: Avoid

Asking a Deaf, DeafBlind or hard of hearing colleague to solve the logistics or to teach the team. Assuming one method works for everyone. Treating access as a favor.

#### Item: Come back

After the meeting: "Did the person have what they needed? Did you ask them?"

### Leader move: Ask for the method, not the name

**Control:** You control the first question. If you ask "Who are you thinking of?", you get a name and a defense of it. If you ask "How will you decide?", you get a method.
**Failure:** Managers often weigh in on the person: "Oh, give it to the one who did such a nice job last time." That sends the supervisor back to habit, with your approval.
**Next:** In your next meeting with a supervisor, hold back any names until they have told you the criteria.

### Scenario

**Context:** A supervisor of case consultants wants to move a consultant off a set of complex waiver cases and give the cases to someone else. The reason given is "they seem overwhelmed". You also recall the consultant recently asked for a change in start time for medical appointments.
**Prompt:** What do you ask or do next?

**Option:** Agree. The supervisor knows the team, and lighter work sounds supportive.
**Response:** It might be a kind thought. But under *Muldrow*, a change in duties can matter even without a change in pay or rank. A change that comes soon after an accommodation request also deserves a careful look before it goes ahead.

**Option (recommended):** Ask what the supervisor has actually seen in the work, whether they have asked the consultant, and how the timing relates to the recent request. Suggest they talk with Human Resources or the ADA coordinator before moving any cases.
**Response:** This tests the reason against evidence and brings the consultant's own view into it. It also catches a possible retaliation problem before it happens, without you deciding that anyone did anything wrong.

**Option:** Tell the supervisor they cannot move the cases, because it could look like retaliation.
**Response:** A flat no may be wrong, too. If the work is truly unmanageable and the consultant agrees, a change could help. Ask for the facts and bring in the right office, rather than ruling by reflex.

### Knowledge check

**Question:** Which question best helps a supervisor make an assignment fairly?
**Options:**
- "Who's your strongest person?"
- "What does the work need, and how will you decide among people who could do it?"
- "How many people from each group have had stretch work this year?"
- "Who hasn't had a turn?"

**Correct answer:** "What does the work need, and how will you decide among people who could do it?"
**Feedback if correct:** Right. Criteria come first and apply to everyone.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Look for the question that sets criteria before names. Picking by group is not a fair method, and neither "strongest" nor "whose turn" tells the supervisor what the work needs.

### Transfer prompt

**Prompt:** Choose one of these three guides for a decision coming up in the next month. Finish this plan: "If ____ comes up with my supervisor, then I will ask ____ before I offer any names or opinions."

## Lesson 4: Bringing people in and rating them: hiring, onboarding, reviews

**Summary:** Guides for the moments where criteria, early support and evidence decide what happens to someone's career.
**Minutes:** 14

**Objectives:**
- Help a hiring supervisor set criteria before seeing candidates.
- Help a supervisor plan an onboarding that gives every new person the same start.
- Help a supervisor base a rating on evidence and see what follows from it.

**Takeaways:**
- Criteria go on paper before anyone reads a resume.
- A good start should not depend on who a new hire happens to know.
- A fair rating can still lead to an unfair result at the next step. Look at what follows.

### Text: What we know and what is moving

Structured interviews have strong support. A 2022 review ranked them at the top of selection procedures, although that paper has drawn criticism. Older research found smaller group differences in highly structured interviews. Formal tools are not automatically good, though. Panel research on US firms suggests some rules limiting discretion can provoke resistance. Research on pay found that gaps in salary growth persisted among employees with equal performance ratings. So the handoff from rating to consequence needs a look of its own.

Hiring also sits in a live legal dispute. As of October 2026, *United States v. Minnesota* challenges the state's affirmative action system. A docket summary lists DHS's Hiring Justification Policy as an exhibit and reports that no relief has been granted. Minn. Stat. 43A.191 says an agency missing hiring goals must justify its nonaffirmative action hires. Ask Human Resources for the current instructions on that step. Do not guess, and do not rely on an older memo. *Ames* applies to hiring and promotion decisions for every candidate. Teach and practice this: decide on the job's criteria, and do not weigh a person's race or sex in an individual selection.

### Statement

This course cannot tell you whether your area has structured interview guides or clear review criteria. Find out. If a tool does not exist, coaching a supervisor to use it is theater. Getting the tool built is your work.

### Accordion: Conversation guide: hiring

#### Item: When to use it

Before the posting goes out, and again before interviews.

#### Item: Open with

"What does this person need to be able to do in the first year? Let's get that on paper before anyone sees a name."

#### Item: Ask

- "What are the criteria, and how will each be scored or weighed?"
- "Does every candidate get the same questions? Who's on the panel, and have they used this guide?"
- "Who needs to know about the interview access process, and how will candidates ask for an accommodation?"
- "Where will this posting be seen? Who are we missing, and who can we reach openly?"
- "Which steps need Human Resources, and have you confirmed the current hiring justification instructions with them?"

#### Item: Listen for

"I already know who'd be great." "We need the right fit." Criteria changing after someone has been seen. Comfort with a person standing in for evidence of skill.

#### Item: Offer

Time to do this right. Your own support when the best-scored candidate is not the expected one.

#### Item: Avoid

Suggesting a candidate. Discussing a person's background, accent or family outside the criteria. Saying a candidate's identity would help the team, or that it would be a problem for clients. The EEOC's position is that client or customer preference is no defense to race or color discrimination.

#### Item: Come back

After the selection: "Would you stand behind the record if someone asked how you decided? What would you change in the guide next time?"

### Accordion: Conversation guide: onboarding

#### Item: When to use it

Two weeks before a new hire starts.

#### Item: Open with

"Walk me through this person's first two weeks. What will they have on day one, and who'll be their person?"

#### Item: Ask

- "Have you asked them, before they start, what they need to work well, and told them how to request an accommodation?"
- "Are equipment, accounts and software ready, and do they work with any assistive technology the person uses?"
- "Who's the peer or mentor? Do they have time for it, and is that work counted?"
- "When will you meet in the first week, and what will you ask?"
- "Would every new hire get the same start, or does it depend on who they know?"

#### Item: Listen for

"They'll pick it up." One person always being the informal onboarder. Different starts for people who already know the team.

#### Item: Offer

To remove some work from whoever does the onboarding. To meet the new person yourself, briefly, in the first month.

#### Item: Avoid

Assuming what someone needs because of a visible trait. Asking about health or personal background to be friendly.

#### Item: Come back

At 30 days: "What does the new person say has been unclear? What did you change?"

### Accordion: Conversation guide: reviews and what ratings lead to

#### Item: When to use it

Before review drafts are final, and before ratings feed into anything else.

#### Item: Open with

"Pick your hardest review. What's the evidence, from across the whole year?"

#### Item: Ask

- "What were the expectations, and did everyone know them at the start?"
- "Are these comments about the work or about style?"
- "Did something from an accommodation, a leave or a report end up in here? Should it be there?"
- "If two people have the same rating, do they get the same treatment for what follows, such as assignments, recommendations and development?"
- "Which part of the contract covers the appraisal for this person?"

#### Item: Listen for

Only recent events. "Not leadership material" with no example. Lots of detail for some people and two lines for others.

#### Item: Offer

To read a draft against the criteria, without changing the rating yourself unless you are the one responsible for it. See Consistency Across Ratings, Discipline and Pay if you want to do this across supervisors.

#### Item: Avoid

Asking a supervisor to change an individual's rating to make a group's numbers look better. Using anything from this app in a review. It holds nothing about anyone.

#### Item: Come back

A month after reviews: "What did the ratings lead to? Anything surprise you?"

### Leader move: Protect the criteria when the pressure comes

**Control:** You control whether criteria hold when someone senior or someone popular has a favorite.
**Failure:** A manager tells a supervisor to run a fair process, then mentions over coffee who they would like to see hired. The supervisor hears the second message.
**Next:** Say early, and to the whole panel, "I have no candidate in mind. Follow the criteria and tell me if the criteria turn out to be wrong."

### Scenario

**Context:** A supervisor is hiring a policy specialist. After reading resumes, the panel wants to add a new must-have criterion: experience with one specific legislative process. It happens to match one internal candidate that the panel knows well. Interviews have not started.
**Prompt:** What do you do next?

**Option:** Allow it, since it is a real skill and interviews have not started yet.
**Response:** It may be a real skill, but it surfaced only after the panel saw the names. Adding it now looks like, and may be, fitting the job to the person. The record would be hard to defend.

**Option:** Tell the panel to ignore it and never discuss it again.
**Response:** If the skill matters for the job, ignoring it does not help anyone. The question is whether it is a true job need and how to handle it properly.

**Option (recommended):** Ask what in the job requires this skill and whether it could be learned quickly. Ask Human Resources whether criteria can be changed at this stage, and how. Make any change openly and apply it to everyone, or leave it for later development.
**Response:** This separates the job need from the known person. It uses the Human Resources process, and it leaves a record that would make sense to a candidate who asked.

### Knowledge check

**Question:** Two employees get the same rating. One gets a raise recommendation and a high-profile project. The other gets neither. What does research on pay suggest a manager should do?
**Options:**
- Nothing, because equal ratings prove the process was fair.
- Look at the step after the rating, including who decided what and why.
- Lower one rating so that the difference makes sense.
- Ask for each employee's group membership and adjust the result to match.

**Correct answer:** Look at the step after the rating, including who decided what and why.
**Feedback if correct:** Right. Research at one large firm found gaps in salary growth among employees with equal ratings. The step after the rating needs its own review.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Look again at where the difference arose. Changing a rating or adjusting by group misses the process, and treating a person differently because of group membership creates a new problem.

### Transfer prompt

**Prompt:** Find the next hire, new-staff start or review deadline among your supervisors. Finish this plan: "If ____ is coming up by ____, then two weeks before it I will sit down with the supervisor and ask ____."

## Lesson 5: Decisions with consequences: corrective action, equity analysis, advancement and retention

**Summary:** Guides for discipline, approvals and the conversations that decide who stays and moves up.
**Minutes:** 14

**Objectives:**
- Help a supervisor prepare for corrective action with facts, consistency and the right people.
- Review an equity analysis as part of an approval, not as a form to sign.
- Help a supervisor offer growth and retention conversations to everyone.

**Takeaways:**
- Call labor relations before corrective action, not after.
- An equity analysis should be able to change the decision. If it cannot, it is paperwork.
- Stay and growth conversations belong to everyone on the team.

### Text: The facts you need, as of October 2026

Corrective action is governed by contract and policy, and each contract is different. In the MAPE 2025-2027 contract, Article 8, section 8, keeps oral reprimands and investigations without discipline out of the personnel file. Check the agreement that applies to your people. Do not carry MAPE terms over to AFSCME, MMA or Managerial Plan employees. Under Minn. Stat. 13.43, the existence and status of a complaint and the final disposition of discipline are public, with reasons, while other personnel data is generally private. Ask Human Resources how this applies before you say anything.

The DHS Equity Policy, in the undated copy reviewed, requires a formal equity analysis for long-term actions that go to a supervisor, manager, director or commissioner for approval. Confirm the current version with your division. If it applies, you may be the person reviewing the analysis. It is a real control point that no training has to invent.

On retention, a study at one high-tech firm using new hires and manager moves linked better people management to lower turnover. The EEOC treats mentoring, sponsorship and training access as terms or privileges of employment. So keep them open to everyone.

### Accordion: Conversation guide: corrective action

#### Item: When to use it

When a supervisor first says something like "I think I need to write this person up." The sooner the better.

#### Item: Open with

"What's the expectation, how did the person know about it, and what have you seen?"

#### Item: Ask

- "What have you already said to them, and when?"
- "What have you done when someone else did something similar?"
- "Have you called labor relations or Human Resources? Which contract covers this person?"
- "Could anything else be in play, such as an accommodation, leave or a recent complaint?"
- "If they ask for a union representative, do you know what to do?"

#### Item: Listen for

Facts that begin only after a conflict or a report. Words about attitude with no behavior attached. A much stricter response than the team has seen before.

#### Item: Offer

Help preparing. A reminder that they do not have to work this out alone or do it fast. Your comparison across supervisors, within what you are allowed to see.

#### Item: Avoid

Telling them what discipline to give before anyone has checked the contract. Discussing the case with others on the team. Letting speed outrun consistency.

#### Item: Come back

After it is finished: "What's your plan for working with this person next week? Do they know what success looks like now?"

### Accordion: Conversation guide: approving with an equity analysis

#### Item: When to use it

Before you sign a long-term action, or when a supervisor brings you one. Also earlier, when a supervisor is just starting one.

#### Item: Open with

"Who's affected by this, and what did you learn that you didn't already know?"

#### Item: Ask

- "Who did you hear from, and who didn't you reach? Did people have the access they needed in order to weigh in?"
- "What changed in the proposal because of the analysis?"
- "Where could a neutral rule shut people out, and what's the option that avoids it?"
- "What will we look at later to see if we were right?"
- "Is a question here beyond us? Who should we ask?"

#### Item: Listen for

"We filled it in at the end." "Nothing changed." "No impact anticipated" with no evidence behind it. Any suggestion of treating an individual differently because of a protected trait. A pattern is a reason to review the process. It is not a reason to decide about one person.

#### Item: Offer

Time. If you always need it by tomorrow, you are the reason it gets filled in at the end. Your willingness to send it back or change the plan.

#### Item: Avoid

Signing without reading. Treating disparate impact as either dead or enforced just as before. As of October 2026, HHS has removed its Title VI effects provisions, while Title VII's statutory provision and the Minnesota Human Rights Act's language remain. See Reading Patterns and Reviewing Process.

#### Item: Come back

At the review date you set: "What did we learn? What do we change?"

### Accordion: Conversation guide: advancement and retention

#### Item: When to use it

Twice a year, and whenever a vacancy, an acting role or a development opening appears.

#### Item: Open with

"Have you asked everyone on your team what they want next and what would make them stay?"

#### Item: Ask

- "Who've you assumed doesn't want to move up? Have you asked them?"
- "How do people hear about acting roles, training and stretch work?"
- "Who do you meet with most? Who hardly at all?"
- "Who's at risk of leaving, and what do you know about why?"
- "What's stuck that I could unstick?"

#### Item: Listen for

Sponsorship that goes only to people who remind the supervisor of themselves. Assumptions tied to age, disability, family or language. Opportunity limited to people who speak up.

#### Item: Offer

Your introductions, open to all who qualify. Help with classification or budget questions. Asking Human Resources about the rules on acting roles.

#### Item: Avoid

Mentoring or development limited to particular groups. The EEOC says access limits can be unlawful. Matching people to mentors by race or sex. Asking people to promise they will stay.

#### Item: Come back

In six months: "What came of those conversations? Who took a step? Who left, and what did you learn?"

### Leader move: Be the person who can say no to yourself

**Control:** You control your own deadlines, and whether an approval can actually change the decision.
**Failure:** A manager asks for an equity analysis on Thursday for a Friday decision that has already been made. Everyone learns that the form is theater.
**Next:** On your next approval, ask for the analysis before the plan is final, and ask what it changed.

### Scenario

**Context:** A supervisor brings you a two-year change to how provider inquiries are handled, moving them to an online form only. The equity analysis says "no impact anticipated". You have to approve or return it by next week. The change would save real staff time, and your division needs that time.
**Prompt:** What do you do next?

**Option:** Sign it. It saves time and there is no sign of harm.
**Response:** "No impact anticipated" is a claim, not a finding. A form-only channel can shut out people who use phone, interpreters, relay services or other languages. If you sign this, the supervisor learns the step is empty.

**Option (recommended):** Ask who was consulted, how people who cannot easily use the form would reach the division, and what would be checked after launch. Ask for an alternative channel and a review date, and adjust your own deadline if needed.
**Response:** This tests the analysis, keeps the time savings possible and makes the check real. It also shows the supervisor that you mean what the step says.

**Option:** Reject the change outright, because it could harm people.
**Response:** You do not know that yet. The staff time matters too. A good review asks for evidence and options rather than choosing a side at the start.

**Option:** Tell the supervisor to rewrite the analysis so it sounds more thorough.
**Response:** Better wording does not fix the problem and teaches the wrong lesson. The goal is to change the decision if the facts call for it.

### Knowledge check

**Question:** Which statement about corrective action fits this course?
**Options:**
- All state contracts handle oral reprimands the same way as MAPE.
- Call labor relations or Human Resources early, and check the agreement that covers the employee.
- Discipline is the supervisor's private decision, and calling others weakens authority.
- Act fast, because speed is the main test of fairness.

**Correct answer:** Call labor relations or Human Resources early, and check the agreement that covers the employee.
**Feedback if correct:** Right. Contracts differ, and only the MAPE contract was read for this course.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Look for the answer that brings in the right people and the right contract. Speed matters less than getting the facts and the process right.

### Transfer prompt

**Prompt:** Think of the next approval on your desk, or the next time a supervisor says "I need to write someone up". Finish this plan: "If that lands with me, then before I respond with an opinion I will ask ____, and I will not decide until ____."

## Lesson 6: Their load, your load, and following up

**Summary:** The guide for a supervisor's own load, and how to follow up across all these guides without turning into a monitor.
**Minutes:** 11

**Objectives:**
- Use the own-load guide with a supervisor who is stretched.
- Avoid assigning equity work by identity.
- Build a light follow-up rhythm with no tracking of people's learning.

**Takeaways:**
- Exhausted supervisors take shortcuts on the decisions in this curriculum.
- Do not hand equity work to someone because of who they are, and count this work when it is done.
- Come back to decisions in ordinary meetings.

### Text: Why load is part of fairness

Fair decisions take time. Asking for criteria, checking the contract, calling the ADA coordinator and rehearsing feedback all cost minutes that supervisors may not have. When they are short on time, they use the first name that comes to mind. There is little direct research on this. Evidence on supervisors' own well-being and on supervisors of color carrying extra load is thin and comes mostly from higher education, where the idea is called cultural taxation, meaning extra and often unrewarded work expected of underrepresented staff. One randomized study of supervisors at garment factories in India found that treated supervisors were less likely to quit after soft-skills training, so supervisors' retention can move, too. Treat all of this as practical judgment, not a proven method.

### Accordion: Conversation guide: their own load

#### Item: When to use it

Every few months, and when you see signs such as late nights, cancelled one-to-ones or decisions getting rushed.

#### Item: Open with

"What's on your plate that isn't in anyone's picture of your job?"

#### Item: Ask

- "What did you stop doing this month because there wasn't time? One-to-ones? Feedback?"
- "Which part of this job is hardest to carry right now? You can share as much or as little as you want."
- "Are you being asked for equity work or advice because of who you are, on top of your job? Do you want it? Is it counted?"
- "Who do you talk to when a case or a staff situation stays with you?"
- "What can I take off, delay or say no to?"

#### Item: Listen for

Pride in being stretched. One person always being asked to review, translate, mentor or sit on committees. Signs of stress from the work itself, including from cases involving harm.

#### Item: Offer

To remove something specific. To say no on their behalf. To count equity and mentoring work in how you see their job, if they have chosen to do it. Information about support such as the employee assistance program, if your area offers one. Ask Human Resources.

#### Item: Avoid

Asking about a diagnosis or a personal situation. "We're all busy." Offering wellness advice in place of fewer tasks. Asking someone to represent a group.

#### Item: Come back

In a month: "Did the thing I took off actually stay off?"

### List: A follow-up rhythm that stays honest

- Keep one standing question for each regular meeting: "What decision is coming up that you'd like to think through?"
- Use one guide at a time. Thirteen guides are a shelf, not a checklist.
- Say what you learned too. Tell a supervisor when your own decision needed a second look.
- Do not create a spreadsheet of who has had which guide. It would become the tracking this app promises not to do, and the conversations would change.
- Staff can read these guides as well. They are open to everyone, so write and speak as though your team is listening.
- Look for the organization's job in what you hear. If several supervisors stall on the same step, the problem may be the process. Take that to your own manager or to Human Resources.

### Leader move: Take something off

**Control:** You control what your supervisors are asked to do. Very few people above them see the whole list.
**Failure:** Managers add fairness steps and remove nothing. Supervisors then experience fairness as extra work and drop it first when things get busy.
**Next:** Ask each supervisor to name one recurring task that could stop. Stop one this month.

### Scenario

**Context:** One of your supervisors is frequently asked to review documents for cultural fit, to mentor new staff and to sit on hiring panels because, a colleague says, "people trust them, and they bring a perspective". The supervisor has not complained. Their one-to-ones with staff have been slipping.
**Prompt:** What do you do next?

**Option:** Leave it. They haven't complained, and the work is valuable.
**Response:** Silence isn't consent, especially from someone who may feel unable to refuse. The slipping one-to-ones suggest there is a real cost, and their staff are paying part of it.

**Option:** Remove them from all of it, to protect them.
**Response:** This decides for them, and it could take away work they value or visibility that helps their career. Ask first.

**Option (recommended):** Ask privately what they have taken on, which parts they want to keep and what it costs. Make the rest someone else's job or stop it. Count what they keep. Ask others in the division to stop routing this work to one person because of who that person is.
**Response:** This leaves the choice with the supervisor, makes the work visible and counted, and fixes the routing. The evidence for this comes mostly from higher education, so ask and adapt.

**Option:** Ask them to tell the management team how this feels for people in their group.
**Response:** Nobody speaks for a group, and this adds another task of the same kind. The problem belongs to the management team, not to them.

### Knowledge check

**Question:** You want to know whether these guides are helping. Which step fits the course?
**Options:**
- Keep a spreadsheet showing which supervisors have had each guide.
- Ask staff to rate each supervisor's use of the guides.
- Ask, in regular meetings, about upcoming decisions and what your supervisors found hard, and fix what you hear.
- Check the app for which supervisors have been reading.

**Correct answer:** Ask, in regular meetings, about upcoming decisions and what your supervisors found hard, and fix what you hear.
**Feedback if correct:** Right. It keeps follow-up on the work and leaves learning private.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Look for the option with no tracking. The app has no such view. A spreadsheet or a staff rating of one small team would also create new risks, including identifying people.

### Transfer prompt

**Prompt:** Choose the supervisor under you who seems most stretched. Finish this plan: "If I meet with them this month, then I will ask what they stopped doing, and I will take ____ off their plate by ____."

## Job aid

**Title:** Thirteen conversations before the decision
**Subtitle:** One opening question for each everyday decision

**Purpose:** Help a manager support a supervisor before a decision, using one good question and a clear route for anything that is a duty.

**Remember:**
- Ask about the decision. Never ask about a course.
- Stop coaching and route when a duty starts.
- Nothing goes into a review, a tracking sheet or this app.
- Remove one obstacle only you can remove.

### Duties: act the same day

- Accommodation: "Tell me what they asked for, without the medical details. Have you called the ADA coordinator or Human Resources?"
- Report of harm: "Have you reported it to Human Resources or the Affirmative Action Officer? If not, let's do that now."

### Before the decision: opening questions

- First week: "Do you know who to call before you need them?"
- Assignments: "Before you decide who, tell me how you'll decide."
- One-to-ones and feedback: "What did you actually see, and what did it mean for the work?"
- Meetings and access: "How will everyone get what they need and be able to contribute?"
- Hiring: "What must this person be able to do in the first year? Let's write it down before anyone sees a name."
- Onboarding: "Walk me through the first two weeks. Who's their person?"
- Reviews: "What's the evidence, from the whole year? What will this rating lead to?"
- Corrective action: "What's the expectation, what have you seen, and have you called labor relations?"
- Equity analysis: "Who's affected, who did you hear from, and what changed because of it?"
- Advancement and retention: "Have you asked everyone what they want next?"
- Own load: "What's on your plate that isn't in anyone's picture of your job?"

### Whom to ask

- Accommodation: the ADA coordinator or Human Resources.
- Harassment, discrimination, retaliation: Human Resources or the Affirmative Action Officer.
- Contracts, discipline, grievances: labor relations and the contract itself.
- Hiring justification, classification, pay: Human Resources.

**Do next:** Choose one decision coming up in the next two weeks. Use its question at your next regular meeting. Come back to it later.
