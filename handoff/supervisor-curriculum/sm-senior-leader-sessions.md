# sm-senior-leader-sessions: Senior Leader Decision Sessions

**Subtitle:** A guide for voluntary, self-organized sessions where senior leaders think through decisions that only they can make. Agendas and discussion prompts are ready to use.
**Duration:** 65 minutes of reading across 5 lessons, plus four optional sessions of about 75 minutes each
**Content type:** practice
**Status:** reviewed
**Last reviewed:** October 31, 2026
**Evidence date:** October 2026

**Course objectives:**
- Organize a voluntary session with no records, no attendance, no identity-based breakouts and no required disclosure.
- Think through who owns equity work, with what budget and toward what outcome.
- Choose measures you would report even if nothing moved.
- Plan how to lead when law and guidance shift.
- Consider how program integrity can be governed for consistency, appeals and language access.

**Applied next step:** Pick one of the four session topics. Invite two or three peers. Use the opening script and the agenda as written, or change them to suit you.

## Lesson 1: How these sessions work

**Summary:** Why the sessions focus on decisions, the ground rules that make them safe, and the duties those rules do not suspend.
**Minutes:** 13

**Objectives:**
- Explain why decisions are the focus and not awareness.
- Apply the ground rules.
- Keep decisions in their proper channels.

**Takeaways:**
- The session decides nothing for the organization. Each leader leaves with what they personally will do.
- No records, no attendance, no breakouts by identity, no required disclosure.
- Reporting duties and data law still apply.

### Text: Why decisions

There's no published equity track for senior leaders in the programs reviewed for this curriculum, so treat this format as a reasoned choice, not a proven model. But the evidence points this way. Panel research on US firms found that establishing responsibility was followed by the broadest gains, and made other programs work better. A randomized trial at a retail chain found that turnover fell when the chief executive asked store managers to work on it. A review of behavior modeling training found that skills carried over best when learners' superiors had been trained as well. And awareness-only training has tended to show smaller effects on behavior. So what you can add at this level is not more awareness. You name an owner, set aside money, choose what to look at and follow up.

None of this shows that sessions like these will reduce disparities in DHS services or the DHS workforce. They offer a place to think clearly about choices that are yours.

### List: Ground rules

- Voluntary. Anyone from staff to commissioner may join or not, and may leave at any time. Nobody asks later who came.
- No attendance. No sign-in, no list and no report that a session took place with certain people.
- No records of the discussion. No minutes, no recording, no transcripts, no AI notetakers and no summary of who said what. Flip charts come down at the end.
- No identity-based breakouts. Groups form by interest or at random. The EEOC's position is that separating employees by protected characteristic can be unlawful even with identical programming.
- No required disclosure. Nobody has to share their identity, their experience, their bias or their health. Nobody is asked to confess or affirm a belief.
- Disagreement carries no consequence. Say what you think. Your questions and objections stay yours.
- Nobody speaks for a group. If someone chooses to share their own experience, they speak only for themselves.
- No individuals. No named employees, providers, people served or open cases.

### Accordion: What those rules do not change

#### Item: The reporting duty

Under Policies 1436 and 1329, supervisors and managers must immediately report harassment or discrimination concerns to Human Resources or the Affirmative Action Officer. If someone describes such a thing, the fact that the session keeps no records doesn't remove the duty. Say so at the start, so people can choose what they share.

#### Item: Government data

What you write on state systems or in state notebooks may be government data. If you keep personal notes, keep them about your own next steps, with no names. Ask your data practices contact if you have questions.

#### Item: Legal holds

A docket summary reports a preservation order entered in September 2026 in *United States v. Minnesota*. A session with no records doesn't change any duty to keep existing documents. Ask your legal staff what applies to you.

#### Item: Real decisions

Decisions that belong in a governance process go through it, with its normal records. An unrecorded session is a place to think. It must not be a place where decisions are made out of sight. Each leader decides afterward, through normal channels, on their own authority.

#### Item: Tribal matters

Tribal learning is referred to ADSA and the Tribal liaison, and these sessions do not cover it. Minn. Stat. 10.65 requires Tribal-state relations training for commissioners, deputies, assistant commissioners and employees whose work likely includes Tribal implications. Ask the liaison about that training.

#### Item: Required training

These sessions aren't training credit, and they do not meet Minn. Stat. 43A.21. The state's own training does that.

### Text: Opening script, ready to use

"Thanks for coming. This is voluntary, and you can step out at any time. We're not taking attendance or notes, and nobody will be asked afterward what you said. Nobody has to share anything personal. We won't discuss named people or open cases. One limit: if someone describes harassment or discrimination, those of us who are managers and supervisors still have a duty to report it. So please take that into account. We aren't deciding anything for the organization today. Each of us will leave with what we'll personally do. Disagreement is welcome."

### List: Setting it up

- Choose the host by interest, not rank. Rotate the role. See Running an Optional Live Practice Session for how to host.
- Invite widely, and in the same way, to those who would have a role in the decision.
- Think about who reports to whom. If a person's boss is in the room, they may not feel free to speak or to skip it.
- Plan access first. Send materials ahead of time in usable formats. Arrange CART and interpreters by default. Ask your ADA coordinator how to arrange these.
- Ask your own manager or Human Resources if there's a question about using work time.

### Statement

This course is general information as of October 2026, not legal advice.

### Leader move: Do not make it mandatory by accident

**Control:** You control how you talk about these sessions. Your interest can turn an option into an expectation.
**Failure:** A commissioner mentions at a staff meeting that they hope all directors will attend. Now everyone does, and everyone wonders who is counting.
**Next:** When you speak of it, say, "It's optional. I won't ask who came." Then don't.

### Scenario

**Context:** You're a deputy-level leader, and you plan a session on ownership of equity work. Your chief of staff suggests keeping a sign-in sheet and short minutes, to show the organization that leadership takes this seriously and to create a record of commitment.
**Prompt:** What do you do next?

**Option:** Agree, because a record shows seriousness.
**Response:** A sign-in sheet and minutes break the rules that let people speak freely, and they turn a voluntary session into one that can be monitored. Show seriousness in another way.

**Option (recommended):** Keep the session free of records. Say that any commitments will be made by leaders afterward, through normal channels, where they can be seen and followed up. Share your own commitment that way.
**Response:** This keeps the session open, and it gives real accountability a proper home. The public record is the decision you make, not who sat in a room.

**Option:** Skip the sign-in sheet, and ask your chief of staff to quietly note who attends, so that you know who's engaged.
**Response:** That's worse. It's a hidden version of the record you promised not to keep, and it would destroy trust the moment anyone found out.

### Knowledge check

**Question:** During a session with no records, a participant describes ongoing harassment on a team. Several managers are present. What applies?
**Options:**
- Nothing, because the session is off the record.
- The managers still have a duty to report immediately to Human Resources or the Affirmative Action Officer under state policy.
- The host should write up minutes of the discussion for the group.
- Everyone should stay after and investigate together.

**Correct answer:** The managers still have a duty to report immediately to Human Resources or the Affirmative Action Officer under state policy.
**Feedback if correct:** Right. This is why the opening script says so up front. Reporting goes to those offices. It doesn't create minutes or an attendance record of the session.
**Feedback if incorrect:** The session's rules don't suspend policy duties. Look for the answer that keeps the reporting duty in place.

### Transfer prompt

**Prompt:** Choose whom you'd invite to a first session. Finish this plan: "If I host a session with ____, then I'll say out loud at the start ____, and I will not ____ afterward."

## Lesson 2: Session 1: An owner, a budget and an outcome

**Summary:** A session plan for deciding who really owns this work, what it gets and what it should change.
**Minutes:** 13

**Objectives:**
- Host a 75-minute session on ownership.
- Test whether an owner has real authority and resources.
- Name an outcome rather than an activity.

**Takeaways:**
- Responsibility has more support than most other program features, though it comes from panel data.
- An owner with no budget or authority isn't an owner.
- Name an outcome you could be wrong about.

### Text: Background to share

Panel research covering 708 private workplaces found that establishing responsibility was followed by the broadest gains in management diversity, with training and diversity evaluations among the least effective. A county auditor in Oregon concluded that its workforce equity plan needed more accountability to make progress. And the DHS Equity Policy already sets out some control points, such as an equity analysis for long-term actions going to a supervisor, manager, director or commissioner for approval. Confirm its current version. The research for this course could not tell who, if anyone, holds this kind of supervisor work in your area. You may know better.

Be careful about what the research shows. It's from private firms, it isn't randomized and firms that adopt programs aren't typical. Treat it as a reason to discuss ownership, not as a promise.

### List: Agenda, 75 minutes

- 5 minutes: Opening script.
- 10 minutes: Individually and silently, write down who you think owns this work, what their budget is, and how you'd know if it were working. Keep your notes.
- 20 minutes: Compare your answers in pairs or threes, formed at random. Where do your answers differ?
- 25 minutes: Whole group on the prompts below.
- 10 minutes: Individually, write down one thing you will do through your own channels.
- 5 minutes: Close. Take down anything on the walls. Nobody collects anything.

### Accordion: Discussion prompts, ready to use

#### Item: Owner

Who could lose sleep over this? Who can stop a process that's not working? If that person left, what would happen to the work? Is it an individual, or a committee nobody answers for?

#### Item: Budget

What's actually paid for? Interpreters and CART? Paid time for people who contribute their lived experience? Staff time to fix the hiring and review tools? What would we stop funding to pay for this?

#### Item: Outcome

Name a result in a person's life or work, not an activity. For example, accommodation requests get answered quickly, or families who use interpreters reach an assessment as fast as others. What might we be wrong about? When would we know?

#### Item: Authority

Which tools does the owner need that they don't control, such as structured interview guides, review criteria or assignment rules? If those tools don't exist, is any training meaningful?

#### Item: Who is carrying it now

Who has been doing this work without pay, title or credit? Have we been relying on people because of who they are? What would it take to count this work properly?

#### Item: What we would not do

Which option would look like progress without changing a decision? A new section of a website? More course completions?

### Leader move: Pay for it, or say it's not a priority

**Control:** You control money and staff time.
**Failure:** An owner gets named in a memo with no budget and no authority. Nothing changes, and people grow more cynical.
**Next:** After this session, tell your budget lead what you'd fund, or tell yourself honestly why you won't.

### Scenario

**Context:** After the session, you plan to name a mid-level manager as owner of supervisor practice work. They'd keep their full current job. They have no budget line and no authority over Human Resources processes. They happen to be one of the few senior people of color in the division, and a colleague says they're the natural choice.
**Prompt:** What do you do next?

**Option:** Name them. They'd be excellent, and people trust them.
**Response:** You'd be assigning work with no resources, in part because of who they are. It's the pattern this curriculum warns against, and it sets them up to carry the failure.

**Option:** Avoid naming anyone until the budget improves.
**Response:** Waiting for perfect conditions usually means nothing happens. You can make a smaller, honest commitment now.

**Option (recommended):** Define the job first: its authority, time and budget. Remove other duties to make room. Offer it openly on job criteria. If you can't resource it, shrink the scope and say so.
**Response:** This treats ownership as real work, opens it to everyone qualified and doesn't rest on anyone's identity. The person your colleague suggested can apply for it if they want to.

### Knowledge check

**Question:** Which is an outcome rather than an activity?
**Options:**
- Forty supervisors complete a course.
- A new page goes live.
- The time from an accommodation request to a response gets shorter.
- A task force meets monthly.

**Correct answer:** The time from an accommodation request to a response gets shorter.
**Feedback if correct:** Right. It's a change in what happens to people. You could also be wrong about it.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Look for the option that describes a change in someone's experience. Counting completions, pages or meetings counts activity.

### Transfer prompt

**Prompt:** Finish this plan: "If I decide this work needs an owner in my area, then by ____ I will define the authority and budget, and take ____ off that person's current job."

## Lesson 3: Session 2: Measures you would report even if nothing moved

**Summary:** A session plan for choosing measures ahead of time and being honest about what they can show.
**Minutes:** 13

**Objectives:**
- Host a 75-minute session on measures.
- Commit to a measure and a period before seeing results.
- Accept the limits of what a small population can prove.

**Takeaways:**
- Completion and satisfaction don't show change.
- Write the measure, the period and what would count as change before looking.
- DSD alone won't be able to prove effects on equity outcomes. Say so.

### Text: Background to share

Training evaluations often look good on measures that don't relate to later learning. Reactions reflect course features, and self-assessed knowledge tracks motivation and satisfaction. Larger programs have struggled to show effects. A trial across 14,741 New York City police officers found insufficient evidence that disparities fell. A workshop trial across 92 university departments couldn't reach conventional significance on hiring. So the realistic aim for a division is to look at whether decisions improve and whether staff report specific behaviors. Treat administrative data as monitoring.

The privacy limit is real, too. The floor is 10 respondents for any staff-facing result, higher for identity cross-tabs, which rules out individual supervisor scores on most teams. This app reports nothing on anyone, by design. Any measures belong in your organization's own systems, in aggregate.

### List: Agenda, 75 minutes

- 5 minutes: Opening script.
- 15 minutes: Individually, list what you've seen reported as progress over the past two years. Mark each as an activity, a decision or an outcome.
- 20 minutes: In random small groups, choose two measures that would matter to staff or to people served.
- 25 minutes: Whole group on the prompts below.
- 10 minutes: Individually, write what you will ask your own team to look at, and by when.

### Accordion: Discussion prompts, ready to use

#### Item: Pre-commitment

What will we look at, for what period, and what would count as change? Would you still report this if it hadn't moved? If you wouldn't, why choose it?

#### Item: Bad news

When did someone last bring you bad news about this work? What happened to them? What would it take for a null result to be seen as useful?

#### Item: Who sees it

Which measures can be shared with staff without identifying people? Who has access to the data, and does their work require it? What about those under the floor?

#### Item: What the numbers cannot say

What can't we prove with our size? How do we talk about that honestly without using it as an excuse?

#### Item: The temptation

Where might we be tempted to adjust individuals to fix a number? How will we respond when someone suggests it? A pattern prompts a process review. It's not a decision about a person.

#### Item: False reassurance

Lab experiments suggest that having diversity structures can make people see an organization as fair, even with evidence that it's not. How do we avoid saying that we're done?

### Leader move: Ask for the null

**Control:** You set what your staff believe they can tell you.
**Failure:** Reports always show progress. Then you can't tell which results are true, and your staff know you don't want to hear otherwise.
**Next:** Ask your team for one measure this year that didn't move, and thank whoever brings it.

### Scenario

**Context:** Your division committed last year to a shorter time from accommodation request to response. It hasn't changed. Your communications lead has drafted an update that opens with 300 course completions and a new toolkit, and doesn't mention the time. The legislature's interest in the program is high.
**Prompt:** What do you do next?

**Option:** Approve it, because the completions and the toolkit are both true.
**Response:** They're true but misleading, and they leave out what you committed to. People who are waiting on accommodation will notice.

**Option (recommended):** Rewrite it to lead with the measure you committed to. Say it hasn't moved, what you've learned about where requests stop and what you'll change. Mention other activity separately as activity.
**Response:** It's harder to publish but credible, and it points to the next step. It also tells your staff that bad news can travel upward.

**Option:** Delay the update until the number improves.
**Response:** Waiting until the news is good teaches everyone that reports are selective. It leaves people without information, and it may not get any better.

### Knowledge check

**Question:** Why does a floor of 10 respondents rule out individual supervisor scores from team surveys on many teams?
**Options:**
- Supervisors' scores are always accurate, so they don't need to be reported.
- Many teams have fewer than 10 respondents, so people could be identified.
- State law prohibits all surveys.
- Staff don't have views on supervisors.

**Correct answer:** Many teams have fewer than 10 respondents, so people could be identified.
**Feedback if correct:** Right. It's a real limit on what anyone can claim about one supervisor, and that should be said openly.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Look for the answer about the floor of 10 respondents and the risk of identifying people.

### Transfer prompt

**Prompt:** Finish this plan: "If I'm asked to sign a report on equity or access, then I'll first ask ____, and I won't sign it if ____ is missing."

## Lesson 4: Session 3: Leading when guidance shifts

**Summary:** A session plan on leading through changes in federal and state direction, with a dated summary of where things stand.
**Minutes:** 13

**Objectives:**
- Host a 75-minute session on shifting guidance.
- Separate what is required, what is an enforcement position and what is your own choice.
- Decide how you will communicate, and what you will keep steady.

**Takeaways:**
- Distinguish law, agency position and your own choice.
- Rest the work on statutes, not orders that can lapse.
- Do not change things quietly. Date your changes, and ask your legal staff before you remove documents.

### Tabs: Where things stand, as of October 2026

#### Tab: State law

Minn. Stat. 43A.21, subd. 3(b), added in 2023, requires all managers and supervisors to be trained on inclusive work environments, disability awareness, cultural competence and other equity and diversity areas. Its page history shows no amendment since 2023. The 2027 session could change it.

#### Tab: State orders and plans

EO 23-12 rescinded EO 19-01. Under Minn. Stat. 4.035, an order expires 90 days after the issuing governor leaves office, and the governor's term ends in January 2027, so EO 23-12 could lapse around April 2027 unless renewed. The DHS Affirmative Action Plan 2024-2026 ended June 30, 2026. It said DHS would assess managers at hire for cultural competency, required the Intercultural Development Inventory and said that completion of training would be tracked. No 2026-2028 plan was found. That plan and this voluntary app are separate. This app has publicly promised not to use the IDI or scores in decisions, and that it doesn't track anyone.

#### Tab: Federal positions

The EEOC's March 2025 technical assistance, still posted, says training can create a hostile environment depending on content, application or context. It also says that access to mentoring and training can't turn on protected class. A July 2025 DOJ memo warns against requiring people to affirm positions or confess biases or privileges, and against penalizing dissent. It also acknowledges that harassment-prevention training that doesn't single out groups is lawful. These are agency positions, not law.

#### Tab: Courts

*United States v. Minnesota* challenges the state's affirmative action system. A docket summary lists DHS's Hiring Justification Policy as an exhibit and reports dispositive motions due by July 15, 2027, with no relief granted. *Young* (10th Cir., 2026) reportedly rejected a claim based on a single training. *Diemert* (9th Cir., Sept. 28, 2026) reportedly sent a Human Services Department employee's claim to trial, based on trainings plus about ten incidents over five years. Neither binds Minnesota. Those opinions weren't read for this course. The sources suggest that how managers behave afterward counts along with the content.

#### Tab: Disability and access

The statutes and *Olmstead* stand. A June 2026 Justice Department legal opinion disclaims an integration mandate, and it's said to bind the executive branch only. DOJ reportedly withdrew its 2011 guidance. Minnesota's Olmstead Plan continues; a September 2026 version is posted, though its adoption was not confirmed. Do not teach that the federal government enforces an integration mandate, and do not teach that the duty has disappeared. Minnesota's digital accessibility standard already applies under MNIT policy, as reported, and the federal Title II date moved to April 26, 2027.

### List: Agenda, 75 minutes

- 5 minutes: Opening script.
- 15 minutes: Read the dated summary above. Mark what's law, what's an agency position and what's a choice your organization made. Check the dates yourself, since these change.
- 20 minutes: Random small groups take the prompts below.
- 25 minutes: Whole group: What stays steady whatever happens?
- 10 minutes: Individually, write what you'll say to your own staff, and what you'll ask your legal staff.

### Accordion: Discussion prompts, ready to use

#### Item: What stays steady

Which duties hold no matter who wins or loses? Accommodation, immediate reporting, consistent criteria, access to services. How often do we say this to staff?

#### Item: What we are choosing

Which of our practices are required by statute, and which are our own choices? Which language have we kept because it's accurate, and which from habit? Where have we dropped accurate words from fear?

#### Item: Behavior after training

If how managers act afterward counts, what do you expect from a manager when someone disagrees with a training or a policy? How do we keep someone from paying a price for disagreeing?

#### Item: Measuring people's beliefs

Should anything we do assess a person's beliefs, or a score from an instrument, for selection or for evaluation? What would we use instead that looks at behavior?

#### Item: Talking to staff

What do your staff hear from you about all this? Are they hearing rumors? Who's worried, and about what? How do you speak to staff who think we've gone too far and those who think we've given up?

#### Item: A transition

A new governor takes office in January 2027. What should be documented and steady before then? What's yours to decide, and what's not?

### Leader move: Date everything, hide nothing

**Control:** You control whether changes are visible.
**Failure:** Words disappear from a page with no explanation. Staff read it as surrender, or others read it as concealment.
**Next:** For any change in your area, say what changed, on what date and why.

### Scenario

**Context:** A director tells you they plan to quietly remove older equity language from hiring materials and web pages before anyone asks about it, including wording tied to the hiring justification process. The director thinks this protects the division. Your staff have been asking what's going on.
**Prompt:** What do you do next?

**Option:** Agree, and do it quickly, since the less attention the better.
**Response:** A preservation order is reported in the pending case. Removing or changing documents without asking could cause a serious problem. Quiet changes also feed rumors among staff.

**Option (recommended):** Ask the director to stop until legal staff have said what must be kept. Then review the materials for accuracy, and make any changes openly, with dates and reasons. Tell staff what is and isn't changing.
**Response:** This addresses the legal risk, keeps accurate language and gives your staff a straight answer. A change can still happen, in the open.

**Option:** Refuse any change, since we shouldn't bow to pressure.
**Response:** Some of the language may need to change for accuracy, or because a plan's period has ended. A blanket no isn't a review. Look at the content.

### Knowledge check

**Question:** Why should a state's own equity training rest on Minn. Stat. 43A.21 rather than on EO 23-12?
**Options:**
- The executive order was never valid.
- An order expires 90 days after the issuing governor leaves office, so EO 23-12 could lapse around April 2027 unless renewed, while the statute stays until changed.
- Statutes can never change.
- The federal government requires it.

**Correct answer:** An order expires 90 days after the issuing governor leaves office, so EO 23-12 could lapse around April 2027 unless renewed, while the statute stays until changed.
**Feedback if correct:** Right. The statute itself could change in the 2027 session, so check again then.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Look for the answer that explains the difference between an order that can lapse and a statute that stays until changed.

### Transfer prompt

**Prompt:** Finish this plan: "If someone in my area proposes to change or remove equity language, then I'll ask ____, and I'll tell staff ____ with a date."

## Lesson 5: Session 4: Program integrity governance

**Summary:** A session plan on how senior leaders can govern oversight for consistency, appeals and language access.
**Minutes:** 13

**Objectives:**
- Host a 75-minute session on oversight governance.
- Ask what leaders need to see in order to judge whether oversight is fair and accurate.
- Consider how public statements affect communities and providers.

**Takeaways:**
- Ask to see confirmed results and access effects side by side.
- Find out how appeals feed back into method.
- Choose your words in public about conduct, never communities.

### Text: Background to share

DHS reports stepped-up program integrity actions, including freezes on new enrollment in some services, payment suspensions and provider revalidation, as reported in summaries. Minnesota's federal Medicaid funds are also reported as under deferral on program-integrity grounds. Some providers have said publicly that they lost funding despite meeting requirements. That's their account. No data were found on how these actions fall by race, language or community, and no benchmark for fair oversight training was found. So part of this session is about what you don't know.

Fair Oversight in Program Integrity covers supervisor habits. Equitable Oversight Design for Program Integrity Leaders goes into notices and hearings. This session is about what only senior leaders can settle: what you ask to see, how fast you ask for it and what you say in public.

### List: Agenda, 75 minutes

- 5 minutes: Opening script. Add this: no open cases, no named providers.
- 15 minutes: Individually, write down what you'd need to see to know that oversight is accurate and fair. Then check whether you currently see it.
- 20 minutes: Random small groups take the first three prompts.
- 25 minutes: Whole group on the rest.
- 10 minutes: Individually, write down one question you'll ask your program integrity lead.

### Accordion: Discussion prompts, ready to use

#### Item: What you see

Do you see how many flags are confirmed, or only how many are raised? Do you see how many people lost services, and how fast they were reconnected? Do you see results by provider size and region? If you can't, who could produce that?

#### Item: Consistency

Are there written criteria for each level of response? When two teams treat similar facts differently, how would you know? Who checks a sample?

#### Item: Appeals

What do appeals and reversals teach you? Does anyone feed them back into the method? Are people able to use the appeal route if they read another language or use another format? Who decides, and are they separate from the original decision?

#### Item: Language access

What do your notices look like to someone reading them in another language or format? As of October 2026, federal guidance has been pulling back while Title VI and Section 1557 remain statutes. What's your own standard, based on what the work needs? Whom do you ask about duties under Minnesota law?

#### Item: Pressure and speed

What are you asking for, and how fast? Where does speed push out a second read? Could your staff tell you that a flag is bad? When did they last do so?

#### Item: Public words

What's your rule for statements about fraud? Do they describe conduct? What might a family hear who gets services lawfully from a provider in a community that's in the news? Who checks a statement before it goes out?

#### Item: What would change our mind

If reviews of the method showed that it burdened some providers without finding much, what would you do? Would you be willing to report that?

### Leader move: Ask for both columns

**Control:** You decide what appears on the page in front of you.
**Failure:** A leader asks only about actions taken and dollars identified. Staff conclude that accuracy and access don't matter.
**Next:** Ask for the next report to show results and access effects side by side.

### Scenario

**Context:** Under public pressure, a leadership team is drafting a statement about a review in one service. An early draft mentions the community that many of the affected providers belong to, because reporters have already used that framing. Reviewers have verified only some of the flagged cases, and some of those had innocent explanations.
**Prompt:** What do you do next?

**Option:** Keep the mention, since it's already in the news and it shows you're being open.
**Response:** Repeating that framing links a community to fraud under your authority, before verification, and it could harm families and honest providers. It's not what openness means.

**Option:** Say nothing until every case is verified.
**Response:** People who get services need information, and silence feeds fear. You can say something accurate now.

**Option (recommended):** Describe the conduct the review looks for and the process. Say verification is ongoing. Make no reference to a community. Tell people what to do if their services are affected. Ask for the confirmation rate before any statement that claims results.
**Response:** It's truthful, it doesn't prejudge anyone and it's useful to those affected. It also shows your team how you expect these statements to be written.

### Knowledge check

**Question:** Which request best helps a senior leader judge whether oversight is accurate and fair?
**Options:**
- "How many actions did we take?"
- "Which community has the most fraud?"
- "Of the cases we flagged, how many were confirmed, what did appeals show and how many people lost services?"
- "How many reviews can we finish by next month?"

**Correct answer:** "Of the cases we flagged, how many were confirmed, what did appeals show and how many people lost services?"
**Feedback if correct:** Right. This looks at accuracy and at effects on people, side by side.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Look for the question that covers both accuracy and effects. Counting actions alone tells you little, and asking about a community repeats the problem.

### Transfer prompt

**Prompt:** Finish this plan: "If I next meet with the person who leads program integrity in my area, then I'll ask ____, and if they can't answer I'll ask who can by ____."

## Job aid

**Title:** Hosting a senior leader decision session
**Subtitle:** Four sessions, one set of ground rules

**Purpose:** Give senior leaders a ready way to think through decisions together, voluntarily and without records.

**Remember:**
- Voluntary. No attendance, no records of the discussion.
- No identity-based breakouts. No required disclosure. Disagreement carries no consequence.
- Reporting duties under Policies 1436 and 1329 still apply.
- The session decides nothing for the organization. Leaders act afterward through normal channels.

### Every session

- Say the opening script.
- Form small groups at random or by interest.
- Plan access first.
- Take down anything on the walls. Collect nothing.
- End with each person's own next step, kept by them.

### The four sessions

- Owner, budget, outcome: Who could stop a process? What's actually paid for? What result might we be wrong about?
- Measures: What would we report even if it didn't move? What can't we prove at our size?
- Shifting guidance: What's law, what's an agency position and what's our choice? What stays steady?
- Program integrity: Do we see confirmed results and access effects together? What do appeals teach us? What's our rule for public words?

### Refer out

- Tribal learning: ADSA and the Tribal liaison.
- Required training: the state's own training under Minn. Stat. 43A.21.
- Legal questions, including document retention: your legal staff.

**Do next:** Pick one session topic, invite two or three peers and read the opening script aloud when you begin.
