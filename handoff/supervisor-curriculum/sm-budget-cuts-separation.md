# sm-budget-cuts-separation: Budget Cuts, Reassignment and Separation

**Subtitle:** What a supervisor actually controls when positions, duties or people are affected: the input you give, the criteria you use, the conversation you have, and what you do for the people who stay.
**Duration:** About 58 minutes
**Content type:** formal-support
**Status:** reviewed
**Last reviewed:** October 31, 2026
**Evidence date:** October 2026
**Scope:** For supervisors and managers facing budget-driven change, or planning for it. This is general information, not legal advice. It is voluntary. It describes no actual DSD or DHS plan, and we have no information about one.

**Course objectives:**
- Separate what you decide, what you influence and what you only carry out.
- Give input on cuts and reassignments using work-based criteria set before names.
- Check a proposed change for uneven effects, and keep access duties in place.
- Hold a reassignment or separation conversation with dignity, accuracy and proper support.
- Reset the work and rebuild steady supervision for the people who stay.

**Applied next step:** Find the articles on layoff, reassignment and work rules in each contract or plan that covers your team. Write down one question for labor relations before you need the answer.

## Lesson 1: Before anything is announced

**Summary:** What you know and do not know, what you may say, and how to give input if someone asks you where the cuts could come from.
**Minutes:** 14

**Objectives:**
- Sort a budget situation into what you decide, influence and carry out.
- Hold confidential information without misleading people.
- Write criteria before names if you are asked for input.
- Say no to promises you cannot keep.

**Takeaways:**
- Do not promise anyone their job is safe. Do not hint that it isn't.
- Separate rumor, proposal and decision, every time.
- Criteria come before names.
- No private warnings to people you like.

### Text: Where this comes from
Budget pressure is not abstract at DHS right now. The Minnesota Attorney General and KFF have reported that federal officials deferred hundreds of millions of dollars in Minnesota Medicaid funds on program-integrity grounds, and that a suit over it was reported stayed as of May 2026. The figures vary from one report to the next. We don't know what any of this means for your unit, and neither may your manager. You could also face cuts for ordinary reasons: a grant ending, a legislative change or a reorganization.

Other courses cover part of this. "Trust during uncertainty and change" (dc-t20) teaches honest updates when the future is undecided. "Sharing difficult news with a team" (dc-t39) teaches how to deliver hard news. "Sustaining equity work when resources are tight" (dc-l100) teaches how to plan equity work under constraints. This course covers what those leave out: the personnel side, where a supervisor's decisions touch a particular person's job.

### Tabs: What you actually control
#### Tab: You decide
How you treat people. What you tell them truthfully. How you document your input. Whether the person gets a good handoff, an accessible conversation, a fair reference to the right office, and a decent last day. How you reset the work after.

#### Tab: You influence
Which functions you describe as essential. Whether your manager hears the real cost of a cut. What criteria get used. Whether someone looks for uneven effects. Which access costs get protected.

#### Tab: You carry out
Budget totals. Which positions are affected, in most cases. Layoff order and rights where a contract sets them. Timing and notice. Ask labor relations and Human Resources how those rules work. Do not explain them from memory.

### Text: Holding what you know
You may know something before your team does. You may be told not to share it. Sometimes you will know nothing, and no one will believe that.

Be honest about what you can say. Do not lie, and do not say "nothing is happening" if something is. It's fine to say, "I can't talk about everything I hear in management meetings, and I won't guess. When I can tell you something, I will, and here's the next date I'll update you." The same rule from "Trust during uncertainty and change" applies here: a proposal is not a decision.

Here is the temptation that does the most harm: warning someone you like. A quiet word to a trusted employee feels like loyalty. It gives that person an advantage others don't have, it spreads, and it can follow patterns of who you are close to. It also may break a rule you were given. If someone needs early notice, that is for Human Resources to arrange.

### Statement
You can't promise someone a job. You can promise them the truth as soon as you can tell it.

### Text: If you are asked for input
You may be asked: "If you lost one position, which would it be?" or "List your unit's functions in priority order." Your answer can shape someone's life. Treat it as a selection decision.

The same discipline from "Hiring Decisions: Criteria Before Candidates" (sm-hiring-decisions) applies. Write down what the unit must do, what's required by law or by contract, and what would happen to people served if each function stopped. Do this before you think about who holds each job. Then ask labor relations whether your input is about functions or about people, because the rules for each may differ. Describe functions and not the people doing them, unless you're told otherwise.

### List: Criteria you can stand behind
- What legal, contract or program duty does this function meet?
- Who loses what if it stops, and who is affected most?
- Can someone else do it, and at what added cost?
- Does the function exist only on paper, or does it hold something else up?
- What hidden work sits in this position, such as community contact, onboarding, interpreter scheduling or accessibility checks?
- What did I write down, and when?

### Accordion: Criteria to avoid or check
#### Item: "Fit," "attitude," "flexibility"
These are hard to check and easy to apply unevenly. If you mean a specific skill, name that skill and explain how the work uses it.

#### Item: "Availability" or "attendance"
These can pull in leave, accommodation and caregiving. Do not use them without talking to Human Resources first.

#### Item: "Who would take it best"
You'd be guessing about someone's finances, family and resilience, and those guesses can follow stereotypes. It's not a work criterion.

#### Item: Protected traits, either way
Do not weigh race, sex, disability or another protected trait in an individual decision, whether to protect someone or to expose them. *Ames v. Ohio Department of Youth Services* (June 5, 2025) applies the same Title VII standard to every employee. The EEOC's March 2025 technical assistance says there is no "diversity interest" exception. And the state's affirmative action system is itself being litigated in *United States v. Minnesota*, with no relief granted as last reported. Looking for uneven effects is a separate job, and Lesson 2 covers it.

### Leader move: Your manager hears the real cost
**Control:** You control whether the people deciding hear what a cut will really do. Be plain and specific.
**Failure:** Do not say your unit can absorb it because that is what your manager wants to hear. Do not overstate the harm either, since you'll lose credibility when it counts.
**Next:** Draft one page: functions, duties behind them, who is affected if each stops, and what you are unsure about.

### Scenario
**Context:** Your manager asks you, confidentially, to list which of your unit's positions could be cut with the least harm, by Thursday. Your unit includes policy staff and consultants who work with counties and waiver providers. You immediately think of a newer employee who has struggled a bit. You also think about a consultant who has an accommodation and uses more of your time.
**Prompt:** What do you do?

**Option 1:** List the two people you thought of, with a short note about each.
**Response:** You've let names come first, and one of them is linked to an accommodation. That's a serious risk for the person and the agency. "More of my time" may simply be what the accommodation involves.

**Option 2:** Ask your manager and labor relations what kind of input is needed. Then write the functions, the duties behind them and who is affected if each stops, before you think about people.
**Response:** This is the best step. You've checked the process, and your reasoning is about the work. If you later have to discuss individuals, the criteria will already exist.
**Recommended:** yes

**Option 3:** Tell your manager you can't choose, because every position is essential.
**Response:** It may be how it feels, and it gives decision makers nothing to work with. Someone else will decide with less information. You can name the real costs and still answer.

**Option 4:** Talk it over with your two most senior staff, in confidence, to get their view.
**Response:** You were told this is confidential, and they'd be weighing in on colleagues' jobs and maybe their own. It puts them in a bad position and it will likely spread.

### Knowledge check
**Question:** A consultant asks you directly, "Is my job safe?" You've heard proposals but nothing is decided. What is the best answer?
- "Don't worry, you're fine."
- "I can't say for sure. I'd start looking if I were you."
- "Nothing has been decided that I can share. I won't guess, and I'll update you by [date], even if there's nothing new." (correct)
- "I can't talk about that," and leave it there.
**Correct answer:** "Nothing has been decided that I can share. I won't guess, and I'll update you by [date], even if there's nothing new."
**Feedback if correct:** Yes. It's honest about what you can say, it makes no promise you can't keep, and it gives them something dependable.
**Feedback if incorrect:** A promise and a hint can both turn out false and do real harm. A flat refusal is more honest but leaves the person with nothing. Say what you can and give a date.

### Transfer prompt
**Prompt:** Write one if-then plan: "If I'm asked for input on a cut, then before I think about who holds each job, I will write down ____ and ask ____."
**Options:**
- If I'm asked to name positions, then I will first ask labor relations what the rules require.
- If I want to warn someone privately, then I will ask Human Resources whether early notice is possible for that person.
- If I hear a rumor, then I will say what's known, what isn't, and when I'll update.

## Lesson 2: Reassignment and uneven effects

**Summary:** Reassignment seems gentle because no one loses a paycheck. This lesson shows why it needs the same care, how to check for uneven effects, and which duties a budget cannot erase.
**Minutes:** 15

**Objectives:**
- Explain why a reassignment with no pay change can still cause harm.
- Check a change for uneven effects without making race-conscious individual decisions.
- Keep accommodations and access costs in place during a change.
- Check for notice and work rule steps.

**Takeaways:**
- Same pay does not mean no harm.
- A pattern is a reason to review the process. It isn't a reason to change one person's outcome by race or sex.
- A tight budget does not cancel an accommodation.
- Check the contract before you announce.

### Text: A reassignment can be a real harm
In *Muldrow v. City of St. Louis* (April 17, 2024), a city employee was transferred. Rank and pay stayed the same. Duties, schedule and perks changed. The Supreme Court held that a person challenging a transfer must show "some harm" to an identifiable term or condition of work, and it does not need to be significant.

That doesn't mean every reassignment is illegal. It means this: if a protected trait motivated a change, the change can be challenged even when pay stays the same. And *Ames* (June 5, 2025) makes clear the same standard applies to every employee, including those in majority groups. So apply your criteria the same way to everyone, and write them down.

Beyond the law, think about what a person actually loses: a caseload they know, a community relationship they've built over years, a schedule that fits their child care or dialysis, a role that was leading toward promotion, work with people who share a language with them.

### List: Before you reassign anyone
- What work needs doing, and why this person? Write the reason in terms of the work.
- Who else could do it? Why not them?
- What does this person lose: visibility, schedule, telework, a development path, a relationship?
- Does this person have an accommodation? Does it work in the new role?
- Does the contract set any steps for this? For MAPE-represented staff, Article 5 lists employer rights, including to assign and direct, and Article 28 requires discussion of new or amended work rules, usually with 10 working days' posted notice. For others, check their contract or plan.
- Who has looked at it?

### Text: Looking for uneven effects
A rule can be neutral on its face and still land unevenly. "Last hired" may fall hardest on recent hires. If recent recruiting brought more staff of color or disabled staff into certain roles, a cut to those roles falls on them. A cut to "non-core" functions may fall on community engagement or language access, and on the people who do that work. A return-to-site or location change may fall hardest on people with disabilities, caregivers or people far from the metro area. We have no DSD data on this. These are questions to ask, not findings.

Here's what the law looks like as of October 2026, and it's unsettled. Federal agencies have largely stepped back from disparate impact. HHS removed its Title VI disparate-impact provisions on July 24, 2026, and a June 2026 Office of Legal Counsel opinion, as reported, calls the EEOC's Title VII framework unconstitutional; that opinion binds federal agencies, not courts. Title VII's disparate-impact provision is still in the statute, private suits remain possible, and the Minnesota Human Rights Act prohibits maintaining "a system of employment which unreasonably excludes" applicants. So don't tell anyone disparate impact is dead, and don't tell them it's enforced as before.

### Statement
A pattern tells you to look at the process. It never tells you to change one person's outcome because of their race or sex.

### Tabs: What to do if you see a pattern
#### Tab: Say it
Tell your manager and Human Resources what you noticed, in plain terms: "This cut seems to fall mostly on the staff who do language access work and on recent hires. Can we look at that?" Stick with what you've seen. Do not guess about individuals' identities or look up confidential demographic information.

#### Tab: Review the process
Ask whether the criterion actually measures what the work needs. Is there another way to reach the same savings? Would phasing, or a different function, cost less? The answer may be that the rule stands. You've still done the check, and it's on record.

#### Tab: Do not fix it privately
Do not quietly swap names or shade a description to protect someone. It's unfair to someone else, it can be its own violation, and it hides the problem from those who could fix it.

### Accordion: What a budget doesn't cancel
#### Item: Accommodations
A tight budget isn't a reason to withdraw or refuse an accommodation on your own authority. Undue hardship is a formal determination, and you shouldn't make it. Bring the cost question to Human Resources or the ADA coordinator. An accommodation should also move with a person being reassigned, as "Accommodations Across Transitions" (gc-accommodation-transitions) explains.

#### Item: Interpreters, captioning and accessible formats
These are often the first line someone proposes to cut because they look like extras. Staff and members of the public depend on them. Raise it with your manager before the line disappears, and ask who owns that decision. As "Sustaining equity work when resources are tight" (dc-l100) says, a constraint is information for the decision, not permission to waive a requirement.

#### Item: Language access
Federal guidance has changed: Executive Order 13166 was revoked in 2025. Title VI and Section 1557 remain statutes. Treat language access as part of whether people can reach the program at all. Ask who can tell you what applies to your program.

#### Item: Reporting duties
A reorganization doesn't pause your duty to immediately report discrimination or harassment under Policy 1436. A person who raised a concern must not be reassigned or cut because of it. If that person is affected, get a second look. See "When You Are Named in a Complaint" (sm-named-in-complaint) and "Preventing retaliation" (dc-l73).

#### Item: Work with Tribal governments
If a change touches work with Tribal governments, contact the Tribal liaison and ADSA early. This course does not teach that relationship. Consultation has its own duties and its own people.

### Leader move: Show your reasoning
**Control:** You control whether you can explain a reassignment in two plain sentences that would make sense to anyone on the team.
**Failure:** Do not call a reassignment a "great opportunity" if it isn't one. People know, and it costs you trust.
**Next:** Before announcing any change, read your reason aloud and ask: would I give this same reason if the person were someone else on the team?

### Scenario
**Context:** Your division has to reduce travel and shift work. You supervise consultants who cover regions across Minnesota. You must consolidate two regions into one. One option moves a consultant who has built strong relationships with Hmong families and county staff in one region, and who has an accommodation that limits driving, to a metro-only caseload with no external partner work. The other option moves a consultant with less history in their region, who has no restriction, and who would have to drive more. Your manager prefers the first because it saves more money.
**Prompt:** What do you do next?

**Option 1:** Go with the first option. It saves more, and the manager prefers it. Pay isn't changing.
**Response:** Pay isn't the whole story. The person loses partner work and community relationships, and the change may be linked to their accommodation. Families also lose someone they trust. You haven't compared the real costs yet.

**Option 2:** Write down the work-based comparison, including what each person and the families would lose and whether the accommodation works in each option. Ask Human Resources and labor relations to look at it, and give your manager the real comparison.
**Response:** This is the best step. You've compared whole options on the same questions, you've treated the accommodation as a fact to plan around rather than a reason to reduce the job, and the decision maker gets the real costs.
**Recommended:** yes

**Option 3:** Choose the second option because the first consultant shares a community's background with the families, and that's what the families need.
**Response:** The relationships and language skills are real, work-based facts and can count. Matching staff to clients by race or national origin is another matter: the EEOC says client preference is no defense to race or color discrimination. Base it on skill, relationship and caseload history, and stay off identity.

**Option 4:** Ask the two consultants to work out which of them will move.
**Response:** You'd be handing them a management decision, without their having full information about the budget or each other's accommodations. The person with less standing may give in.

### Knowledge check
**Question:** Which statement matches the law as of October 2026?
- Disparate impact is dead, so uneven effects don't matter.
- Federal enforcers have largely stepped back from disparate impact, but Title VII's provision and Minnesota law remain, and private suits are possible. (correct)
- Disparate impact is enforced by federal agencies the same way it always was.
- A reassignment with no pay change can't be challenged.
**Correct answer:** Federal enforcers have largely stepped back from disparate impact, but Title VII's provision and Minnesota law remain, and private suits are possible.
**Feedback if correct:** Correct. It is unsettled, so state it that way. Either way, a pattern is a reason to review the process, not to change one person's outcome by a protected trait.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Both extremes are wrong. Federal agencies have pulled back, but the statute and Minnesota law are still there. *Muldrow* also means a change can matter even if pay stays the same.

### Transfer prompt
**Prompt:** Think of a change you might have to make. Write one if-then plan: "If I reassign ____, then I will first write ____ and check ____."
**Options:**
- If someone has an accommodation, then I will confirm it works in the new role before anything is announced.
- If a cut seems to fall on one kind of work or one group of staff, then I will raise that with my manager and Human Resources.
- If interpreter or accessibility costs are on the list, then I will ask who owns that decision and what duty sits behind it.

## Lesson 3: The conversation

**Summary:** How to prepare for and hold a conversation about reassignment, layoff or separation. You will probably have this conversation only a few times in your career. Each person will remember it for the rest of theirs.
**Minutes:** 15

**Objectives:**
- Prepare with Human Resources and labor relations before you speak.
- Set up the conversation so the person can fully take part.
- Say the hard part clearly and early.
- Handle common responses without promising, arguing or over-explaining.

**Takeaways:**
- Prepare with Human Resources first. Know who says what.
- Make the meeting accessible without having to ask why.
- Say it in the first minute.
- Don't invent a reason. Don't promise anything.

### Text: There are different kinds of separation
A layoff, the end of a grant-funded position, failing probation, resignation, retirement, discharge for cause and a transfer out each follow different rules. This course has no procedure for any of them, because the research behind it did not include one. For each case, ask Human Resources and labor relations what applies, who has authority and what must be in writing.

Some of what applies is public. As of October 2026, Minnesota Statutes section 13.43 lists the final disposition of discipline with reasons among public personnel data. Other personnel data is private. So ask before you describe why someone left, whether to the team, a provider or someone calling for a reference.

Discharge for performance or conduct is covered in "Corrective Action and Labor Agreements" (sm-corrective-action). This lesson mainly concerns news the person didn't cause.

### List: Prepare with Human Resources and labor relations
- Who has the authority to say this? Should you, or someone else?
- What exactly can be said, and what must not be said?
- What's in writing, and who hands it over?
- Does the person have a right to a union representative? How and when are they told?
- What are the person's options, deadlines and rights, and who explains them?
- What happens right after: access, equipment, last day, who tells the team?
- Is there anything unusual here, such as an open complaint, leave or an accommodation? Tell Human Resources before the meeting.

### List: Set it up so the person can take part
- Choose a private place. If they are remote, use video or the way you usually meet.
- If the person uses an interpreter, book a qualified one, through your usual arrangement. Do not ask a coworker to interpret, ever.
- Have captioning ready if they use it. Have the written notice in a format they can read.
- Plan for the language they work in. If someone's first language isn't English, ask Human Resources what's available, and don't assume.
- Allow time. Don't schedule anything right after it.
- Ask Human Resources what else they should know about in advance, such as who else will be in the room.

### Statement
Say the hard part in the first minute. Everything before it is for you, not for them.

### Accordion: The conversation, step by step
#### Item: Open
"I have hard news, and I want to tell you directly. [Name of person from Human Resources] is here to go through the details with you."

#### Item: Say it
"Your position is being eliminated, effective [date]." Use the words that fit, the ones Human Resources gave you. "This is a decision about the budget and the position. It's not about the quality of your work." Say this only if it's true.

#### Item: Stop talking
They need a moment. Don't fill it. You may feel an urge to talk about how hard this is for you. Don't.

#### Item: Give the next steps
Let Human Resources explain rights and options. Tell them what you know about the next few days. Give them the written notice. Tell them who to call with questions, and offer the Employee Assistance Program.

#### Item: Ask what they need right now
"Do you want to stay, go home or take some time? Is there anything you want me to tell the team, or not tell them?"

#### Item: Close
"I'll check in with you on [date]." And do it.

### Tabs: Common responses
#### Tab: "Why me?"
Say what's true and what you've been authorized to say. If criteria were used, state them plainly. If it's set by the contract, say so and let labor relations explain. Don't make up a reason, and don't compare them to a colleague.

#### Tab: Anger
Let them speak. Don't defend the agency or correct them in the moment. "I hear you. You have every reason to be upset." If you feel unsafe, follow your emergency procedure. Don't read anger as a threat on its own.

#### Tab: Silence or tears
Give them time. Offer water, a break or to continue later. Don't pat them on the shoulder or tell them it will all work out.

#### Tab: "Is this because of my complaint?" or "because of my leave?"
Take it seriously. Do not argue. "I hear you asking that. Human Resources is here and can take that up with you, and you have the right to raise it." If it's a report of discrimination or retaliation, you have to report it. Tell Human Resources right away.

#### Tab: "Can you do anything?" or "Will you write me a reference?"
Don't promise. Say what you can actually do, and ask Human Resources about references before you agree to anything, because state data rules affect what you may share.

### Leader move: Things you do not say
**Control:** You control your words. Prepare the first three sentences and say them as written.
**Failure:** Do not say "I know how you feel," "This is a blessing in disguise," "I fought for you," or "If it were up to me." Each one puts your comfort first, or points blame somewhere they can't reach.
**Next:** Practice the first three sentences aloud with Human Resources or your manager, more than once.

### Scenario
**Context:** A grant is ending, and a position on your team will end with it. The person in it is Deaf and works with you through a staff interpreter on most days. Human Resources has confirmed the rules and the notice. The team's usual interpreter is a state employee who knows the person and the team well. You think it might be kinder to tell the person in a quick private chat in writing first, and hold the formal meeting afterward, so that no third person hears the news first.
**Prompt:** How do you set this up?

**Option 1:** Send a private chat message first. It's more private, and you can both type.
**Response:** You've given the news in a way you wouldn't give it to a hearing employee, and they lose the chance to respond and ask questions in their own language. Choosing for them is the problem.

**Option 2:** Ask a hearing coworker who knows some sign language to help so it stays in the team.
**Response:** Do not do this. A coworker isn't a qualified interpreter, and now a colleague knows about a private personnel matter. Accuracy matters a great deal here.

**Option 3:** Ask Human Resources how to arrange a qualified interpreter for a confidential personnel meeting, consider whether someone other than the person's usual interpreter should be used, and set the same meeting you'd hold for anyone, with the written notice in hand.
**Response:** This is the best step. The person gets the news directly, in their own language, with the same rights as anyone else. It's reasonable to consider existing relationships when you choose an interpreter. If you can find out the person's preference without giving away the news, follow it.
**Recommended:** yes

**Option 4:** Have someone from Human Resources handle it entirely so you don't make a mistake.
**Response:** Human Resources should be there, but stepping out entirely can feel like abandonment when you're their supervisor. Unless they advise you otherwise, be there.

### Knowledge check
**Question:** During a layoff conversation, the employee says, "This is because I complained last year." What should you do?
- Explain why that's not true, so they don't leave believing it.
- Ignore it and go on with the steps.
- Take it seriously, don't argue, tell them they can raise it, and tell Human Resources right away. (correct)
- Offer to look into it yourself.
**Correct answer:** Take it seriously, don't argue, tell them they can raise it, and tell Human Resources right away.
**Feedback if correct:** Yes. It may be a report of retaliation. You don't decide it, you don't argue, and you make sure it reaches the right place.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Arguing or looking into it yourself takes on a role that isn't yours, and ignoring it may breach your duty to report. Listen, tell them about their route, and tell Human Resources.

### Transfer prompt
**Prompt:** Think of the hardest news you might have to give. Write one if-then plan: "If I have to tell someone ____, then I will first ask Human Resources ____, and my first sentence will be ____."
**Options:**
- If the person uses an interpreter or captioning, then I will arrange it through Human Resources in a way that protects confidentiality.
- If there's an open complaint, leave or accommodation, then I will tell Human Resources before the meeting.
- If I feel the urge to say "I fought for you," then I will say what happens next instead.

## Lesson 4: Those who leave and those who stay

**Summary:** How to treat someone well on the way out, and how to reset the work and rebuild steady supervision for those who stay.
**Minutes:** 14

**Objectives:**
- Support a dignified departure.
- Tell the team what happened without saying more than you should.
- Reset the work so the people who stay don't simply absorb it.
- Review who was affected and what you learned.

**Takeaways:**
- People watch how you treat those who leave.
- Cut the work, not only the people.
- Say what has stopped. Put it in writing.
- Those who stay may need honest updates, not a pep talk.

### List: A dignified departure
- Ask how they want the team told, and whether they want to tell people themselves.
- Agree on a handoff that honors their knowledge. Ask what no one else knows they do.
- Tell the people who depend on them, such as families, counties or providers, who to contact next. Keep the reason out of it.
- Make sure they have what they need: the Employee Assistance Program contact, the Human Resources contact and any union contact.
- If they want a farewell, make one. If they don't, don't. Don't let it depend on how well liked they were.
- If you can, offer an exit conversation, and ask who the right person is to hold it. "Stay and exit interviews" (stay-and-exit-interviews) can help.
- Credit their work specifically, in front of those who should know.

### Text: What to tell the team
Tell them soon. Ask Human Resources what you can say. Something like: "As of [date], [person] will be leaving, and their position has ended because the grant is ending. Here is who covers what. Here is what is stopping. Here's what I know about what comes next, and what I don't."

If the departure is for performance or conduct, say much less: "[Person] no longer works here. I can't discuss individual personnel matters, and I wouldn't discuss yours." Do not speculate. Do not let silence suggest something that isn't true.

"Sharing difficult news with a team" (dc-t39) covers how to do this in detail, including how to hear disappointment and come back with an update.

### Statement
If the people are gone and the work stays, you have cut the wrong thing.

### Text: Reset the work
The biggest failure after cuts is quiet: the work stays and those left carry it. Those most committed will take on the most, and they may be the same people who carry hidden work already. Six months later you've lost them as well.

Evidence on layoffs and fairness was not in the research for this course. There is good evidence that supervisors affect whether people stay. Hoffman and Tadelis (2021) found people management skills strongly predict lower turnover at one high-tech firm, with designs that support a causal reading. Friebel, Heinz and Zubanov found that when a CEO asked managers of randomly selected stores "to do what they can" to reduce turnover, turnover fell by a quarter for nine months, and employees reported more attention and support. Neither was in state government, and neither was about budget cuts. They do suggest that your attention is worth something here.

### List: A work reset
1. List everything the departed person did, including what wasn't in their job description.
2. Mark each item: keep, move, reduce or stop.
3. For each thing moved, name what comes off the receiver's plate.
4. Get your manager to agree in writing about what stops. If you can't get agreement, write down what you asked for and what you were told.
5. Tell your team what has stopped, and tell the people who relied on it.
6. Look again in 30 days and in 90.

### Accordion: What those who stay may need
#### Item: Honest answers about what's next
"Is there more coming?" Say what you know, what you don't, and when you'll update. Keep to that date, even if you have nothing new.

#### Item: Permission to be upset
You don't need them to be positive. Don't ask them to be grateful. If you hear disagreement, treat it as information.

#### Item: A check on their load
Ask each person, in their one-to-one: "What has landed on you since this happened? What should I take off?" Look for the people who say nothing.

#### Item: Equal treatment of their worry
Some may feel more exposed: recent hires, people with accommodations, people on temporary appointments, visa holders, people who just came back from leave. Don't assume who is worried. Ask everyone the same question, and tell them what you know.

#### Item: Your own support
You're also someone who has just lost a colleague, and you may have delivered the news. See "Your Own Load" (sm-your-own-load).

### Leader move: Look back
**Control:** You control whether anyone looks at what happened. After the dust settles, review who was affected, on what criteria, and what happened to the work and the people served.
**Failure:** Do not declare it done because the budget closed. Do not pull names and identity data together on your own to check; keep it to process, and take any pattern question to Human Resources.
**Next:** Write a half-page note for your manager: what the criteria were, what stopped, what you'd do differently, and what you still don't know.

### Scenario
**Context:** Two months after losing a position, your team seems fine. Work is getting done. In your assignment log, you see that one consultant has taken on most of the departed person's external partner work, on top of her own caseload. She hasn't complained. She mentions that she's been working evenings "just until things settle." Your manager has told you the team is a model of resilience.
**Prompt:** What do you do?

**Option 1:** Thank her in the next team meeting and mention her in your report to your manager.
**Response:** Thanks is good, but it changes nothing. Public praise can also tell everyone that this is what's expected.

**Option 2:** In your next one-to-one, name what you've seen, ask what she'd drop, take something off her plate this week, and tell your manager what's being held up and at what cost.
**Response:** This is the best step. You've used your record, you've made a real change and you've corrected the "resilience" story with facts. Ask Human Resources about any pay or time rules for evening work.
**Recommended:** yes

**Option 3:** Leave it alone. She hasn't complained, and the work is getting done.
**Response:** People often don't complain, especially when they're worried about their own jobs. It's your job to notice, and you already have.

**Option 4:** Divide all partner work equally among the team from now on.
**Response:** It's a real change, but equal isn't the same as fair, and you haven't looked at who has the relationships or the skills. Nor have you asked what should stop.

### Knowledge check
**Question:** After a position is cut, what is the best sign that you have reset the work?
- Everyone is working hard, and morale is steady.
- You've thanked those who stepped up.
- You've listed what the person did, decided what stops, taken something off each person who took on work, and told your manager and those affected. (correct)
- All the work has been divided equally.
**Correct answer:** You've listed what the person did, decided what stops, taken something off each person who took on work, and told your manager and those affected.
**Feedback if correct:** Yes. A reset means real decisions about the work, stated openly.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Effort and thanks can hide the problem, and equal shares may not be fair. A reset means real decisions about what stops, what moves and what comes off someone's plate.

### Transfer prompt
**Prompt:** Write one if-then plan: "If my team loses a person, then within two weeks I will ____, and at 90 days I will ____."
**Options:**
- If the work stays and the person leaves, then I will list keep, move, reduce or stop, and get what stops in writing.
- If someone says nothing but is doing more, then I will raise it in the next one-to-one.
- If my manager praises the team's resilience, then I will reply with what has slowed and what it's costing.

## Job aid

**Title:** Cuts, reassignment and separation: a supervisor's checklist
**Subtitle:** Before, during and after.

**Purpose:** Help you stay fair and truthful when positions, duties or people are affected, and call the right people before you act.

**Remember:**
- You can't promise a job. You can promise the truth when you can tell it.
- Criteria come before names.
- A pattern calls for a review of process, never a change based on one person's race or sex.
- If the people go and the work stays, you cut the wrong thing.

### Section: Call first
- Labor relations: contract articles on layoff, reassignment and work rules, and representation.
- Human Resources: what you may say, who says it, what's in writing, references.
- ADA coordinator: any accommodation or cost question.
- Tribal liaison and ADSA: any change touching work with Tribal governments.

### Section: Before you give input or reassign
- What duty sits behind this function?
- Who loses what if it stops?
- What hidden work is in this position?
- Would I give this reason for anyone on the team?
- Does any accommodation still work?
- Is anyone who raised a concern affected? Get a second look.
- Do uneven effects show? Say so to Human Resources.

### Section: The conversation
- Prepare with Human Resources. Practice your first three sentences.
- Arrange a qualified interpreter, captioning or accessible notice. Never use a coworker to interpret.
- Say it in the first minute.
- Stop talking. Then give next steps.
- If they raise a complaint or leave, take it to Human Resources right away.
- Don't invent reasons or promise anything.

### Section: After
- Ask how they want the team told.
- Tell the team what's changing, soon, within what you may say.
- List the work: keep, move, reduce or stop.
- Take something off each receiver's plate.
- Check at 30 and 90 days.
- Write a short look-back for your manager.

**Do next:** Find the articles on layoff, reassignment and work rules in each contract that covers your team, and write down your first question for labor relations.
