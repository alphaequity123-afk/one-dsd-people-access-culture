# sm-fair-assignments: Fair Assignments, Schedules and Visibility

**Subtitle:** Practice the same decision twice with the people swapped. If your criteria hold both ways, you can stand behind the decision.
**Duration:** About 60 minutes across five lessons. Take them in short sittings.
**Content type:** practice
**Status:** reviewed
**Last reviewed:** October 31, 2026
**Evidence date:** October 2026
**Scope note:** This is general information as of October 2026, not legal advice. This course is voluntary and does not count as the training Minn. Stat. 43A.21 requires; the state's own training does that.

**Course objectives:**
- Use the swap test: state your criteria, swap the people, and see whether the decision stands.
- Separate a real job requirement, such as a language skill, from an assumption about who a person is.
- Apply consistent criteria to stretch work, routine work, schedules, telework and reassignments.
- Distinguish applying the same criteria from giving the same outcome, including where an accommodation is involved.
- Keep a short record that lets you explain a decision and review your own process.

**Applied next step:** Choose the next assignment you will make that someone would want or want to avoid. Before you think about names, write the criteria in one or two lines. After you decide, write the reason in one line. Run the swap test on it before you announce it.

## Lesson 1: The swap test

**Summary:** Fair decisions are easier to test than fair intentions. This lesson teaches a four-step test built on mirror-image pairs, and why the law now makes it the accurate way to practice.
**Minutes:** 12
**Objectives:**
- Explain what *Ames* and *Muldrow* mean for routine assignments.
- Apply the four steps of the swap test.
- Identify client or community preference as a weak reason for an assignment.
- Restate a preference as a work criterion, or recognize that you cannot.

**Takeaways:**
- One standard applies to everyone, in every direction.
- Swap the people, keep the facts, and see if your reason survives.
- "They'll relate better" is an assumption until you can state a skill.

### Text: Why practice in pairs

Most supervisors do not set out to treat people differently because of race, sex, disability, religion or national origin. The trouble usually lies in a reason that sounds sensible for one person and would sound wrong for another. You can only hear that when you try it the other way around.

The law also supports practicing in both directions. In *Ames v. Ohio Department of Youth Services* (2025), the U.S. Supreme Court unanimously held that majority-group employees do not have to meet a higher bar under Title VII. In *Muldrow v. City of St. Louis* (2024), the Court held that someone challenging a transfer must show "some harm" to a term or condition of employment, and it need not be significant. The EEOC's March 2025 technical assistance, an agency position and not law, treats mentoring, sponsorship and training access as terms or privileges of employment.

So work assignments, schedule changes, stretch work and nominations can all matter legally, for any employee, if a protected trait motivated the decision. As of October 2026, the Eighth Circuit's application of these cases to public employers has not been confirmed in the research for this course. Consistent criteria and a short record are the sensible practice in any case.

This does not make equity irrelevant. The swap test is an equity tool. Unstated criteria tend to favor whoever is already known, already visible, and already like the person deciding. Stated criteria open that door to everyone.

### List: The swap test

1. **State the criteria.** Write what the work needs before you think about who. Use one or two lines.
2. **Apply them.** Choose the person, and write the reason using those criteria.
3. **Swap the people.** Keep every fact about the work and the record the same. Change the person's race, sex, age, religion, disability, national origin or family situation.
4. **Listen to your reason.** Would you still say it? Would you be comfortable saying it to the person who did not get the work? If not, the reason was not about the work.

### Tabs: Mirror pair 1: Who fronts the outreach

**Tab: Version A**
A community outreach lead is needed for a waiver information session at a Somali community center. A supervisor passes over an experienced white consultant, saying, "The community will relate better to our Somali American consultant." No one has checked who speaks Somali, or who knows the waiver best.

**Tab: Version B**
A lead is needed for a legislative briefing. A supervisor passes over an experienced Somali American consultant, saying, "Legislators will relate better to our white consultant." No one has checked who knows the bill best.

**Tab: What the pair shows**
Version B sounds wrong immediately. Version A uses the same reasoning. Each assigns work by assumption about race or national origin and audience preference. The EEOC says client or customer preference is no defense to race or color discrimination, and that there is no "diversity interest" exception.

**Tab: The work-based version**
Ask what the session needs. If it needs someone who can present in Somali, say so. Fluency is a skill you can state and check, and anyone who has it qualifies. If it needs an interpreter and someone with deep waiver knowledge, book the interpreter and choose by waiver knowledge. Do not assume someone speaks a language because of their name or background. Do not presume to know what a community wants. Ask community partners about the session's needs, and keep the choice of staff on work criteria.

### Statement

Criteria first. Names second. Then swap the names and see if your reason still stands.

### Leader move: Hearing your own reason

**Control:** You control whether you say your reason out loud to yourself before you decide.
**Failure:** Do not use "fit," "relate," "comfortable" or "natural" as your reason without saying what they mean for the work.
**Next:** The next time one of those words comes to mind, finish this sentence: "The work needs someone who can ___." If you cannot finish it, you do not have a criterion yet.

### Scenario

**Context:**
**Version A:** You need someone to lead visits to a group home whose residents include several older women who need personal care. A male case consultant has the most experience with this provider. A coworker says, "Families will be more comfortable with a woman," and you are inclined to assign a female consultant with less experience.
**Version B:** You need someone to lead a tense meeting with a provider's board. A female case consultant has the most experience with this provider. A coworker says, "That board will take a man more seriously," and you are inclined to assign a male consultant with less experience.
**Prompt:** What should guide you in both versions?

**Option:** Follow the coworker's advice in each version. They know these audiences, and your first duty is a smooth meeting.
**Response:** This uses audience preference as the reason both times, and it costs the most experienced person the work because of sex. The swap makes the problem visible. Version B would be hard to defend out loud, and Version A has the same structure.

**Option:** Keep the assignment in Version B with the experienced woman, but use the less experienced woman in Version A, since personal care is different.
**Response:** You may be noticing a real difference. A person's privacy in personal care can be a real concern, but the assignment here is a consultant's visit, and you would need to establish what the work involves. Do not decide this on assumption. Ask what the visit requires, and ask Human Resources if a sex-based limit is being proposed.

**Option (recommended):** State what the work needs in each case, assign by experience and skill with this provider, and address any real need directly. For example, ask what the visit involves and what the people being served want for their own care, and back the consultant in the board meeting.
**Response:** Your reasons now survive the swap. You also deal with the real concerns: the privacy and choices of the person receiving services, and the board's conduct. Neither one has to become a reason to sideline a staff member by sex.

### Knowledge check

**Question:** According to the EEOC's March 2025 technical assistance, what is the role of client or customer preference in decisions about race or color?
**Options:**
- It is a defense if the client's preference is well documented.
- It is no defense to race or color discrimination. (correct)
- It applies only to private employers.
- It is allowed for public agencies with diversity goals.

**Correct answer:** It is no defense to race or color discrimination.
**Feedback if correct:** Correct. This is an agency position and not itself law, but it is the current position posted by the agency that enforces Title VII. It is a reason to base assignments on stated skills.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Look again. The EEOC says client or customer preference is no defense, and it says there is no "diversity interest" exception. Base the assignment on stated skills.

### Transfer prompt

**Prompt:** Pick a real assignment coming up. "If I notice myself thinking someone would 'relate better' or 'be more comfortable', then I will write the work need in one line, run the swap test, and check [a skill I can verify]."
**Plan starters:**
- Which upcoming assignment involves a community or an audience?
- What skill does it really need?
- How will you check that skill for everyone, instead of assuming?

## Lesson 2: Stretch work and invisible work

**Summary:** Some work builds careers, and other work just keeps things running. This lesson uses mirror pairs to test who gets each kind, and what you assume when you decide for someone.
**Minutes:** 12
**Objectives:**
- Separate visible work from invisible work on your own team.
- Test assumptions about family, age or capacity with a mirror pair.
- Offer opportunities without deciding for people in advance.
- Rotate routine work by method.

**Takeaways:**
- Offer the opportunity. Let the person decide what they can take on.
- Name the invisible work, and rotate it.
- Do not assign equity work to people because of who they are.

### Text: Two ledgers

Every team keeps two ledgers, even if nobody writes them down. One records visible work: the legislative briefing, the director update, the cross-agency group. The other records work that keeps things running: taking notes, onboarding the new hire, fixing the shared drive, hosting the potluck, answering everyone's questions about a culture or a language.

Stretch work has real effects. Formal mentoring and cross-training are associated with gains in management diversity in corporate studies, though these are associations and not proof. In one financial firm, more face-to-face time with managers raised promotion rates. Visibility is one of the ways careers move, so it deserves a method.

### Tabs: Mirror pair 2: Deciding for someone

**Tab: Version A**
A week of site visits in greater Minnesota is coming up, and it will be good experience for promotion. A supervisor does not offer it to a consultant who is a mother of young children, thinking, "She's got her hands full." The supervisor offers it to a consultant with no children at home.

**Tab: Version B**
The same trip is coming up. A supervisor does not offer it to a consultant who is a father of young children, thinking, "He'll want to be home." Or the supervisor assumes the father's spouse handles home, and offers it to him but not to the mother.

**Tab: What the pair shows**
The supervisor decided for the person, based on an assumption about family. Try another swap, with a 62-year-old consultant, on the assumption "they're winding down." Or a consultant who uses a wheelchair, on the assumption "travel would be hard." Each time, the person loses the chance to say yes.

**Tab: The work-based version**
Offer the trip to everyone who meets the criteria, using the same message. State what it involves. If a person needs an accommodation to travel, that goes through the process. It is not a reason to skip them. Let people decline for themselves, and do not ask them to explain.

### Tabs: Mirror pair 3: Who keeps the notes

**Tab: Version A**
A supervisor regularly asks a woman on the team to take notes and order lunch, saying, "You're so organized."

**Tab: Version B**
A supervisor regularly asks a man on the team to carry equipment and fix the projector, saying, "You're good with this stuff." He has started missing the first part of each meeting.

**Tab: What the pair shows**
Both are compliments with a cost. Each person loses a share of the meeting and gets little credit. The trait behind the assumption can be different in each case, and the method for fixing both is the same.

**Tab: The work-based version**
List the routine tasks and rotate them on a schedule anyone can see. Then swap once more: if someone asks to be excused from notes because of a disability, that is a possible accommodation request. It is not a rotation failure.

### Tabs: Mirror pair 4: The equity task

**Tab: Version A**
A supervisor asks the only Black consultant on the team to lead the team's discussion on racial disparities in waiver access, on top of a full caseload, because "you'd bring so much to it."

**Tab: Version B**
A supervisor asks the only white consultant to lead the same discussion, on top of a full caseload, because "people need to hear this from someone like you."

**Tab: What the pair shows**
Each version assigns work based on the person's race. It also assumes that one person can represent a group or has a duty to lead this work. The work may be valuable, but the reason is wrong both times.

**Tab: The work-based version**
Define the task and the skills it needs. Open it to everyone, count it as real work, and take something else off the person's plate. The course Who Carries the Equity Work goes further.

### Leader move: The open offer

**Control:** You control whether an opportunity reaches everyone who qualifies.
**Failure:** Do not tap someone in the hallway because they came to mind first. Do not screen people out on their behalf.
**Next:** For your next stretch assignment, send one message to everyone eligible. Say what it involves, what skills it needs and how you will choose.

### Scenario

**Context:**
**Version A:** You need someone for a high-profile project with the director's office. Your first thought is a white male policy analyst who speaks up in every meeting. A Hmong American woman on the team has written the analysis that drove the last two decisions. She rarely speaks in large meetings, and you have heard her called "quiet."
**Version B:** You need someone for the same project. Your first thought is a Hmong American woman who speaks up in every meeting. A white male analyst on the team has written the analysis that drove the last two decisions. He rarely speaks in large meetings, and you have heard him called "quiet."
**Prompt:** What approach holds up in both versions?

**Option:** Choose the person who speaks up, in both versions. The director's office needs a confident presenter.
**Response:** This at least gives the same answer both ways. But you have not checked whether presenting is the main skill, or considered the written analysis. A stated criterion might support this choice, but your first impression should not stand in for one.

**Option:** Choose the quiet analyst in Version A, to correct for who usually gets noticed, and the speaker in Version B.
**Response:** This changes the outcome by the person's race and sex, and that fails the swap test. Noticing an uneven pattern is useful, but the fix is a better method and not a different standard for different people.

**Option (recommended):** Write what the project needs first, such as analysis, drafting and presenting, and weigh each. Tell everyone eligible about it and how you will choose. Decide on the record of work, and note the reason.
**Response:** This surfaces work that a "who speaks up" habit would miss, in either version. If presenting matters, you can say how much and check it. The decision holds up when the people are swapped.

**Option:** Give it to whoever asks first, so that you stay out of it.
**Response:** Staying out of it is still a decision. "First to ask" rewards people who already hear about opportunities and feel comfortable asking.

### Knowledge check

**Question:** You think a consultant probably will not want a travel assignment because of family responsibilities. What should you do?
**Options:**
- Skip them, to spare them having to say no.
- Ask them privately about their childcare before deciding.
- Offer it using the same message you give everyone, and let them decide. (correct)
- Offer it only if no one else takes it.

**Correct answer:** Offer it using the same message you give everyone, and let them decide.
**Feedback if correct:** Yes. You keep the chance open and stay out of their family life. If they need an accommodation, that has its own route.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Not quite. Skipping them or asking about childcare puts an assumption about family in the place of a work criterion. Offer it to everyone in the same way.

### Transfer prompt

**Prompt:** Think of the next opportunity you control. "If a stretch assignment comes up, then I will send [the same message] to [everyone eligible] before I think about who should get it. And by [date], I will have put [one routine task] on a visible rotation."
**Plan starters:**
- List the routine tasks on your team, and who has done them for the last three months.
- Draft your standard opportunity message.
- Name one person's invisible work you should credit in your next update.

## Lesson 3: Schedules, telework and flexibility

**Summary:** Schedules and telework affect daily life, so they are easily decided by sympathy and familiarity. This lesson tests those decisions, and shows why applying the same criteria does not always produce the same outcome.
**Minutes:** 13
**Objectives:**
- Apply the same criteria to flexible work requests, whatever the personal reason.
- Find the notice and appeal terms that apply under the MAPE contract.
- Explain why an accommodation can lawfully differ from what others receive.
- Respond to a coworker's "why do they get that?" without revealing anything private.

**Takeaways:**
- Judge the request against the work. Do not rank people's reasons.
- Same criteria does not always mean same outcome.
- Answer fairness questions without exposing anyone.

### Text: Do not rank people's reasons

Many flexible work requests come with a story: a child, a parent, a class, a commute, a congregation. It is tempting to weigh the stories, but you are poorly placed to judge which life deserves more, and ranking them invites you to favor lives that resemble yours.

Start with the work. What coverage does the team need? Which duties can be done remotely and which cannot? What response time do people rely on? Those criteria can then apply to anyone.

Under the MAPE 2025-2027 contract:

- **Telework (Article 27, Section 1(I)).** Where the employer has determined an employee can satisfactorily meet business needs and job expectations while teleworking, it "shall consider" the request. If a request is denied, the employee can ask for the reasons. The employer first meets with the employee before a supervisor or manager changes or denies an arrangement. When practicable, it gives 21 days' notice before changing or cancelling one. The employee may appeal within 30 days. A union representative may attend if the employee asks. This section is not subject to the grievance procedure.
- **Flex-time (Article 27, Section 1(B)).** If a request for a flex-time plan is denied, the employee can ask for the reasons.
- **Permanent hours change (Article 27, Section 1(A)).** At least 14 calendar days' notice, unless mutually waived.

Other agreements may differ, so check the one that covers each person. And check DHS's current telework policy with Human Resources.

### Tabs: Mirror pair 5: Telework reasons

**Tab: Version A**
Two policy specialists do the same work, with the same results. One asks for three telework days to care for a parent, and the supervisor agrees, saying, "Family comes first." The other, a younger employee with no children, asks for the same three days to attend evening graduate classes after a long commute. The supervisor refuses, saying, "We can't have everyone gone."

**Tab: Version B**
Now swap. The younger employee caring for a parent gets the same reaction, "You have your whole career ahead, you'll figure it out." The older employee in graduate school gets approved, "Good for you, going back at this age."

**Tab: What the pair shows**
The work did not change in either version. The supervisor's sympathy did. When the outcome depends on whose story you warm to, you do not yet have criteria.

**Tab: The work-based version**
Set the coverage and duty criteria for the team. Test each request against them. If both fit, approve both. If they cannot both fit, use a stated method, such as rotating days. Write the reason in work terms. A request connected to a medical condition or a religious practice goes down its own route, as the next pair shows.

### Tabs: Mirror pair 6: Faith and time

**Tab: Version A**
A Muslim consultant asks to adjust their lunch and rest time on Fridays for prayer. The supervisor says, "We can't make exceptions for religion."

**Tab: Version B**
A Christian consultant asks to not work a Sunday outreach event. The supervisor swaps them out without comment, saying, "Of course, it's Sunday."

**Tab: What the pair shows**
One faith's practice feels like the default, and the other's feels like an exception. The same process applies to both.

**Tab: The work-based version**
Treat both as possible religious accommodation requests. Asking for one is protected activity, according to the EEOC. Send them promptly to Human Resources, and do not judge how serious or real the belief is. Your practical test is the work: what coverage is needed, and what can be adjusted?

### Tabs: Mirror pair 7: Same criteria, different outcome

**Tab: Version A**
A case consultant has an approved accommodation for a later start because of a medical treatment. A coworker asks for the same later start because it suits their commute. The supervisor says yes, "to be fair." Now morning coverage has a gap.

**Tab: Version B**
The same coworker asks. The supervisor says no, and adds, "Their situation is medical, and yours isn't."

**Tab: What the pair shows**
Version A treats fairness as sameness and hurts the work for no reason. Version B answers correctly in outcome but discloses something private. Neither is the right approach.

**Tab: The work-based version**
An accommodation is an individual duty. It is not a perk to be shared out. Assess the coworker's request against the team's regular criteria for coverage, as you would anyone's. If it cannot work, say why in terms of coverage. Say nothing about anyone else: "I handle individual arrangements privately, for everyone. Let's look at your request and what coverage the team needs."

### Statement

The same criteria apply to everyone. An accommodation adds a legal duty on top, and that can produce a different result. It never gives you a reason to expose it.

### Leader move: Answering the fairness question

**Control:** You control whether the question "Why do they get that?" turns into a discussion of someone else's life.
**Failure:** Do not defend someone's arrangement by explaining its reason. Do not hint.
**Next:** Prepare one calm sentence that states how you handle arrangements for everyone. Then turn to the person's own question.

### Scenario

**Context:**
**Version A:** A white consultant who has been on your team for ten years, and whom you trust, works from home four days a week under an informal understanding. A newer Black consultant with the same duties and a strong record asks for the same arrangement. Your first reaction is, "You need more time in the office to prove yourself."
**Version B:** A Black consultant who has been on your team for ten years, and whom you trust, works from home four days a week under an informal understanding. A newer white consultant with the same duties and a strong record asks for the same arrangement. Your first reaction is the same.
**Prompt:** What do you do next, in either version?

**Option:** Deny the request. The difference is tenure, not race, and tenure is a legitimate factor.
**Response:** Tenure might be a legitimate factor, but you have not stated it as a criterion, and you do not know what work need it serves. Under the MAPE contract the employee may ask for the reasons, and the employer meets with them first. "Prove yourself" will be hard to explain.

**Option (recommended):** Write the team's telework criteria based on duties, results and coverage. Apply them to this request and to the long-standing arrangement. Check with Human Resources and your contract before you change anything. Meet with the employee and give the reason in work terms.
**Response:** Your decision now holds up after the swap. It also deals with the real issue: an informal arrangement nobody can explain. If the criteria do favor experience for a specific reason, such as independent decisions in the work, you can say so and show how someone earns it.

**Option:** Approve the request, because refusing could look discriminatory.
**Response:** Deciding from fear of how it looks is still not a criterion. It may also be the right outcome, but you would not be able to explain why, and the next request will raise the same problem.

**Option:** Revoke the long-standing arrangement today, so that everyone is treated the same.
**Response:** Abrupt removal can harm someone who has done nothing wrong, and it could cut across an accommodation you do not know about or the contract's notice and meeting steps. Set the criteria first, then act.

### Knowledge check

**Question:** A coworker asks why a teammate has a later start. The teammate's later start is an approved accommodation. What is the best response?
**Options:**
- "It's medical, so I can't change it."
- "I handle individual arrangements privately, for everyone. Would you like to talk about your own schedule?" (correct)
- "Ask them. It's their story to tell."
- "Everyone can have one now, so it's fair."

**Correct answer:** "I handle individual arrangements privately, for everyone. Would you like to talk about your own schedule?"
**Feedback if correct:** Yes. You reveal nothing, and you keep the door open to the person's own request, which you assess against the team's criteria.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Look again. Saying "medical" discloses private information, and sending the coworker to the teammate puts that person on the spot. An accommodation does not have to be extended to everyone to be fair.

### Transfer prompt

**Prompt:** Think of a flexible work request you may receive or have received. "If someone asks to change where or when they work, then I will test it against [criteria], leave their reasons out of my comparison, and send anything medical or religious to [contact]."
**Plan starters:**
- Write your team's coverage needs in three lines.
- List informal arrangements that you could not explain to a new hire.
- Look up the telework and scheduling terms in each contract your team uses.

## Lesson 4: Caseloads and reassignments

**Summary:** Moving someone's work can matter even when pay and title stay the same. This lesson tests caseload matching and reassignments, including moves that happen after someone speaks up.
**Minutes:** 12
**Objectives:**
- Explain why reassignment needs a work reason under *Muldrow*.
- Separate a verifiable skill from assumed cultural match in caseload decisions.
- Use the MAPE factors for permanent reassignment.
- Spot a reassignment that may look like retaliation.

**Takeaways:**
- A skill can be a criterion. An assumption about identity cannot.
- Ask people what they want before you match them to anything.
- Check timing after anyone has made a report or a request.

### Text: Matching is not the same as sorting

In disability services, people often do better when staff understand their language and community. Language access and cultural responsiveness matter. A supervisor can and should plan for these needs.

The trouble starts when a supervisor assigns staff by their race or national origin, on the theory that it serves clients better. The EEOC says client preference is no defense to race or color discrimination. The research on same-group matching is real in some settings, such as better outcomes for employees under same-race managers in one retail firm, but that does not become an assignment rule for your team.

The answer is to plan around needs you can state, and let anyone with the skill do the work:

- People using services need information in a language they understand. So plan for qualified interpreters, translated materials and staff with demonstrated language skills.
- A person using services may have preferences about their own support. Listen to them and raise them with your manager and Human Resources if meeting one would mean assigning staff by a protected trait.
- A consultant may have deep experience with a community, built through their own work. That is a skill you can describe, and it could belong to anyone who did that work.

### Tabs: Mirror pair 8: Caseload by neighborhood

**Tab: Version A**
A supervisor assigns a Black consultant all providers on Minneapolis's north side, saying, "You'd connect." The consultant's caseload now has longer travel and more crisis calls. Nobody asked the consultant.

**Tab: Version B**
A supervisor assigns a white consultant all the providers in rural counties, saying, "You'd connect." The consultant's caseload now has longer travel and fewer promotion-track projects. Nobody asked the consultant.

**Tab: What the pair shows**
Each version sorts people by race and an assumption about who fits where, and each may change the person's duties, travel and prospects. *Muldrow* says the harm need not be significant.

**Tab: The work-based version**
Set out what caseload balance needs, such as travel, complexity, licensing knowledge, language needs and development goals. Ask each person what they want. Assign against those factors, and review the balance periodically.

### Tabs: Mirror pair 9: The language skill

**Tab: Version A**
A consultant who is fluent in Spanish gets every Spanish-speaking family, on top of a full caseload, with no credit. "You're so good with them."

**Tab: Version B**
A consultant who took Spanish in college and has kept it up says they can help with Spanish-speaking families. The supervisor says, "Let's leave that to the Latina consultant. She's better for them."

**Tab: What the pair shows**
A skill is being used as if it belonged to an identity. In Version A, the skill brings extra unpaid work. In Version B, someone's offer gets dismissed. Both versions skip checking the skill level, and both skip thinking about the load.

**Tab: The work-based version**
State the language need and the level it requires. Ask Human Resources how language skills are verified and whether pay or classification terms apply. Count that work when you weigh caseloads. Use qualified interpreters where staff skills are not the right answer.

### Text: The MAPE factors for a permanent reassignment

Under Article 16, Section 2 of the MAPE 2025-2027 contract, when the employer decides on a permanent reassignment within 35 miles, it must consider, but is not limited to, the employee's ability to perform the job, qualifications, interest in the job, current workload, and classification or class option seniority. That is a good list to borrow when you plan any significant move. Check with labor relations before you make one, and check other agreements directly.

### Leader move: The move after the report

**Control:** You control whether an already-planned change goes ahead automatically after someone has made a report or a request.
**Failure:** Do not assume that because a plan predates a complaint, its timing cannot look like retaliation.
**Next:** Before any move affecting someone who has recently reported a problem, joined an investigation or asked for an accommodation, call Human Resources. Bring the original plan, and the date it was made.

### Scenario

**Context:**
**Version A:** You are rebalancing caseloads. A woman on your team reported last month that a male colleague's remarks had made her uncomfortable. Your rebalancing plan, drafted before her report, moves her to a less sought-after county. The colleague stays where he is.
**Version B:** You are rebalancing caseloads. A man on your team reported last month that a female colleague's remarks about men had made him uncomfortable. Your rebalancing plan, drafted before his report, moves him to a less sought-after county. The colleague stays where she is.
**Prompt:** What do you do in both versions?

**Option:** Go ahead. The plan came first, and you can show the date.
**Response:** The date helps, and you should keep it. But it does not end the question. A less desirable transfer is one of the EEOC's examples of retaliation. Check before you act.

**Option:** Treat Version A as a real concern, and Version B as probably fine, since a man reporting remarks about men is not the same kind of problem.
**Response:** This is what the swap test is for. Reports get the same treatment, whichever direction they come from. *Ames* sets one standard, and Policy 1436 tells supervisors to take reports seriously.

**Option (recommended):** Hold the move for this person in both versions. Bring the plan, its date and its reasons to Human Resources. Ask whether the plan should change, or whether another way of balancing the work would serve as well.
**Response:** This protects the person who spoke up, and it tests your plan. It may still go ahead, with a reason you can show. The answer does not depend on who reported whom.

**Option:** Move the colleague instead, as a consequence of the remarks.
**Response:** You would be imposing what looks like discipline before anyone has reviewed the facts, and possibly without the contract steps. Leave that to the process.

### Knowledge check

**Question:** Which of these is a work-based criterion for assigning a caseload?
**Options:**
- The consultant shares a background with most of the families.
- The consultant has demonstrated fluency in the language the families use. (correct)
- The consultant seems like they would connect with that community.
- The community would probably prefer this consultant.

**Correct answer:** The consultant has demonstrated fluency in the language the families use.
**Feedback if correct:** Correct. Fluency is a skill anyone can have, and you can state and check it. Remember to count it as work.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Look again. The other answers rest on identity or assumed preference. A demonstrated skill can apply to anyone.

### Transfer prompt

**Prompt:** Think about the next time you rebalance work. "If I plan to move someone's caseload or duties, then I will list [the factors], ask the person what they want, and check [recent reports or requests] with Human Resources before I act."
**Plan starters:**
- Which factors matter most for caseload balance on your team?
- Where does language skill work go uncounted?
- Whom do you call about a reassignment?

## Lesson 5: Keeping a record, and reviewing your own process

**Summary:** The swap test works only if you can see your decisions. This lesson teaches a one-line record and a private monthly review of process, and how to respond to a pattern without changing the standard for anyone.
**Minutes:** 11
**Objectives:**
- Keep a one-line record for significant assignments.
- Review your own process: criteria, record and second look.
- Respond to an uneven pattern by fixing the method.
- Avoid recording identity or personal information in your log.

**Takeaways:**
- One line is enough if it states the work reason.
- A pattern is a reason to examine your process. It does not settle who gets the next assignment.
- Keep personal traits out of the log.

### Text: The one-line record

A contemporaneous note is short. It says what the work was, who got it, the criteria and the reason.

> Q1 legislative briefing. Criteria: knows the bill, can brief in 10 minutes, has capacity. Assigned to the policy analyst who drafted the bill language. Offered to all four analysts on 1/12.

Now consider what is not in it: nothing about the person's personality, health, family, race or anything else about who they are.

Keep the record where Human Resources tells you to. Notes that you write can be government data, and personnel data other than certain public items is private. Treat them accordingly. This is also not a list for the app. Do not type names here.

### List: Your monthly process check

Spend ten minutes once a month. Answer these three questions for each significant assignment, schedule decision or reassignment:

1. Were the criteria stated before names came up?
2. Is there a record of the reason?
3. Was it offered openly, or did someone get a second look?

Then look across the list. Who keeps getting the visible work? Who keeps getting the routine work? Whose one-to-ones get moved? This check is about your own process, it stays private, and it holds no demographic data. You do not need to label anyone to notice that the same two people are doing all the briefings.

### Tabs: Mirror pair 10: The pattern

**Tab: Version A**
A supervisor looks over the log. The last four stretch assignments went to white employees. The supervisor thinks, "The next one should go to an employee of color, to even things out."

**Tab: Version B**
A supervisor looks over the log. After a push from a director, the last four stretch assignments went to employees of color. The supervisor thinks, "The next one should go to a white employee, to even things out."

**Tab: What the pair shows**
In both versions, the supervisor is about to choose the next person by race. The pattern is worth taking seriously, and the proposed fix is the wrong one. The EEOC says there is no "diversity interest" exception, and *Ames* applies the same standard to each direction.

**Tab: The work-based version**
Treat the pattern as a prompt. Look at how the assignments were offered. Did everyone hear about them? Were the criteria tied to the work, or to experience that only some people had the chance to get? Did anyone ever get to build that experience? Fix those things, then apply the criteria to everyone. That is what can actually change who has a shot, in either version.

### Text: Honest limits

Good process does not make you or your team fair for good. In lab experiments, the presence of diversity structures led people in high-status groups to see organizations as fairer, even with clear evidence of unfairness. In other lab experiments, people with managerial experience favored men in bonuses when a company was presented as meritocratic. So do not treat your log as proof of anything. Use it to ask better questions.

Rules also have limits. A long-running study of U.S. workplaces concluded that some reforms limiting discretion can activate the problem they target. One plausible reason, not a tested one, is that rules imposed without engaging managers provoke workarounds. So explain your criteria to your team, ask whether they are the right ones, and change them when someone shows you a better way.

### Leader move: The second look

**Control:** You control whether anyone else sees your reasoning before a significant decision.
**Failure:** Do not ask for a second look only when you are already worried. Do not ask for one from someone who always agrees with you.
**Next:** Ask a peer or your manager to look at one set of criteria before you use it. Ask only, "What does this leave out?" Do not include people's names.

### Scenario

**Context:**
**Version A:** Your monthly check shows that three people have had nearly all the development assignments this year. They are also the three who have worked with you longest. Two newer staff, both immigrants to the U.S., have had none. A team member says, "People have noticed."
**Version B:** Your monthly check shows that three people have had nearly all the development assignments this year. They are also the three who have worked with you longest, and they are all immigrants to the U.S. Two newer staff, both born in the U.S., have had none. A team member says, "People have noticed."
**Prompt:** What is your next decision in either version?

**Option:** Give the next two assignments to the two newer staff, and tell them you are making up for the imbalance.
**Response:** This addresses the visible imbalance but skips the criteria, and it may mark those staff as being chosen for reasons other than their work. Use a method that gets people ready and gives them a real chance.

**Option:** Explain to the team member that everyone was chosen on merit, and move on.
**Response:** You have not checked this, and your log suggests familiarity played a part. A flat "merit" answer may also close the door on someone raising a real concern. If they are raising discrimination, your reporting duty may apply.

**Option (recommended):** Look at how those assignments were offered and what experience they required. Set up an open offer and criteria for the next round. Talk with each newer person about development goals, and listen to the team member. If they raise discrimination, report it.
**Response:** You fix the method and the same steps apply in both versions. Development conversations give newer staff real preparation, and the assignment itself still goes by the criteria.

### Knowledge check

**Question:** Your review shows an uneven pattern in who gets visible work. What is the best use of that finding?
**Options:**
- Choose the next person by the trait that is underrepresented.
- Review how work was offered and what criteria were used, then fix the method. (correct)
- Add each person's race and sex to your log, so you can track it.
- Do nothing, since a pattern on a small team means nothing.

**Correct answer:** Review how work was offered and what criteria were used, then fix the method.
**Feedback if correct:** Yes. A pattern prompts a review of process. It does not justify a trait-based choice for an individual.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Look again. Small patterns can mislead, but they are still worth a look. Do not choose by trait, and do not add demographic details to your personal log. Review the method.

### Transfer prompt

**Prompt:** Set your own check. "On [day each month], I will spend ten minutes on my assignment log. If I see the same people getting the visible work, then I will change [how I offer it] before the next assignment."
**Plan starters:**
- Ask Human Resources where your log should be kept.
- Start the log with last month's decisions.
- Pick someone to give you a second look on one set of criteria.

## Job aid

**Title:** The swap test
**Subtitle:** Use it for assignments, schedules, telework, reassignments and nominations.

**Purpose:** To check that a decision about someone's work rests on criteria that would hold for anyone.

**Remember:**
- One standard applies to all employees, in every direction.
- A small harm can count, even if pay and title do not change.
- Same criteria does not always mean same outcome. An accommodation is an added duty, and it stays private.
- A pattern prompts a review of process. Do not choose an individual by a trait to balance it.

**Section: Before you decide**
- Write what the work needs, in one or two lines, before you think of names.
- Offer it openly, using the same message for everyone eligible.
- State language or other skills as skills you can check.

**Section: The test**
- Swap the person's race, sex, age, religion, disability, national origin or family situation.
- Keep the work and the record the same.
- Would you still say your reason? Would you say it to the person who did not get the work?
- Watch for these words: fit, relate, comfortable, natural, "hands full," "connect," "prove yourself."

**Section: Before you act**
- Has this person recently made a report, joined an investigation or asked for an accommodation? Call Human Resources first.
- Is this a permanent hours change, reassignment, telework change or new rule? Check the agreement, and call labor relations.
- MAPE: Article 16, Section 2 for reassignment factors. Article 27 for hours and telework. Article 28 for work rules. Check other agreements directly.

**Section: After you decide**
- Write one line: the work, the criteria, the person and the reason. Leave out personal traits.
- Once a month, check three things: criteria first, record kept, open offer or second look.

**Do next:** Use this page on your next assignment, and write the one-line record the same day.
