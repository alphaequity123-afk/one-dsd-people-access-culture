# sm-named-in-complaint: When You Are Named in a Complaint

**Subtitle:** What to do on the first day, how to keep supervising fairly while a review runs, how to take part honestly, and how to go on working after the outcome.
**Duration:** About 62 minutes
**Content type:** formal-support
**Status:** reviewed
**Last reviewed:** October 31, 2026
**Evidence date:** October 2026
**Scope:** For supervisors and managers who have been named, or who want to be ready. This is general information, not legal advice. It is voluntary, and it does not investigate anything, decide anything or stand in for your own contract, your representative or the agency's process.

**Course objectives:**
- Take the right steps in the first day after you learn you are named, and avoid the moves that do the most damage.
- Keep supervising the whole team while a review runs, with a second look on decisions that could discourage someone from raising a concern.
- Take part in a review honestly, keep records intact, and find out what rights your own contract or plan gives you.
- Find support that does not draw your team into the matter.
- Go on working with everyone after any outcome, including one you think is wrong.

**Applied next step:** Before you need it, write down three things and keep them where you can find them: the Human Resources contact you would call, the contract or plan that covers your own job, and the person who would represent or advise you.

## Lesson 1: The first day

**Summary:** Most lasting mistakes happen in the first hours, and they are usually made by people trying to clear their name or fix things fast. This lesson gives you the order of steps.
**Minutes:** 13

**Objectives:**
- Separate what a complaint is from what a finding is.
- Follow the first-day steps in order.
- Respond properly when an employee tells you directly that the complaint is about you.
- Name the moves that cannot be taken back.

**Takeaways:**
- A complaint is a claim. Treat it seriously without treating it as a verdict.
- Call before you talk to anyone on your team.
- Keep every record. Delete nothing.
- If someone tells you directly, you still report it. You do not handle it.

### Text: How you find out
You might hear from Human Resources or an investigator. Your manager might call you. A staff member might say it to your face. Or it might reach you as a rumor, with no one official saying anything.

However it arrives, you will probably react before you can think. Anger, fear and shame are all common, and all of them push you to do something right away. You may want to explain yourself, find out who complained, or smooth things over. Those urges feel like good leadership. They are the source of most of the harm this lesson tries to prevent.

You supervise people who have the right to raise concerns, including concerns about you. Holding that fact steady for the first day is most of the job.

### Statement
A complaint is not a finding. It is also not nothing. Act as if both of those are true.

### List: Your first-day steps, in order
1. Stop. Do not reply to anyone on the team about this. Do not send a message you have been drafting in your head.
2. Write a short private note: what you were told, who told you, and when. Facts only. Leave out your theory about why.
3. Call Human Resources, or the person who contacted you. Ask the questions in the next block.
4. Keep everything. Messages, files, drafts and notes all stay as they are. Records on state systems are government data, and tidying them can look far worse than anything they contain.
5. Find the contract or plan that covers your own job, and find out who can advise or represent you. Your staff's contract may not be yours.
6. Then go back to work, and do the next ordinary thing in the ordinary way.

### List: What to ask on that first call
- Who is handling this, and who do I contact from now on?
- What can you tell me now, and when will I hear more?
- Is there anything I should stop doing, or do differently, while this runs?
- Who should review my decisions about the team for now?
- What may I say if my team or my manager asks?
- Who can I go to about my own rights? Is that a different person from you?

### Text: If a staff member tells you directly
Sometimes the first you hear is an employee saying, "I think what you did was discrimination," or "I am going to report you."

Minnesota Management and Budget Policies 1436 and 1329, as read in October 2026, say managers and supervisors must treat reports seriously, respond when they become aware of a problem, and immediately report to Human Resources or the Affirmative Action Officer. Nothing we read carves out a report about you. So treat the duty as applying, and if you have any doubt, ask Human Resources the same day.

Say something like this: "Thank you for telling me. I have to pass this to Human Resources, and I should not be the one who handles it. You can also go to them directly. Nothing about your work changes because you raised this." Then stop talking. Do not argue the facts or explain your intent, and do not ask for details. Make the report that day.

Reporting on yourself feels strange. It protects the employee from a stalled concern, and it protects you from the charge that you sat on it.

If the comment is about harm with no connection to a protected class, the course "Responding when someone says that was harmful" (dc-l76) covers how to receive it. If you cannot tell which it is, call. That is not your call to make alone.

### Accordion: Moves that are hard to take back
#### Item: Trying to work out who complained
You will want to know. Do not look for it, ask about it or test your guesses. If you guess, you may start treating that person differently, and you may be wrong.

#### Item: Asking the team what people are saying
Even a trusted senior employee is put in an impossible spot. They may become a witness, and they now know you are looking. Your question can read as pressure.

#### Item: Reaching out to "clear the air"
A private talk with the person you think complained can feel like repair. It can also feel like a supervisor leaning on someone to withdraw.

#### Item: A general apology to the team
"If I have upset anyone" tells everyone that something is going on, and it invites them to guess. It also speaks about facts no one has reviewed.

#### Item: Starting a file on someone
Collecting notes on one employee after you learn of a complaint looks like what it often is. Keep your records the way you kept them before, for everyone.

### Leader move: The first call
**Control:** You control the order: note, call, keep records, then work. You also control whether your team sees an ordinary day.
**Failure:** Do not let the need to be understood run ahead of the process. A good explanation given to the wrong person at the wrong time can do lasting damage.
**Next:** Save the Human Resources contact in your phone today, before anything has happened.

### Scenario
**Context:** You supervise a team of case consultants. Late in the day your manager calls. Human Resources will contact you about a complaint concerning how you have assigned waiver case reviews. Your manager does not know who filed it, or says so. The next morning you have a team meeting, and two consultants stop talking when you walk into the room.
**Prompt:** What do you do before the meeting starts?

**Option 1:** Open the meeting by saying you have heard there is a complaint, and that your door is open to anyone who wants to talk.
**Response:** This tells the team that something is happening and asks them to come to you about it. An open door sounds generous, but from the person who is named it can feel like an invitation to withdraw or explain. Wait until you have asked Human Resources what you may say.

**Option 2:** Write your private note, call Human Resources first thing and ask who should look at assignments for now, then run the meeting as planned without mentioning it.
**Response:** This is the best next step. You have a record of what you were told, you have asked the question that matters most, and your team gets an ordinary meeting. Assignments keep moving, with a second look if Human Resources advises one.
**Recommended:** yes

**Option 3:** Quietly ask the senior consultant you trust most what people are saying.
**Response:** This pulls a staff member into the matter and could make them a witness. It may also feel like a test of loyalty. Even if you mean well, you cannot control how the question lands.

**Option 4:** Spend the evening going through six months of assignments to work out who filed and to prepare your answer.
**Response:** Your records may well matter later, and you should keep them intact. But the goal here is identifying the person, and that is the part to drop. If you are asked for records, gather them for the review, not for your own theory.

### Knowledge check
**Question:** An employee tells you in a one-to-one that they think a decision you made was discrimination based on their national origin. What is your duty as a supervisor?
- Ask for details so you can decide whether it is serious enough to report.
- Report it to Human Resources or the Affirmative Action Officer right away, and do not handle it yourself. (correct)
- Wait, because you cannot be expected to report on yourself.
- Offer to reverse the decision so that no report is needed.
**Correct answer:** Report it to Human Resources or the Affirmative Action Officer right away, and do not handle it yourself.
**Feedback if correct:** Right. The state policies as read in October 2026 call for immediate reporting, and nothing we read makes an exception when you are the subject. You can listen and thank the person. You do not investigate or bargain.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Look again. Asking for details, waiting or offering a fix all keep the matter in your hands, and that is the problem. The duty is to report it immediately to Human Resources or the Affirmative Action Officer.

### Transfer prompt
**Prompt:** Write one if-then plan: "If I learn I am named in a complaint, then before I speak to anyone on my team I will call ____ and ask ____."
**Options:**
- If it comes from Human Resources or my manager, then I will write my note and ask who reviews my decisions for now.
- If an employee tells me directly, then I will thank them, say I must pass it on, and report it that same day.
- If I only hear a rumor, then I will call Human Resources and ask whether there is anything I need to know, without asking who.

## Lesson 2: Keep supervising everyone while it runs

**Summary:** The work does not stop, and neither does your job. This lesson shows you how to keep making ordinary decisions without punishing anyone, freezing up or overcorrecting.
**Minutes:** 13

**Objectives:**
- Apply one test to any decision that could discourage someone from raising a concern.
- Recognize freezing and overcorrecting as failures too.
- Arrange a second look on decisions, with the right person.
- Handle a real performance problem while a complaint is open.

**Takeaways:**
- Retaliation is usually a small decision with a plausible reason.
- Treat everyone the way you treated them before.
- A real performance problem still gets addressed, with a second look first.
- Keep your records at the same level for everyone. Do not start a new file on one person.

### Text: Why this matters more than the complaint
A complaint can turn out to have no basis and retaliation can still be real. Retaliation claims are about what happens after someone raises a concern. State Policy 1436 prohibits it, Minnesota Statutes section 363A.15 addresses reprisals, and the EEOC publishes federal guidance on it. The course "Preventing retaliation" (dc-l73) teaches the subject in full. This lesson adds what changes when the decision maker is the person who was named.

Here is what changes: you now have a personal stake in every decision about these people. You may not feel any different. Other people will read your decisions differently, and they will be right to look closely. You cannot fix that by being a good person. You can address it with process.

### Statement
You do not need to be sure you are being fair. You need a decision someone else can check.

### List: The check for any decision while the matter is open
- Would I have made this decision if no complaint existed?
- Am I treating this person the way I treat others in the same spot?
- Did I write down the work-based reason before I acted?
- Could this discourage a reasonable person from raising a concern or taking part?
- Who else has looked at it?

### Text: Which decisions need the second look
Formal discipline is the obvious one, and the list is longer than that. Assignments, schedule changes, telework arrangements, training nominations, stretch work, ratings, reference comments and who gets invited to which meeting can all matter. In *Muldrow v. City of St. Louis* (April 17, 2024), the Supreme Court held that a transfer plaintiff needs to show only "some harm" to a term or condition of work, and rank and pay had not changed in that case. Retaliation claims keep a separate standard, "materially adverse." You do not need to sort those standards out yourself. The practical lesson is that a change does not have to touch pay to count.

Ask Human Resources who should give the second look. Your own manager is often the natural choice, unless your manager is also part of the matter. Tell that person the decision and your reason. You do not need to discuss the complaint with them to do this.

### Tabs: Three situations
#### Tab: You know who filed
Do not raise it with them. Keep your one-to-ones and keep the same agenda. Do not cancel, shorten or add a witness unless Human Resources says to. If you feel you cannot be fair right now, tell Human Resources. That is not a weakness, and they may have options. If someone else takes over a task, make sure the change does not cost the employee anything.

#### Tab: You do not know who filed
This is harder, and in some ways cleaner. You cannot target someone you have not identified, so do not narrow it down. Apply the check to every decision about every team member. Watch for your own suspicion. It tends to attach to whoever has recently disagreed with you, and that may not be the person.

#### Tab: You have been told to step back
The agency may change who supervises someone, or move some of your duties, while the matter runs. An interim step is not a finding. Ask what is changing, for how long, and what you may tell the team. If a change seems likely to cost the person who raised the concern, say so to Human Resources. Interim steps should not become a penalty for reporting, as "Responding to discrimination and harassment reports" (dc-l72) explains.

### Accordion: Two failures that look like caution
#### Item: Freezing
You stop giving feedback, avoid the person and let problems slide. It feels safe, but they lose the supervision they are owed. Silence can also feel like exclusion. Other staff will notice that the rules have changed for one person.

#### Item: Overcorrecting
You hand out the best assignments and extra praise, and you sign everything right away. This treats someone differently because of the complaint, and it can signal who you think complained. It is also unfair to the rest of the team, whose work is still theirs to earn.

### Leader move: The real performance problem
**Control:** You control whether a real problem is handled the way you would handle it for anyone: specific, about the work, and recorded the same way as usual.
**Failure:** Do not escalate faster than you normally would, and do not sit on it until the matter closes. Both make the problem about the complaint.
**Next:** Call Human Resources before you act, say what the work problem is, and ask whether you or someone else should have the conversation.

### Scenario
**Context:** You are named in a complaint, and Human Resources has told you who filed it. That person is a consultant on your team who has now missed two deadlines on provider reviews. You would normally bring this up at your next one-to-one, which is in two days.
**Prompt:** What is your next step?

**Option 1:** Say nothing until the complaint closes, so no one can say you retaliated.
**Response:** This is freezing. The deadlines still matter to providers and to the people they serve. If the problem grows, you will face a harder conversation later and an awkward question about why you waited.

**Option 2:** Call Human Resources today, describe the work problem, and ask whether you or someone else should raise it. Then have it raised in the ordinary way, in the ordinary setting.
**Response:** This is the best next step. The work gets addressed, someone else has looked at it first, and you have kept the same level of response you would use for anyone.
**Recommended:** yes

**Option 3:** Issue a written warning now, because you need a clean record in case this comes up in the review.
**Response:** You would be moving faster and harder than your usual practice, for a reason connected to the complaint. That is exactly the pattern a retaliation concern looks for. Corrective steps also have contract rules that you would need to check first.

**Option 4:** Move the provider reviews to another consultant so the problem goes away.
**Response:** This looks kind, but taking work away after a complaint can harm someone, even with no change in pay or rank. It also skips the conversation that they are owed.

### Knowledge check
**Question:** While a complaint about you is open, which of these needs a second look before you act?
- A change to the named employee's schedule only.
- Formal discipline only.
- Any decision about any team member that could discourage someone from raising a concern, including assignments and training nominations. (correct)
- Nothing, as long as your reasons are honest.
**Correct answer:** Any decision about any team member that could discourage someone from raising a concern, including assignments and training nominations.
**Feedback if correct:** Yes. Honest reasons matter, and so does someone else being able to check them. A change does not have to touch pay to count.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Think wider. Everyday decisions can harm someone or discourage a report, and you may not know who filed. The check applies across the team.

### Transfer prompt
**Prompt:** Write one if-then plan for the next decision you will make about staff: "If I have a personal stake in a decision about someone, then I will write my work-based reason first and ask ____ to look at it."
**Options:**
- If it is an assignment or schedule change, then I will write the reason and get a second look before I announce it.
- If it is a performance concern, then I will call Human Resources before I raise it.
- If I notice I am avoiding someone, then I will keep the next one-to-one exactly as scheduled.

## Lesson 3: Taking part in the review

**Summary:** How to prepare, what to bring, what to say and what not to do, and what the data rules mean for what becomes known.
**Minutes:** 12

**Objectives:**
- Prepare for an interview or request for records.
- Keep witnesses and colleagues out of your preparation.
- Find out what your own contract or plan provides.
- Explain in general terms what state data law says about complaints.

**Takeaways:**
- Answer what you know. "I do not remember" is a true answer when it is true.
- Hand over what is asked for, including what you wish you had not written.
- Do not talk to possible witnesses about the matter.
- Check your own contract. Do not assume it matches your staff's.

### Text: What to expect, and what we do not know
This course cannot tell you the local steps. The research behind it did not include a DHS complaint procedure, so we do not know who will contact you, in what order, or how long it will take. Ask Human Resources at the start, and ask again if time passes with no word.

You may not be told everything. You may not be told who filed, what others said or how the review is going. That can be hard to live with. It often exists to protect the people who take part.

### List: Prepare the way you would for any serious fact-finding
- Read what you have been given, and ask what you do not understand.
- Lay out dates and decisions from your existing records. Do not change, annotate or rewrite those records. Make any summary a separate document.
- Separate what you saw and did from what you assumed or heard.
- Think about what you would like the reviewer to look at, and say where it can be found. Do not go collecting statements for them.
- Ask for any access you need, such as an interpreter, captioning, a format you can read, written questions or a break. An interview is a part of your job, so your own access needs count here.

### List: In the room
- Tell the truth, including the parts that do not help you.
- Answer what was asked. If you do not remember, say so. If you remember later, go back and correct it.
- Describe what happened and what your reason was. Do not offer opinions on the other person's character or motive.
- Do not guess out loud about who said what.
- Ask what you may and may not discuss afterward, and follow it.

### Statement
You do not have to win the review. You have to be truthful in it.

### Text: Your own rights come from your own contract
Supervisors usually sit in a different bargaining unit from the staff they supervise. Under Minnesota Statutes section 179A.10, supervisors are in unit 16, or unit 12 for engineering, while general professional staff represented by MAPE are in unit 14, and managerial positions are excluded from units altogether. That means your rights in a review may be set by a different document from the one you use for your team.

For one example: the MAPE 2025-2027 contract, Article 8, section 8, keeps oral reprimands and investigations that end without discipline out of the personnel file. That is a term for MAPE-represented staff. Do not assume yours says the same. Read your own contract or plan, or ask your representative or labor relations. Whether you may have someone with you in an interview is the first question to ask.

### Text: What becomes known
Minnesota data law may surprise you. As of October 2026, Minnesota Statutes section 13.43 lists among public personnel data the existence and status of complaints, and the final disposition of discipline along with the reasons. All other personnel data is private unless another rule applies. The same section has protections for the identity of harassment complainants and witnesses, in subdivision 8.

What this means for you: do not promise anyone that a matter will never be known, and do not assume that it will be. Do not tell people who took part. If someone asks you what is public, send them to Human Resources or the agency data practices contact. These rules have detail that this course does not cover.

### Leader move: Witnesses
**Control:** You control whether you stay away from the people who may be asked about this.
**Failure:** Do not ask anyone what they said or will say, or remind them how you have supported them. Even a kind word can read as pressure when you supervise them.
**Next:** If a team member brings it up, say: "I can't talk about that. If you have been asked to take part, just tell the truth. Nothing about your work changes because of it." Then go back to work.

### Scenario
**Context:** An investigator asks for your messages about assignments on the team for the past year. While gathering them, you find a chat message you sent a peer supervisor. It is about an assignment, and it includes a sarcastic remark about one of your consultants. You had forgotten it. It reads badly.
**Prompt:** What do you do?

**Option 1:** Delete it. It is one remark, and it does not change the facts.
**Response:** Do not do this. Deleting a record during a review can do more harm to you than any message could, and it may be discovered. State systems hold government data, and you do not control all the copies.

**Option 2:** Leave it out, since it is mostly a personal aside.
**Response:** It is about an assignment, so it is within the request. Leaving it out is a decision to hide it. If you are unsure about the scope of a request, ask. Do not decide alone in your own favor.

**Option 3:** Hand it over with everything else. If you want to add context, do it in a short, separate factual note, and tell your representative.
**Response:** This is the best step. The record stays intact, and you have a fair way to explain it. A remark you regret is far easier to deal with than a gap in your records.
**Recommended:** yes

**Option 4:** Ask the peer supervisor to delete their copy, then hand over yours.
**Response:** This draws a colleague in and looks like interference. It could make things much worse for both of you.

### Knowledge check
**Question:** As of October 2026, which of these does Minnesota Statutes section 13.43 list among public personnel data?
- The full text of every interview in a complaint review.
- The existence and status of complaints, and the final disposition of discipline with reasons. (correct)
- The names of all witnesses in a harassment complaint.
- Nothing about complaints is ever public.
**Correct answer:** The existence and status of complaints, and the final disposition of discipline with reasons.
**Feedback if correct:** Correct. That is why you should not promise a matter will stay unknown. The same section protects the identity of harassment complainants and witnesses. Ask Human Resources or the data practices contact about any specific case.
**Feedback if incorrect:** The statute treats some complaint facts as public and keeps other personnel data private, with added protection for harassment complainants and witnesses. The public items are the existence and status of complaints and the final disposition of discipline with reasons.

### Transfer prompt
**Prompt:** Write one if-then plan: "If I am asked to take part in a review, then before the first interview I will find out ____ from ____."
**Options:**
- If I do not know my own rights, then I will read my contract or plan and ask whether I may have someone with me.
- If I need access support, then I will ask for it when the interview is scheduled.
- If a team member raises the matter, then I will use the one sentence I have prepared and go back to work.

## Lesson 4: Your own footing

**Summary:** A complaint can be one of the loneliest moments in a supervisor's working life. This lesson covers where to get real support without drawing your team in, and how to think straight while it is open.
**Minutes:** 11

**Objectives:**
- Choose people you can talk to without involving your team.
- Keep two honest possibilities open while the matter runs.
- Use the proper route if you have a concern of your own.
- Recognize when you need more help than a colleague can give.

**Takeaways:**
- Your team cannot be your support during this.
- You can talk to a peer about how you are doing without naming anyone.
- Keep the facts of what you did separate from the conclusions others will draw.
- You have the same routes as anyone else if you are being treated unfairly.

### Text: Who you can talk to
You need someone to talk to. Your staff are off the list, including the ones you are close to, because you have power over them and some of them may be asked to take part.

Here are better choices. Your representative or adviser, for the process. The state Employee Assistance Program, which the state describes as free and confidential; check the Minnesota Management and Budget benefits page for current details. A health care provider or counselor of your own. Family or friends outside the agency, within whatever limits you have been given. A peer supervisor, for how you are doing and not for the facts.

That last one takes some care. "I am going through something hard at work and I am not sleeping" is fine. "Here is what the consultant said about me" is not. Do not type the details of the matter into any tool that is not part of the process, including the Ask feature in this app.

### Statement
Your team should not have to carry this with you.

### Text: Two things can both be possible
You may be sure you did nothing wrong. You may be right. You may also have made a decision that had an effect you did not see. You may be completely unsure. This course will not tell you which, and it will not ask you to admit anything. That is the review's job, and it is a question about facts.

Keeping both possibilities open is useful. It keeps you from building a story that you will later have to defend. And it lets you keep learning about your own practice regardless of the result. One way to do this is to write a private account of what you did and why, with dates, and leave out what you think everyone else was feeling. Treat it as you would any personal document, and remember that records on state systems are government data, so ask your adviser about where to keep it.

### Accordion: Pressures you may be carrying
#### Item: Your manager's distance
Your manager may become careful around you, and may be advised to be. That can feel like abandonment. Ask them plainly how they would like to handle ordinary work during this time.

#### Item: Doing the job while being watched
You may feel every decision is being judged. Some are. The second-look habit from Lesson 2 helps here too, because you do not have to carry each decision alone.

#### Item: Being one of few people like you in your role
If you are one of very few supervisors of your background, or a disabled supervisor, you may feel you are being judged on behalf of more people than yourself. That weight is real. It does not change the process, and you are allowed to say so to your adviser. The course "Who Carries the Equity Work" (sm-who-carries-equity-work) has more on this.

#### Item: Believing the complaint is unfair
You may believe the complaint is untrue or made in bad faith. You can tell the reviewer the facts that support your view. Do not act on that belief in how you treat anyone.

### Text: If you have a concern of your own
Being named does not take away your own rights. If you believe you are being harassed or discriminated against, or that your request for accommodation is being ignored, you can raise it with Human Resources or the Affirmative Action Officer like anyone else. Raise it because it is true and it needs handling. Do not use it as a counter-move. If you are not sure how the two concerns fit together, ask your representative first.

### Leader move: Ordinary days
**Control:** You control your routines. Keep your one-to-ones, your meetings and your usual hours. Keep taking your leave too.
**Failure:** Do not vanish into the work or into the matter. Do not send late-night messages about what you have been thinking.
**Next:** Choose one person outside your team and one fixed time each week to check in with them. If you notice you cannot function, or you or someone else is in danger, call or text 988, or follow your emergency procedures.

### Scenario
**Context:** Three weeks into a review, you are worn out. A long-time team member stays late and says, "We all know this is nonsense. Just tell me what happened and I can set people straight." You feel a huge pull of relief, because someone is on your side.
**Prompt:** What do you say?

**Option 1:** Thank them, tell them what happened so they have the facts, and ask them to keep it to themselves.
**Response:** This feels like a relief for one evening. It puts your employee in the middle of a review, it may make them a witness who has heard your version first, and it could spread. You have also handed them something they cannot hold.

**Option 2:** Thank them for the kindness, say you cannot talk about it and they should not defend you to others, and go home. Then call the outside person you have chosen.
**Response:** This is the best response. You accept the care, you protect them from being drawn in, and you take your need for support to a place that can hold it.
**Recommended:** yes

**Option 3:** Tell them it is none of their business and that talk like that has to stop.
**Response:** You are right to set the limit, but the harshness will travel around the team. A kind offer met with a sharp answer can also look like pressure on people's speech while a review is open.

**Option 4:** Ask them who "we all" is, and what others have said.
**Response:** This is fishing for who is on which side. It turns a kind moment into information gathering, and that is what you are trying to avoid.

### Knowledge check
**Question:** You are named in a complaint and want to talk to a peer supervisor. Which approach fits this lesson?
- Share the full facts, as peers can keep things confidential.
- Talk about how you are coping, without naming people or giving details. (correct)
- Ask the peer to speak to the employee for you.
- Talk to no one at all until it closes.
**Correct answer:** Talk about how you are coping, without naming people or giving details.
**Feedback if correct:** Yes. You get support without spreading the matter or drawing a colleague in.
**Feedback if incorrect:** You do need support, and isolation has real costs, but details should stay in the process. A peer can hear how you are doing without hearing who did what.

### Transfer prompt
**Prompt:** Write one if-then plan: "If I am under a review like this, then I will talk to ____ about how I am doing, and I will not talk to ____ about it."
**Options:**
- If I need process advice, then I will go to my representative or adviser.
- If I need someone to listen, then I will use the Employee Assistance Program or someone outside the agency.
- If a team member offers to take my side, then I will thank them and say I cannot discuss it.

## Lesson 5: After the outcome

**Summary:** The review ends, and you still work with these people. This lesson covers each likely outcome, and what continues no matter how it comes out.
**Minutes:** 13

**Objectives:**
- Respond to each likely outcome in a way that does not create a new problem.
- Continue protecting against retaliation after a matter closes.
- Choose a change in practice that is worth making whatever the result.
- Work with the person who complained on ordinary terms.

**Takeaways:**
- No one has to tell you they are sorry, and no one has to thank you.
- A closed complaint does not end the duty not to retaliate.
- You may never hear the whole story.
- Pick one practice to change, and let it be about the work.

### Text: The first thing that does not change
The person who raised the concern is still protected after the matter closes, whatever the result. The same goes for anyone who took part. As the course "Preventing retaliation" (dc-l73) puts it, a usable process cannot depend on a guaranteed successful complaint. Ask Human Resources how long a second look on decisions should continue. Do not assume that it stops the day you get the letter.

### Tabs: Four ways it can end
#### Tab: Not supported
You may feel relieved, vindicated or angry. Do not bring any of that to work. Do not hold a meeting about it or tell the team you were cleared, unless Human Resources advises you to say something and tells you what. Do not treat the person who complained as a liar. A concern can be raised in good faith and not be supported.

#### Tab: Some or all supported
You may receive corrective action, and you may be asked to change something. Find out exactly what is expected. Read your own contract about what happens next, including any right to respond. Then do what is required, and do it visibly in your practice, not in speeches. If repair with someone is possible and is wanted by them, "Team repair conversations" (dc-t67) and "Responding when someone says that was harmful" (dc-l76) can help. Repair cannot be a condition placed on them.

#### Tab: Unclear, or you never hear
Reviews can end without a clear answer for you, or you may hear little. Ask once what you are able to know, and ask what is expected of you now. Then get back to the work. It may never feel finished. You can still be fair without knowing everything.

#### Tab: You disagree with the outcome
You may have routes to respond or grieve, depending on your contract or plan. Use them through your representative, within the time limits. Do not argue the case with your staff, and do not let your disagreement show in anyone's assignments.

### Text: Working with the person afterward
It may be awkward for a long time. You do not have to pretend it is not, and you do not need to fix the feeling. You owe them what you owe everyone: clear expectations, fair assignments, honest feedback, regular one-to-ones, and the same access to opportunity as others. They do not owe you warmth or forgiveness.

If you feel you cannot be fair, say so to Human Resources or your manager. It is much better to raise this than to act it out.

The rest of the team may also need something, if the matter has affected them. "Supporting a team after a harmful incident" (dc-t66) covers that. Stick to what you are allowed to say.

### Statement
You owe them fair supervision. They do not owe you forgiveness.

### List: One change worth making whatever the result
Choose one that fits what you noticed during this time. Keep it about the work.
- Write the reason for significant assignments before you make them.
- Keep a simple assignment log and check it each quarter.
- Keep one-to-ones on a fixed schedule with the same core agenda for everyone.
- Ask your manager to look at a sample of your decisions twice a year.
- Move hard conversations out of chat and into a planned talk.

### Leader move: After it closes
**Control:** You control whether the end of the matter becomes a turning point for the team or passes quietly into ordinary fair practice.
**Failure:** Do not take a victory lap, and do not go into a long public penance. Either one makes your feelings the team's job.
**Next:** Put a date on your calendar three months from now to check your recent decisions about the person who complained against your decisions about others. Ask someone else to look too, if Human Resources agrees.

### Scenario
**Context:** Human Resources tells you the complaint against you was not supported. You know who filed it, and that person is still on your team. Two weeks later, your division asks each supervisor to put forward a staff member for an open leadership development cohort. That person has the strongest case on the criteria. You still feel betrayed.
**Prompt:** What do you do?

**Option 1:** Put forward your second choice. You have a good reason for that person too, and you are not ready to champion the one who complained.
**Response:** This is retaliation in its usual form: a small, plausible decision that costs someone a real opportunity. Access to training and leadership development is treated as a term of employment. A closed complaint does not change that.

**Option 2:** Apply the stated criteria, write down your reason, ask your manager to look at it, and put forward the person the criteria point to.
**Response:** This is the best step. You do not have to feel warmly toward them. You do have to make the decision the criteria make, and you have someone checking your work while your feelings are raw.
**Recommended:** yes

**Option 3:** Skip nominating anyone this round so you do not have to choose.
**Response:** This looks neutral, but it denies that person, and everyone else on the team, the chance. It is also harder to defend than a documented decision.

**Option 4:** Put them forward, and tell them you did it even after everything that happened.
**Response:** The decision is right and the comment undoes it. It ties the opportunity to the complaint and tells them you are keeping score.

### Knowledge check
**Question:** A complaint about you was not supported, and the file is closed. What is true of the person who raised it?
- They are no longer protected, because the complaint was not supported.
- You may now record your concerns about their judgment in their review.
- You still may not treat them worse for having raised the concern. (correct)
- You should keep them at a distance to prevent another complaint.
**Correct answer:** You still may not treat them worse for having raised the concern.
**Feedback if correct:** Right. A complaint does not have to succeed to be protected, and the duty does not end when the matter closes. If you are in doubt about scope in a specific case, ask Human Resources.
**Feedback if incorrect:** A process people can use cannot depend on their winning. Lack of support for a complaint does not open the door to worse treatment or to distance.

### Transfer prompt
**Prompt:** Write one if-then plan: "If a matter about me closes, then within two weeks I will ____, and in three months I will ____."
**Options:**
- If it was not supported, then I will say nothing to the team unless told to, and I will check my decisions in three months.
- If some of it was supported, then I will find out exactly what is expected and choose one change in practice.
- If it never feels finished, then I will ask once what I can know, and go back to the work.

## Job aid

**Title:** Named in a complaint: first steps and steady habits
**Subtitle:** One page for the worst week.

**Purpose:** Help you take the right steps in the right order when you learn a complaint names you, and keep your team out of it.

**Remember:**
- A complaint is not a finding. It is also not nothing.
- Call before you talk.
- Delete nothing.
- Everyone keeps their rights, including you.

### Section: First day
- Stop. Say nothing to the team about it.
- Write a private note: what, who told you, when.
- Call Human Resources. Ask who is handling it, what you may say, and who reviews your decisions for now.
- If an employee tells you directly: thank them, say you must pass it on, and report it that day to Human Resources or the Affirmative Action Officer.
- Find your own contract or plan, and your representative or adviser.

### Section: Never
- Try to find out who filed.
- Ask the team what they have heard.
- Reach out to "clear the air."
- Start a new file on anyone.
- Delete, edit or tidy any record.
- Talk to possible witnesses about the matter.

### Section: Before any decision about staff
- Would I decide this with no complaint on the table?
- Is it the way I treat others in the same spot?
- Did I write the work reason first?
- Could it discourage someone from raising a concern?
- Who else looked at it?

### Section: Taking part
- Tell the truth. Say what you do not remember.
- Hand over what is asked for.
- Ask for the access you need.
- Ask what you may discuss afterward.

### Section: After it closes
- No victory lap and no public penance.
- Keep protecting everyone who took part.
- Check your decisions at three months.
- Choose one change in practice.

**Do next:** Save your Human Resources contact and your own contract today. Do it now, when it costs you nothing.
