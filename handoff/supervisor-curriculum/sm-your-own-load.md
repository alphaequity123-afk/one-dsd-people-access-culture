# sm-your-own-load: Your Own Load

**Subtitle:** Supervisors spend a lot of time protecting other people's capacity. This course is about yours: seeing what you carry, taking it to your manager, stopping the habits that add to it, and using support that actually exists.
**Duration:** About 50 minutes
**Content type:** practice
**Status:** reviewed
**Last reviewed:** October 31, 2026
**Evidence date:** October 2026
**Scope:** For supervisors, leads and managers. It is voluntary. Your progress stays in your browser and nothing is sent to your supervisor or employer. Questions typed into Ask go to outside services, and the app owner keeps a copy. Any private notes are best kept on paper. This is general information, not legal, medical or clinical advice.

**Course objectives:**
- Describe the whole of what you carry, including the parts that do not appear on any list.
- Bring a workload problem to your manager as a decision they can make.
- Stop the habits that move work onto you and hide the cost.
- Find support that works for someone who holds confidential information.
- Use your own rights to leave and accommodation as readily as you would advise staff to.

**Applied next step:** Pick one item from your load that you can drop, move or make visible this month, and put a date on it.

## Lesson 1: See the whole load

**Summary:** Supervisors often measure their load by their calendar. Much of it isn't there. This lesson helps you see what you carry, using five categories.
**Minutes:** 12

**Objectives:**
- Name the five kinds of load supervisors carry.
- Make a private inventory using no one's name.
- Sort what is the job, what has drifted to you and what you took on yourself.
- Keep the inventory about the work, not about you as a person.

**Takeaways:**
- Learning this job takes about a year. Struggling in that year isn't failure.
- The confidential load doesn't show on a calendar.
- You can't fix a load you haven't counted.
- This is a work inventory, not a diagnosis.

### Text: Why this gets its own course
The app's other well-being courses are about how you protect staff. "Sustaining the Work" (gc-sustaining-the-work) teaches the terms for secondary traumatic stress and burnout, and how to supervise in a way that sustains people. "Preventing burnout and moral distress" (dc-l92) teaches how to look at demands and resources for your team. Read them. They apply to you as well.

This course starts from a simple fact: you are also someone's staff. You have demands, and resources, and a manager. You also have a problem your staff do not: you often can't say what's weighing on you, because it's someone else's confidential matter.

We should be plain about the evidence. We found no public supervisor curriculum or direct study on this subject. What we have is close by. The National Frontline Supervisor Competencies, built on Minnesota job analysis, expect supervisors to reach competence over about a year, not at hire. Oregon's child welfare supervisor plan treats secondary traumatic stress as something to prepare supervisors for, and builds in a monthly community of practice for the first 12 months. A randomized study of garment-line supervisors in India, by Adhvaryu and colleagues (2023), found that those given soft-skills training were 15% less likely to quit. That is a long way from a DSD office, but it does suggest supervisors' own staying power can be affected by the support they get.

### Statement
You can't protect your team's capacity by spending your own until there's nothing left.

### Tabs: Five kinds of load
#### Tab: Your own work
Many supervisors keep a caseload, a policy portfolio or a project. Reports, approvals, budgets and meetings come on top. Ask yourself how much of your week that really takes, and whether anyone has ever said what should give way.

#### Tab: Your people
One-to-ones, coaching, reviews, hiring, onboarding, accommodation requests, leave, conflict, corrective action. A single hard situation can take up days you did not plan for. These are the job. They are also often the first thing squeezed.

#### Tab: Things you can't talk about
A complaint you've reported. A colleague's illness. An upcoming cut. A person you know is about to be let go while you plan their next project. This is heavy, it doesn't show on any calendar, and it often cuts you off from the people you'd usually talk to.

#### Tab: Other people's hardest moments
Serious incident reports, a death review, a family in crisis, a hostile public meeting. Staff bring you these too. You may read reports about the people they serve, and then listen to staff who read them first. "Sustaining the Work" covers this exposure.

#### Tab: Being the middle
Decisions come down that you did not make, and you explain them as if you agree. Concerns go up and nothing comes back. You absorb frustration in both directions. If you're asked to do something that conflicts with what you think is right, that may be what "Preventing burnout and moral distress" calls moral distress, and it deserves a look as a work question.

### List: Make your inventory (on paper, with no names)
1. Write down everything you carried in the past two weeks, across all five kinds. Use "a leave coverage problem," not a person's name.
2. Mark each one: part of my job as written; drifted to me; I took it on.
3. Mark what only you can do, because of authority or confidentiality.
4. Mark what you could drop, move or do less of, if someone with authority agreed.
5. Circle what no one above you knows about.

### Text: What this is and isn't
This is not a test of whether you're burned out. It's an inventory of work, and you decide what to do with it. You don't have to tell anyone what it says. In "Sustaining the Work" you'll see that burnout is described by the World Health Organization as an occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition, and that supervisors shouldn't diagnose staff. Don't diagnose yourself either, on the basis of a course. If you are worried about how you are doing, Lesson 4 lists where to go.

### Leader move: The circled items
**Control:** You control whether what you've circled stays invisible. The non-confidential items can be described to your manager in general terms.
**Failure:** Do not take this inventory as a list of personal shortcomings, and do not treat a long list as proof of your importance.
**Next:** Choose one circled item that you can describe without any private detail. Lesson 2 shows you how to raise it.

### Scenario
**Context:** You supervise eight consultants and keep a small policy portfolio of your own. For three weeks, you've spent evenings on the portfolio because your days go to a difficult accommodation case, a vacancy and two staff conflicts. You have told no one, because the work is getting done. You wonder whether this is just what the job is.
**Prompt:** What's your best next step?

**Option 1:** Keep going. It's a rough patch, and asking for help this early in the role would look bad.
**Response:** It may be a rough patch. But no one above you knows about the cost, so nothing can change. And your team is learning that this is what supervising looks like.

**Option 2:** Make the inventory, mark what only you can do, and pick one item to take to your manager with a specific ask.
**Response:** This is the best next step. It turns a feeling into something you can describe, and it starts with one thing that can actually change.
**Recommended:** yes

**Option 3:** Tell your team you're overloaded, so they understand why you've been less available.
**Response:** Being honest that it's a busy time can be fine. But saying you're overloaded with no plan may lead staff to stop bringing you things, and those things include what you need to hear. Your team can't solve this.

**Option 4:** Hand your portfolio to your strongest consultant.
**Response:** Moving work may be part of the answer, but it's a decision to make with your manager and with that consultant, based on what comes off their plate. See Lesson 3.

### Knowledge check
**Question:** What does the National Frontline Supervisor Competencies document expect about how long it takes a supervisor to reach competence?
- Supervisors should be competent at hire.
- About a year, not at hire. (correct)
- About 90 days for all skills.
- There's no expected time.
**Correct answer:** About a year, not at hire.
**Feedback if correct:** Yes. Its validation work also found that many skills are needed at hire or within 90 days, so the pressure is real. Being still in the learning curve during your first year is expected.
**Feedback if incorrect:** The document expects competence over about a year. Many skills are needed early, but you aren't expected to have them all on day one.

### Transfer prompt
**Prompt:** Write one if-then plan: "If I notice I've worked ____ evenings in a row, then I will ____."
**Options:**
- If evening work continues for two weeks, then I will make the inventory and pick one item.
- If something only I can do is crowding out supervising, then I will raise it with my manager.
- If I'm holding something confidential that's weighing on me, then I will use the support I've chosen in Lesson 4.

## Lesson 2: Taking it to your manager

**Summary:** Many supervisors either say nothing or say "I'm overwhelmed." Neither gives a manager anything to act on. This lesson teaches you how to make it a decision.
**Minutes:** 13

**Objectives:**
- Describe a load problem in terms of work, cost and choices.
- Ask a manager to choose among real options.
- Keep private details out of the conversation.
- Decide what to do when the answer is no.

**Takeaways:**
- Bring choices, not just a problem.
- A manager can't drop what they don't know you're carrying.
- Write down what was agreed.
- A no is information. It may also mean something else should change.

### Text: Why this conversation is worth having
The evidence on transfer of training points to the work environment as a major factor, including what your own manager does. Taylor and colleagues (2005) found that transfer was greatest when trainees' superiors were also trained, among other conditions. Kalev, Dobbin and Kelly (2006), in a long-running study of private-sector firms, found that establishing clear responsibility produced the broadest gains in managerial diversity. Those findings are about programs, not about your workload. The point for you is that a lot of what you can do depends on someone above you. Asking is part of the job, and a good manager needs to hear it.

The course "Backing Your Supervisors" (sm-backing-your-supervisors) is written for your manager. You're allowed to point them to it.

### List: The shape of the conversation
1. **What's happening.** "For the past three weeks, I've spent about X hours a week on Y, on top of Z."
2. **What it's costing.** "One-to-ones have slipped twice. The review of [the report] is behind by a week."
3. **What the choices are.** "I see three options: move the report to next month, get help on the vacancy, or drop my portfolio's second project."
4. **What you recommend.** "I'd go with the second, for these reasons."
5. **What you need.** "I need your decision by Friday, and I need you to tell the program director what's moving."

### Statement
"I'm overwhelmed" describes you. "Here are three things, and I can do two" describes the work. Your manager can act on the second.

### Tabs: Keeping it about the work
#### Tab: Without private details
You can say: "I've got a personnel matter that's taking a lot of time, and I can't discuss the details." Your manager may already know, or may need to go through Human Resources. Don't share an employee's medical information or complaint details in order to justify your workload.

#### Tab: Without blaming staff
"My team isn't performing" often isn't accurate and isn't fair. Name the work and the demand. If there's a real performance issue, take that up as its own topic.

#### Tab: If your manager is the source
Sometimes your manager keeps adding work. Name what you're being asked to do and what's already on the list. "You've asked me to take on the new project. That's the third this quarter. Which of the other two should wait?" You can ask this without any accusation.

### Accordion: When the answer is no, or nothing changes
#### Item: Ask what's the priority
"I understand we can't move anything. If I can't do all of it well, which should I do first?" If you get an answer, write it down.

#### Item: Write it down
Send a short note after each conversation: what you said, what was decided, what's next. It isn't a threat. It's a record, and it protects you if something slips.

#### Item: Raise chronic understaffing
"Sustaining the Work" says that bigger mismatches, such as chronic understaffing, should be named and raised through management and, where relevant, labor-management channels. Ask what's available to you. Supervisors usually sit in a different bargaining unit from staff, and managers are outside units altogether, so your route may differ from your staff's. Check your own contract or plan.

#### Item: If the request itself worries you
If you're being told to do something you believe breaks a rule, or that you think is wrong, that's a separate conversation. Ask for clarification in writing and talk to Human Resources. If it involves discrimination or harassment, your duty to report still applies.

### Leader move: Ask before the crash
**Control:** You control when you raise it. Three weeks in is better than three months in.
**Failure:** Do not wait until you are too worn down to think it through or until something has visibly failed. Do not make your request in a hallway, either.
**Next:** Put 20 minutes on your manager's calendar this week, with a subject line that says what it's about.

### Scenario
**Context:** Your manager has asked you to take the lead on a new waiver implementation workgroup. You have one vacancy, an open accommodation process you're working through with Human Resources, and a report due in three weeks. You like this manager, and you want to be seen as someone who steps up. The workgroup is also good visibility for you.
**Prompt:** What do you say?

**Option 1:** "Sure, happy to." You'll figure it out.
**Response:** You've hidden the cost, and it will land somewhere: on the report, on your staff or on your evenings. You've also taught your manager that you always have room.

**Option 2:** "I'd like to do this. Here's what's already on my list. If I take it, the report moves or the vacancy needs someone else's help. Which would you choose?"
**Response:** This is the best response. You've shown you want the work, you've made the cost visible, and you've given your manager a real decision. You haven't shared any private detail about the accommodation.
**Recommended:** yes

**Option 3:** "I can't. I've got too much on my plate, and one of my staff has a medical situation."
**Response:** You've closed off an opportunity without exploring any choices, and you've shared something about a staff member that you shouldn't have.

**Option 4:** Say yes, and quietly give the report to your strongest consultant.
**Response:** You've moved the cost to someone else without telling anyone or taking anything off their plate. The problem hasn't gone away. It's moved.

### Knowledge check
**Question:** Which request gives your manager the most to work with?
- "I'm overwhelmed and something has to change."
- "My team isn't pulling its weight."
- "I have these three commitments and can do two well by the deadline. I recommend moving this one. Can you decide by Friday?" (correct)
- "I can't really talk about why, but I need less work."
**Correct answer:** "I have these three commitments and can do two well by the deadline. I recommend moving this one. Can you decide by Friday?"
**Feedback if correct:** Yes. It describes the work, gives options and asks for a decision.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Feelings and blame are hard to act on, and a vague request leaves your manager guessing. Name the commitments, the choice and when you need an answer.

### Transfer prompt
**Prompt:** Write one if-then plan: "If I'm asked to take on something new, then before I say yes I will name ____ and ask ____."
**Options:**
- If I'm asked to take on new work, then I will say what's on my list and ask what should move.
- If something slips because of load, then I will tell my manager before it's noticed.
- If nothing changes after I ask, then I will ask which item comes first, and write that down.

## Lesson 3: Stop absorbing

**Summary:** Some of your load is imposed, and some comes from habits that feel responsible. This lesson covers the habits, and how to hand off work without simply passing the problem to the people with the least power.
**Minutes:** 13

**Objectives:**
- Recognize the habits that add to your load and hide the cost.
- Delegate using stated reasons and something off the receiver's plate.
- Keep from becoming the only channel through which everything flows.
- Model limits your team can actually use.

**Takeaways:**
- Doing it yourself is often the unfair option in disguise.
- Delegating isn't dumping, if something comes off the other person's plate.
- Your team learns what's expected from what you do, not what you say.
- Taking your own leave is part of the job.

### Accordion: Habits that feel responsible
#### Item: Doing the work yourself
A vacancy opens or someone's out, and you take the cases. It feels like leadership. It hides the gap from your manager, takes you away from supervising and tells your staff that you don't trust them. A few days can be fine. Make it time-limited, and say so.

#### Item: Being the only channel
All questions come to you. All updates go through you. Everyone copies you. Ask: does this actually need me? Could one person take this kind of question? Would a shared tracker do the job?

#### Item: Solving instead of coaching
It's quicker to answer than to ask what they think. It's slower over a year. If you find yourself rewriting people's work, find out if it's the standard you need or your own preference.

#### Item: Absorbing frustration from both directions
You don't have to defend every decision from above, and you don't have to hold every complaint in silence. You can say, "I don't love this either, and here's what I know," and you can pass concerns up, then report back on what you heard.

#### Item: Keeping everyone comfortable
You put off a hard conversation so no one's upset. Then it takes up your attention for months. "Corrective Action and Labor Agreements" (sm-corrective-action) can help you prepare and get help.

### Statement
A supervisor who absorbs everything looks generous. The team loses visibility, development and an honest account of what the work takes.

### Text: Delegating fairly
There is a risk in telling supervisors to delegate. If you hand work to whoever is willing, you may just move the load to the people who already carry the most hidden work. Those are often the people who feel least able to say no. Research by Babcock and colleagues (2017), in the studied settings, found differences by gender in who was asked to take on low-promotability tasks and who accepted. That isn't DHS data, and it's not a rule about any person. It is a good reason to check whom you ask.

So delegate the way you would assign anything else. "Fair Assignments, Schedules and Visibility" (sm-fair-assignments) covers this in depth. Here's the short version.

### List: Before you hand something off
- What's the stated reason this person is getting it: skill, capacity, development?
- What comes off their plate?
- Is this real development, or only the part you don't want?
- Are they free to say no, and do they know that?
- Do they have authority to do it, or will it come back to you?
- Who has received the last few things you handed off? Is it always the same person?
- Is the work yours alone because of authority or confidentiality? If so, it can't move.

### Tabs: Limits your team can use
#### Tab: Hours
If you send email at 10 p.m., someone will answer it at 10 p.m. If you must write at night, schedule it to send in the morning. Say what actually counts as urgent. Say what a response time is. Keep to it.

#### Tab: Leave
Take it, and don't work through it. Name someone to cover. Plan for this before you go, with your manager's agreement. If your team never sees you away, they won't believe you when you tell them to go.

#### Tab: Saying a thing was hard
"That meeting was tough" is a useful thing for a team to hear. "I've been having a hard time sleeping because of what's going on with one of you" is not. Say what's true about the work without making your team responsible for you.

#### Tab: Availability
A supervisor can't always be available. Set regular times. Tell people how to reach you if it's urgent and who to go to if you can't be reached. Protect your one-to-ones first.

### Text: A caution about limits
Some supervisors have much less room to set limits than others. Some are watched more closely. Some would be judged harshly for saying "that was hard." Some carry their load with a disability, or while being asked to do extra work because of who they are. You know your situation. Pick the limits you can actually use. "Who Carries the Equity Work" (sm-who-carries-equity-work) takes that part up directly.

### Leader move: One thing off your plate
**Control:** You control one habit. Choose the one that costs the most and is easiest to change.
**Failure:** Do not try to redo your whole way of working in a week. Do not hand off the thing no one wants and call it development.
**Next:** Look at your inventory. Choose one thing you took on yourself and that someone else could do, or that could stop. Then talk to them, or to your manager.

### Scenario
**Context:** A consultant is out on leave, and you've been handling three of their cases yourself for six weeks. You're behind on reviews. One of your staff, a consultant who speaks Spanish, has been taking calls from Spanish-speaking families on the cases of others too, without being asked, and she's the one who always says yes. You think about giving her the three cases as well, since she knows the families and is always willing.
**Prompt:** What do you do?

**Option 1:** Give her the three cases. She's willing and she knows the families.
**Response:** Willing isn't the same as having capacity. She may already be carrying unassigned language work, and you've never counted that. This moves your problem onto someone with less room to say no.

**Option 2:** First, ask her what she's carrying, count the language work, and take something off her plate or make a proper plan for it. Then, split the three cases by stated reasons and tell your manager what's left uncovered.
**Response:** This is the best step. You've made hidden work visible, kept your hand-off fair and told your manager what the gap really costs. You'll also need to ask what is the proper route for language needs on these cases, and who should be doing it.
**Recommended:** yes

**Option 3:** Keep the cases for another month. You've gotten this far.
**Response:** Reviews and supervising are slipping, and your manager thinks the coverage is working. Six weeks is long enough to tell someone.

**Option 4:** Tell the team you need people to step up, and see who volunteers.
**Response:** You'll probably get the same people. Open volunteering can feel fair, and it tends to follow who already says yes.

### Knowledge check
**Question:** What makes a delegation fair, in the sense used in this lesson?
- The person volunteered.
- It's the part of the work you least want.
- There's a stated reason, something comes off their plate, and they can say no. (correct)
- Everyone gets the same number of tasks.
**Correct answer:** There's a stated reason, something comes off their plate, and they can say no.
**Feedback if correct:** Yes. Otherwise delegating may only move the load to those with least room to refuse.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Willingness and equal counts don't settle it. Check for a stated reason, what comes off their plate, and whether they can actually say no.

### Transfer prompt
**Prompt:** Write one if-then plan: "If I catch myself doing ____ again, then I will ____."
**Options:**
- If I take on someone's work during a gap, then I will set an end date and tell my manager.
- If I'm about to hand something to the person who always says yes, then I will first ask what they're carrying.
- If I'm writing email at night, then I will schedule it for the morning.

## Lesson 4: Support you can actually use

**Summary:** Supervisors often give good advice about support and don't use it. This lesson covers where to talk when so much is confidential, and your own rights to leave and accommodation.
**Minutes:** 12

**Objectives:**
- Build a small support plan you can use even when you can't share details.
- Use peers without breaking anyone's privacy.
- Use your own rights to accommodation and leave.
- Know when to get more help than a colleague can give.

**Takeaways:**
- You can talk about how you're doing without saying who or what.
- Peer support is worth the time to arrange.
- You have the same rights you'd tell your staff to use.
- Getting help doesn't require telling your manager.

### Text: Why peers matter
Reinhold and colleagues found, in a study of training transfer, that peer support was the strongest predictor of motivation to use what you've learned, and that feedback and coaching most strongly predicted actual transfer. Real programs build this in. Oregon's child welfare plan has a monthly community of practice for new supervisors. Multnomah County's new manager program uses cohort sessions every other month. These were not designed around well-being, and we found no outcome evaluation of them. Still, a peer who does the same job is one of the few people who can understand what you're describing.

### List: How to use a peer without breaking privacy
- Talk about the kind of situation, not the person. "I've got an accommodation process that's dragging. How have you handled it?" works. A name, a condition or a unique detail doesn't.
- Choose a peer outside your direct line, if you can, so you won't be talking about people they also work with.
- Agree on the rules out loud. "We don't use names. If it sounds like a reporting issue, we say so."
- Remember: if a peer tells you something that sounds like discrimination or harassment, your own duty to report may apply.
- Do not type staff details into any app, chat or AI tool, including the Ask feature in this one.
- Keep it mutual. Don't become each other's only outlet.

### Statement
You can say exactly how heavy something is without saying what it is.

### Tabs: Where else to go
#### Tab: Employee Assistance Program
The state describes its Employee Assistance Program as free and confidential for employees and the people they live with, with confidential counseling. Check the Minnesota Management and Budget benefits page for current details, since vendors and terms can change. It is not only for staff; it's for you. "Sustaining the Work" has more detail.

#### Tab: Your manager
Your manager may be good for this, or not. You know. Use them for the work (Lesson 2). You don't owe them your health or your history.

#### Tab: Your representative or Human Resources
For questions about your own rights or pay, or if something's wrong in how you're being treated. Harassment and discrimination policies protect you too. Being a supervisor doesn't change that.

#### Tab: Open groups
Employee resource groups at the State of Minnesota are described in their guidelines as open to all, including allies. "Employee Resource Groups and Affinity Spaces" (gc-employee-resource-groups) explains how they work. Some supervisors find connection there. Remember that messages on state systems are government data.

#### Tab: Your own health care provider
For anything that's affecting your health. The Employee Assistance Program can also help you find someone.

### Text: Your own accommodation and leave
Supervisors with disabilities, chronic conditions or caregiving duties sometimes hold back from asking. You may worry about looking unable to do the job, or you may feel you have to set an example of toughness. The duties in the accommodation process run to you as well. EEOC guidance dated October 17, 2002 says no special words are needed. The Minnesota Human Rights Act describes an informal interactive process. Minnesota added episodic or in-remission impairments to its definition of disability in 2024.

Ask the ADA coordinator or Human Resources directly, if you'd rather not start with your manager. If you're a supervisor who needs an interpreter, a screen reader or a flexible schedule, those needs belong to your job as a supervisor, including when you're running meetings, doing interviews or sitting in management meetings. Ask for what you need. Then you can be a real example, of how to ask.

The course "Leave and Return to Work" (sm-leave-and-return) is written for supervisors dealing with staff leave. The first lesson's advice applies to you too: you don't owe a story.

### Accordion: When you need more than this
#### Item: Signs to take seriously
"Sustaining the Work" reports SAMHSA's advice to seek professional help if signs of compassion fatigue continue for more than two weeks or affect quality of life. You don't need to reach any threshold to ask for help. If you're wondering, that's reason enough.

#### Item: When you're thinking of leaving
Many supervisors think about stepping back to a non-supervisory role or leaving altogether. It's not a failure. Ask Human Resources what's possible. Don't decide in the worst week of the year, if you can avoid it.

#### Item: If you're in danger
If you or someone else is in immediate danger or considering self-harm, call or text 988, or follow your emergency procedures. None of this requires telling your supervisor anything.

### Leader move: A small plan, made on a good day
**Control:** You control whether you have a plan before you need it. Make it on a day you're fine.
**Failure:** Do not wait until you need help to find out how to get it. Do not make your only support someone you supervise.
**Next:** Write three names or places: one peer, one confidential option and one for your own rights. Save them in your phone.

### Scenario
**Context:** You're a supervisor with a chronic health condition that has flared up over the past month. You've been coming in anyway and working evenings to make up for slower days. You have told no one. You've told your staff many times that they should ask for what they need. You worry that your manager, who's under a lot of budget pressure, will think you can't handle the role. A big stakeholder meeting is coming up.
**Prompt:** What's your next step?

**Option 1:** Keep going until after the stakeholder meeting, then deal with it.
**Response:** There's always a next big meeting. A condition that flares up may need a change now, and your work and health may both pay for the delay.

**Option 2:** Contact the ADA coordinator or Human Resources directly, ask what's possible, and share only what you choose. Then bring your manager the work plan.
**Response:** This is the best step. You've used the same route you'd tell staff to use, you've chosen what to share, and your manager gets a work plan and not a medical story.
**Recommended:** yes

**Option 3:** Tell your manager all about your health so they understand why you've been slow.
**Response:** You can choose to share, and some people do. But you don't have to give your manager medical details to get what you need, and starting with the process protects you.

**Option 4:** Ask one of your staff to take over the stakeholder meeting, and tell them why.
**Response:** You may want a backup for the meeting. But your staff shouldn't have to hold your health information, and they can't really say no to you.

### Knowledge check
**Question:** You want to talk with a peer supervisor about a hard personnel matter. Which approach fits this lesson?
- Share the employee's name so your peer can give specific advice.
- Describe the kind of situation with no names or unique details, and agree on what you'd each do if something sounds reportable. (correct)
- Type the details into an AI tool first and share the answer.
- Say nothing to anyone, because everything is confidential.
**Correct answer:** Describe the kind of situation with no names or unique details, and agree on what you'd each do if something sounds reportable.
**Feedback if correct:** Yes. You get real help and keep others' privacy. Reporting duties continue to apply.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Names and details don't belong there, and neither do outside tools. Saying nothing has its own cost. Describe the type of situation, without identifying anyone.

### Transfer prompt
**Prompt:** Write your plan: "If this job gets too heavy, then I will first talk to ____, I will use ____ for confidential support, and I will go to ____ about my own rights."
**Options:**
- If I'm carrying something I can't share, then I will call my peer and describe only the kind of situation.
- If my own health or caregiving is the issue, then I will contact the ADA coordinator or Human Resources directly.
- If I've worked evenings for two weeks, then I will book time with my manager.

## Job aid

**Title:** Your own load: a one-page check
**Subtitle:** Use it monthly, or whenever you notice you have stopped noticing.

**Purpose:** Help you see what you carry, turn it into something your manager can act on, and use support before you need it badly.

**Remember:**
- You are someone's staff too.
- "I'm overwhelmed" describes you. "Here are three things, and I can do two" describes the work.
- Delegating is fair only if something comes off their plate.
- You have the same rights you tell staff to use.

### Section: Check the five kinds of load
- My own work.
- My people.
- Things I can't talk about.
- Other people's hardest moments.
- Being the middle.
- What's circled that no one above me knows about?

### Section: Bring it to your manager
- What's happening.
- What it costs.
- The choices.
- What I recommend.
- What I need, and by when.
- Keep out staff names and medical details. Write down what was agreed.

### Section: Check your habits
- Am I doing someone's work with no end date?
- Am I the only channel for something that doesn't need me?
- Who got the last three things I handed off?
- Am I sending email at night?
- When did I last take leave?

### Section: My support plan
- One peer: no names, and clear rules.
- One confidential option: the Employee Assistance Program, or my own provider.
- One place for my rights: Human Resources, the ADA coordinator or my representative.
- In danger: call or text 988, or follow emergency procedures.

**Do next:** Choose one thing to drop, move or make visible this month. Put a date on it.
