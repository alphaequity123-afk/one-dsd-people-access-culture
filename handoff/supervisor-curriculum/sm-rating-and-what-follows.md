# sm-rating-and-what-follows: The Rating and What Follows From It

**Subtitle:** A fair rating can still lead to an unfair result. Learn to write the rating from evidence, and then watch the step where it turns into pay, awards and opportunity.
**Duration:** About 60 minutes across five lessons. Take them in short sittings.
**Content type:** practice
**Status:** reviewed
**Last reviewed:** October 31, 2026
**Evidence date:** October 2026
**Scope note:** This is general information as of October 2026, not legal advice. This course is voluntary and does not count as the training Minn. Stat. 43A.21 requires; the state's own training does that.

**Course objectives:**
- Trace what a rating leads to for the people you supervise.
- Write ratings from specific, year-round evidence.
- Keep medical, leave and protected activity out of the rating, and keep any effects on the record fair.
- Compare people on the same criteria at the point where a rating becomes a consequence.
- Run a review meeting that respects the employee's rights to respond and appeal.

**Applied next step:** Before your next review cycle, write down everything a rating affects in your unit. Ask Human Resources anything you cannot fill in. Start a simple evidence file for each person this week, so that the review does not rest on memory.

## Lesson 1: A rating is the start of something

**Summary:** Supervisors often treat the rating as the end of the process. This lesson shows what comes after it, using the MAPE contract as the worked example, and what research says about the gap between a rating and its reward.
**Minutes:** 11
**Objectives:**
- List the consequences that follow from a rating in your unit.
- Describe the MAPE contract's minimum appraisal requirements.
- Explain what Castilla's research found about ratings and pay growth.
- Identify the decision points you influence after the rating.

**Takeaways:**
- A rating feeds later decisions, including some that are not obviously linked.
- Equal ratings do not guarantee equal outcomes.
- Plan for the handoff, because that is where discretion lives.

### Text: Follow the rating

Picture a rating as the first link in a chain. You write it in the spring. Months later, it shapes who gets a step increase, who gets nominated for an award, who gets the stretch project, who gets encouraged to apply for the next job, and how someone reads the file when a problem arises.

You may not see those later moments as part of the same decision. People's careers do.

A study of one large firm's personnel records found that gaps in salary growth by gender, race and nationality persisted among employees with equal performance ratings. In other words, the rating looked even, and the money did not. That study is observational and from one private organization, so it does not tell you what happens at DHS. It does tell you where to look. A later study at one company reported that gaps in rewards shrank after accountability and transparency procedures were introduced. That was a before-and-after comparison, with no control group.

So this course does two things. It helps you write a fair rating. Then it follows the rating to the next step.

### Tabs: What the MAPE 2025-2027 contract says

**Tab: Annual appraisal**
Article 6, Section 3: at minimum, one annual performance appraisal between the employee and the person designated to review performance. Work plans, coaching sessions and letters of expectation do not substitute for it. Each appraisal states the overall level of performance. The rater signs it and must not be a member of the bargaining unit.

**Tab: Step increases**
Article 24, Section 5: employees may receive a one-step increase annually on their anniversary date, if the employer indicates satisfactory performance and the salary is below the range maximum. A step may be withheld, with written notice to the employee, because performance standards were not met or only marginally attained. If the employee does not get written notice before the anniversary date, the increase shall be granted.

**Tab: Achievement awards**
Article 24, Section 6: at the employer's discretion, an employee with outstanding performance may receive one award per fiscal year, as a lump sum up to $1,000 or a one-step adjustment. Awards in a fiscal year cannot go to more than 35 percent of employees authorized at the start of the fiscal year. Agencies may set up committees with employer and employee representatives to recommend procedures and criteria.

**Tab: Probation**
Article 16, Section 6: wherever practicable, an employee on probation gets at least one performance counseling review at about the midpoint.

**Tab: Other agreements**
This course read only the MAPE contract. If you supervise people covered by AFSCME or another agreement, or by the Managerial Plan, look up the equivalent terms there. Do not assume these carry over.

### Text: Your unit's list

Ask Human Resources and your manager what ratings feed in your unit. Your list might include step increases, awards, nominations for leadership development, who gets considered for acting roles, and informal recommendations you give when a colleague phones to ask about someone.

Some of these items are formal. Others are just your word. The informal ones matter as much. Minnesota's own performance practices and rating procedures were not read for this course, so ask how they work locally.

### Leader move: See the chain

**Control:** You control whether you know what your rating will be used for before you write it.
**Failure:** Do not treat a "Meets expectations" as neutral. Do not assume a colleague in Human Resources will catch a problem later on.
**Next:** Write down each thing a rating affects in your unit, and the date on which each decision gets made. Put the step increase notice dates on your calendar.

### Scenario

**Context:** You are reviewing your team's upcoming anniversaries. One consultant's step date is in three weeks. Their performance dropped in a few areas in the past six months, and you have talked about it twice but not in writing. You are thinking about whether to withhold the step. Another consultant with a similar record has a step date in six months.
**Prompt:** What do you do next?

**Option (recommended):** Call Human Resources or labor relations now. Ask what the standard and timeline are for withholding a step, review the evidence against the standard you would apply to anyone, and plan to treat the two consultants consistently.
**Response:** This is the strongest move. Under the MAPE contract, the employee must get written notice before the anniversary date or the increase is granted, and withholding can be grieved. Consistency matters because a similar record with a later date should get the same analysis.

**Option:** Let the step go through for now and raise the performance issue in the annual review.
**Response:** This may be the right outcome if the record does not support withholding. But you would be deciding by default, not by reviewing the standard. If the performance problem is real, the employee also deserves clear feedback sooner.

**Option:** Send a notice today that the step will be withheld, so that you do not miss the deadline.
**Response:** Meeting the deadline matters, but rushing a notice without checking the evidence or the standard risks a weak decision. You have not documented the earlier talks, and you have not considered whether anything like a pending accommodation request is behind the drop.

### Knowledge check

**Question:** Under the MAPE 2025-2027 contract, what happens if an employee does not get written notice before their anniversary date that a step increase will be withheld for performance?
**Options:**
- The step is delayed until the next annual review.
- The increase shall be granted. (correct)
- The supervisor can give notice within 30 days after the date.
- The employer must hold a hearing.

**Correct answer:** The increase shall be granted.
**Feedback if correct:** Correct, under Article 24, Section 5. Supervisors' substantive judgment about performance is not grievable, but withholding a step is.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Check Article 24, Section 5. Without written notice before the anniversary date, the increase shall be granted. Other agreements may differ.

### Transfer prompt

**Prompt:** Look at your review calendar. "If a rating decision is coming up for [person's role, not their name], then I will check [what it affects] and put [notice dates] on my calendar by [date]."
**Plan starters:**
- Ask Human Resources for the anniversary-date and notice process.
- Ask how awards and nominations work in your division.
- Note which contract covers each person.

## Lesson 2: Write it from evidence

**Summary:** Vague language gives bias room to work, and memory favors the recent and the memorable. This lesson gives you a way to collect evidence through the year and turn it into specific, comparable statements.
**Minutes:** 13
**Objectives:**
- Keep year-round evidence for every person using the same headings.
- Rewrite vague traits as observed work and effects.
- Test language for double standards.
- Tie each rating to position duties and stated expectations.

**Takeaways:**
- Same headings, same frequency, for everyone.
- If you cannot describe what you saw, you cannot fairly rate it.
- The same behavior should get the same word.

### Text: Language can carry a double standard

A study of Fortune 500 performance reviews looked at 88 language attributes. "Taking charge" was associated with the highest ratings for men but not for women. It is one correlational study, and it does not show what happens in any particular unit. Yet it shows something you can check in your own drafts: the same behavior may be read differently depending on who is doing it.

Another study, set in a university, found that moving from a 10-point to a 6-point teaching rating scale changed the gender gap in ratings in male-dominated fields. So even the design of a form may matter. You may not control the form. You do control the words you add.

### Tabs: Vague to specific

**Tab: "Lacks presence"**
What did you see? "In the March provider meeting, the update ran long and the key decision came last. The director asked for the decision twice." Next step: "Lead with the decision needed."

**Tab: "Not a team player"**
What did you see? "Twice this quarter, the file was not updated before handoff, and the next consultant had to call the county to find out the status." Next step: "Update the file before handoff, using the checklist."

**Tab: "Great attitude"**
Praise needs detail too. "You stayed with a family through a difficult transition, and you caught a missing authorization that would have stopped services." A compliment with no evidence does not help the person when the next promotion comes up.

**Tab: "Too direct" or "abrasive"**
Ask yourself what effect it had. "In two meetings, the way a concern was raised meant a provider stopped responding for a week." If there was no work effect, you may be describing style, and you should ask whether the comment belongs there at all.

### List: A year-round evidence file

- Use the same headings for each person: main duties, results, what people relied on them for, invisible work, development goals.
- Add one or two lines each month, right after a one-to-one.
- Note the date and the facts, and leave out your interpretation of why.
- Include good news and problems. A file that only has problems reads like a case being built.
- Count the work people do not get credit for, such as onboarding others, language help and covering for absent colleagues.
- Do not include diagnoses, leave reasons, Employee Assistance Program use, union activity or complaints.
- Ask Human Resources where to keep the file, because your notes can be government data.

### Accordion: Three checks before you finalize a sentence

**Item: The swap check**
Change the person's sex, race, age or disability status in your head. Would you use the same words for the same behavior?

**Item: The "seen it" check**
Can you name a date or a piece of work that supports it? If you only have a feeling, go find out, or take it out.

**Item: The recency check**
Does your evidence cover the entire period, or just the last two months? Does one memorable moment, good or bad, carry the rating?

### Statement

Rate what the person did, against what the job asked for. Write it so that the person can tell exactly what you saw.

### Leader move: Same headings for all

**Control:** You control what you write down, and how evenly.
**Failure:** Do not keep detailed notes on one person and almost none on another. Do not start your evidence file a week before review.
**Next:** Create the file this week. Add one line for each person after each one-to-one this month, and check whether any file is thinner than the others.

### Scenario

**Context:** You are drafting reviews for two policy specialists with similar results. In your drafts, you wrote that a man on the team "takes charge in meetings and pushes for decisions," with a high rating. You wrote that a woman on the team "can be forceful in meetings and needs to work on tone," with a lower rating. When you look at your notes, both have spoken over colleagues in meetings, and both have driven decisions through.
**Prompt:** What do you do next?

**Option:** Keep the drafts. Each reflects how the behavior landed with people, and you heard complaints about her tone.
**Response:** The way behavior lands can matter, but you are describing the same behavior differently. Complaints themselves may reflect the same double standard. Check the work effect for both before you use those words.

**Option:** Rewrite her review to match his, so the words are equal, and keep both ratings where they are.
**Response:** This changes the words but leaves the difference in rating unexplained. It also skips the real question of what each person did and with what effect.

**Option (recommended):** Rewrite both using the same headings: what decisions moved, who got talked over and what effect that had. Then revisit both ratings against the same expectation.
**Response:** You test the behavior, not the label. Both people may get credit for moving decisions, and both may get the same development note on letting others finish. The ratings then rest on evidence.

**Option:** Remove any mention of meeting behavior from both reviews, to avoid the problem.
**Response:** This avoids a hard conversation, and it takes away useful feedback and credit. Both people deserve to know what works and what to change.

### Knowledge check

**Question:** Which statement is ready for an appraisal?
**Options:**
- "Needs to show more leadership presence."
- "Is a pleasure to work with and a real asset."
- "In the May waiver update, the summary omitted the county review step, and two counties planned for the wrong date. Next time, check the summary against the review checklist." (correct)
- "Sometimes rubs people the wrong way."

**Correct answer:** "In the May waiver update, the summary omitted the county review step, and two counties planned for the wrong date. Next time, check the summary against the review checklist."
**Feedback if correct:** Correct. It names the situation, the work, the effect and the next step.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Look for a statement that names what happened, its effect on the work and what comes next. The others are hard to act on and easy to apply unevenly.

### Transfer prompt

**Prompt:** Consider your next draft. "If I catch myself using a word like [presence, attitude, tone or fit], then I will replace it with [what I saw and its effect], or take it out."
**Plan starters:**
- Which vague words do you tend to use?
- Whose file is thinnest right now?
- What invisible work is missing from your notes?

## Lesson 3: Leave, accommodation and protected activity

**Summary:** Some of the riskiest errors in a rating come from good intentions. This lesson covers how to rate someone who has been on leave, who has an accommodation or who has raised a concern.
**Minutes:** 12
**Objectives:**
- Keep medical and leave information off the appraisal.
- Rate the work done against fair expectations after a leave or accommodation.
- Recognize when a rating could look like retaliation.
- Know when to consult the ADA coordinator or Human Resources.

**Takeaways:**
- Do not mention the ADA, FMLA or the Employee Assistance Program on the form.
- Rate the work done, with the access the person actually had.
- A dropped rating after a complaint deserves a second look before it goes out.

### Text: What stays off the form

Article 6, Section 3 of the MAPE 2025-2027 contract says the employer "shall not reference the Employee Assistance Program, ADA or FMLA on the employee appraisal form." Minnesota law keeps medical information in separate confidential files (Minn. Stat. 363A.20, subd. 8). Check other agreements directly, but the safe habit applies everywhere: write about the work, not about the person's health or their leave.

This can be hard to do when you think you are helping. "Despite her health issues, she did very well" is meant kindly. It also places private information on an official record, and it suggests that her results should be seen in that light. Leave it out.

### Tabs: Harder situations

**Tab: Someone was on approved leave**
Rate the period the person worked, based on what they did. Adjust expected output for the time they were away, in the same way for anyone with approved absence. Do not write comments about attendance that actually describe protected leave. Ask Human Resources how to handle the review period and dates.

**Tab: An accommodation was delayed**
If a screen reader or an interpreter was late to arrive and output suffered, you are rating a person who did not have what they needed. Find out when the access was in place, and ask the ADA coordinator how to treat that period. Do not put the accommodation on the form. Look carefully at the expectation you are applying.

**Tab: Someone has an accommodation now**
Essential functions and performance standards still apply. Policy 1433 frames accommodation as enabling a person to perform essential functions. Rate the results against the standard. Do not rate how the person gets there, if the accommodation changes the method, and do not give extra credit out of sympathy.

**Tab: Someone made a report or filed a grievance**
Their rating should reflect the same evidence you would use for anyone. A lower evaluation than deserved is one of the EEOC's examples of retaliation. If this rating is lower than last year's, check that your evidence predates or is separate from the report, and ask Human Resources for a second look before it goes out.

**Tab: Someone disagreed with a training or equity effort**
The EEOC says opposing training can be protected, where there is a fact-specific basis for believing it violates Title VII. Federal guidance warns against penalizing dissent. Nobody's views go on an appraisal. Rate how the person treats colleagues and people served, and the work they do. If someone's conduct is an issue, describe the conduct.

### Text: Comparing the record over time

Before you submit any rating that has gone down, ask three questions:

1. What specifically changed in the work, and do I have dates?
2. Did anything happen in between, such as a leave, a request, a report or a new assignment?
3. Did this person get feedback along the way, or will the rating be a surprise?

If you have an answer you do not like to the second or third question, stop and call Human Resources.

### Leader move: The kind sentence that hurts

**Control:** You control whether well-meant comments about health or family make it onto a record.
**Failure:** Do not write "despite," "given their situation" or "considering everything they have been through."
**Next:** Search your drafts for those phrases. Replace each with a description of the work, or delete it.

### Scenario

**Context:** A case consultant returned from a three-month medical leave in June. Their accommodation, a modified documentation tool, was approved in June and arrived in September. You are drafting the annual review. For July and August, their documentation fell behind. Their results since September are strong. Your draft says, "Struggled to keep up after leave, but has improved."
**Prompt:** What do you do next?

**Option:** Keep it. It is accurate, it is mild, and it ends on a positive note.
**Response:** It ties the performance problem to the leave on an official record, and the months it describes are months when the approved tool had not arrived. Under the MAPE contract, the ADA and FMLA cannot be referenced on the form, and this phrase comes close to that in effect.

**Option (recommended):** Remove the reference to leave. Rate the work done against an expectation adjusted for the period away and the time when the approved tool was not yet in place. Ask the ADA coordinator or Human Resources how to treat that period.
**Response:** This is the strongest move. It rates the person on what they could be fairly expected to do, keeps private details off the form and gets advice on the part you are unsure about.

**Option:** Give the highest rating you can, to make up for a hard year.
**Response:** Sympathy is not evidence. An inflated rating is hard to compare with others', and it leaves the person without honest feedback. Rate what the evidence shows.

### Knowledge check

**Question:** Under the MAPE contract, which is not to be referenced on the appraisal form?
**Options:**
- The employee's overall level of performance.
- The employee's use of the ADA, FMLA or the Employee Assistance Program. (correct)
- Examples of work completed.
- The signature of the rater.

**Correct answer:** The employee's use of the ADA, FMLA or the Employee Assistance Program.
**Feedback if correct:** Correct. Article 6, Section 3 says the form shall not reference the Employee Assistance Program, the ADA or FMLA.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Check Article 6, Section 3. The overall level of performance and the rater's signature are required. The Employee Assistance Program, the ADA and FMLA are not to be referenced.

### Transfer prompt

**Prompt:** Think of someone whose year was not typical. Do not write their name. "If I am rating someone whose year included [a leave, a late accommodation or a report], then I will separate [the periods], check my evidence and ask [role] before I submit."
**Plan starters:**
- Search your drafts for "despite" and "given."
- Find out how Human Resources handles review periods that include leave.
- Identify which ratings have gone down since last year.

## Lesson 4: The handoff: from rating to reward

**Summary:** The rating is done, and now you recommend who gets what. Discretion is widest at this stage. This lesson teaches comparing people side by side, and using the same criteria for awards, nominations and recommendations.
**Minutes:** 12
**Objectives:**
- Set criteria for discretionary rewards before you consider people.
- Compare candidates jointly instead of one at a time.
- Avoid letting visibility stand in for performance.
- Keep your recommendations consistent with the ratings you gave.

**Takeaways:**
- Decide what the award is for before you decide who.
- Look at people side by side, on the same criteria.
- If two people have the same rating and get different results, you should be able to say why.

### Text: Why side by side

In a lab study, people who evaluated candidates jointly, comparing them with each other, moved away from stereotypes and toward performance. Evaluating people one at a time made stereotypes more likely to shape decisions. The study was not done in an agency, but the method costs nothing and it fits the handoff.

Another lab study, with people who had managerial experience, found that those told their company was meritocratic favored men in bonuses. So do not assume a focus on merit protects your decision. The one-company study mentioned earlier points to what may help: stating criteria in advance, comparing on the record and knowing that someone can ask why.

### List: The handoff check

1. **Name the decision.** Achievement award? Nomination? Acting role? Each has its own purpose.
2. **State the criteria.** For an achievement award under the MAPE contract, the term is "outstanding performance." Ask what that means locally, and if there is a committee or procedure.
3. **List everyone eligible.** Start from the roster, not from who comes to mind.
4. **Lay out the evidence side by side.** Use the same few lines for each person.
5. **Decide, and write the reason.**
6. **Compare with the ratings.** If someone with a lower rating gets the award over someone with a higher one, say why.
7. **Ask for a second look** from your manager, on the reasoning, before it goes further.

### Tabs: Where it slips

**Tab: Visibility**
The person who briefed the director gets remembered. The person who wrote the analysis does not. Check whether your evidence credits the work or the delivery.

**Tab: Remote and on-site**
You see some people more often. In one financial firm, more face time raised promotion rates. Ask whether your impression comes from results, or just from seeing someone more often. The same applies to staff in greater Minnesota and staff who work from home.

**Tab: Team awards and quiet work**
If your agency uses team awards, check who gets named. Also check whether case consultants who hold things together with families get credit in the same way as people on high-profile projects.

**Tab: Phone references**
A colleague asks, "What's this person like?" Your informal word is a consequence too. Stick to what is in the record, in the same way for everyone. Ask Human Resources what you may share.

### Leader move: Start from the roster

**Control:** You control whether your list begins with who you remember or with who is eligible.
**Failure:** Do not choose the winner first and then write the justification.
**Next:** The next time you recommend someone for anything, write out every eligible name in a private note, and give each one a line of evidence before you decide.

### Scenario

**Context:** You can recommend one person for an achievement award. Two consultants have the same rating. One has been on several high-profile projects you pitched them for, and the director knows them by name. The other works mostly from Duluth and handles the most complex cases on the team, with few complaints and strong results. Your first thought is the person the director knows, since it will be easier to get approved.
**Prompt:** What do you do next?

**Option:** Recommend the person the director knows. A recommendation has to get approved to matter, and you can recommend the other consultant next year.
**Response:** This makes visibility the criterion, and visibility came partly from your own past assignments. It also repeats the pattern next year, since the same advantage carries forward.

**Option:** Recommend the Duluth consultant, because they have been overlooked and this would make things up to them.
**Response:** This may turn out to be the right person, but "they're overdue" is not the criterion, and it could feel like a consolation prize. Base it on outstanding performance.

**Option (recommended):** Write what outstanding performance means here, and lay out each eligible person's evidence side by side. Recommend on that basis, explain the reasoning in writing, and tell the director what the complex casework achieved.
**Response:** This puts both on the same criteria and it makes the less visible work visible. It may still favor either person. If the evidence is truly even, say so, and ask how your division handles ties.

**Option:** Split the difference by asking if the award can be shared.
**Response:** You may be able to ask if your agency has team or modified awards, and it might be fair in some cases. But it lets you avoid the comparison, and the two did not work as a team.

### Knowledge check

**Question:** What does the research on joint evaluation suggest?
**Options:**
- Rating people one at a time prevents comparison bias.
- Comparing candidates side by side can shift decisions from stereotypes toward performance. (correct)
- Stressing meritocracy reliably removes bias from rewards.
- Equal ratings guarantee equal pay growth.

**Correct answer:** Comparing candidates side by side can shift decisions from stereotypes toward performance.
**Feedback if correct:** Correct. It is lab evidence, not a field trial, but it is a low-cost method to use at the handoff.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Look again. Lab evidence favors joint comparison. Other studies show that merit framing can backfire, and that equal ratings can come with unequal pay growth.

### Transfer prompt

**Prompt:** Think of the next reward or recommendation you control. "If I am recommending someone for [award, nomination or acting role], then I will write the criteria first, list everyone eligible, and compare evidence side by side before I decide."
**Plan starters:**
- Ask how awards work in your division, and if there is a committee.
- Draft what "outstanding" means for your team's work.
- Note whose work is least visible to your own manager.

## Lesson 5: The conversation, and the employee's rights

**Summary:** A rating should not be a surprise. This lesson covers the review meeting, what the employee can do next under the MAPE contract, and how to respond well when someone disagrees.
**Minutes:** 12
**Objectives:**
- Run a review meeting with no surprises.
- Explain the employee's rights to sign, respond and appeal under the MAPE contract.
- Respond to a written response or appeal without retaliating.
- Review your own process privately after the cycle.

**Takeaways:**
- Signing does not mean agreeing.
- A disagreement is the employee's right. It is not a mark against them.
- Check your own process once the cycle is done.

### Tabs: Rights under the MAPE 2025-2027 contract, Article 6, Section 3

**Tab: Signing**
Employees get the opportunity to sign, and signing "does not indicate acceptance or rejection of the appraisal." They receive a copy when they sign. If the employer adds comments after they sign, it must notify them.

**Tab: Written response**
The employee has 30 calendar days from receipt of the finalized appraisal to file a written response in their personnel file.

**Tab: Appeal**
Under Administrative Procedure 20, an employee may appeal their rating to the Appointing Authority within 30 days of the official date of rating. The decision of the Appointing Authority is final. At the employee's request, a union representative may be present during the appeal meetings.

**Tab: What is not grievable**
The supervisor's substantive judgment about performance is not grievable or arbitrable under Article 9. Withholding a step increase is grievable, as Article 24, Section 5 states. This does not mean the rating is beyond question, because the appeal route remains. Other agreements may differ, so check.

### List: A review meeting with no surprises

- Send the draft ahead if your practice allows it, so the person can prepare.
- Ask for their view first. "What are you proudest of? What would you do differently?"
- Go through the evidence, in specifics, including good work they may not know you noticed.
- For any problem, say what you have talked about before and when. If you never raised it, own that.
- Agree on next steps and any development plan. Under Administrative Procedure 21 excerpts in the MAPE contract, the individual development plan is developed jointly by the employee and the supervisor.
- Explain their options: signing does not mean agreeing, and they may respond in writing or appeal.
- Offer a follow-up. Some people need time to think before they answer.
- Make the meeting accessible. Book any interpreter early, and share documents in the format the person uses.

### Text: When someone disagrees

Someone may tell you that the rating is unfair, file a response or appeal. Treat that as an ordinary part of the process. Listen and ask for specifics. If they point to evidence you missed, you can look at it. If they say the rating is discrimination or retaliation, your duty under Policy 1436 applies: report it immediately to Human Resources or the Affirmative Action Officer.

Watch your own reaction over the following weeks. Are you scrutinizing this person's work more? Are you passing over them for assignments? The EEOC lists more scrutiny and less desirable moves among its examples of retaliation. Keep managing them, and keep your reasons tied to the work.

### Text: After the cycle: a private process check

Once the cycle closes, look at your own process. This takes about 20 minutes, involves no names or demographics, and stays private.

- Were the criteria stated before you wrote the reviews?
- Do all files have similar amounts of evidence?
- Did the words differ for the same behavior?
- Did the handoff follow the ratings, or can you explain why not?
- Did anyone's rating surprise them?
- Who reviewed your reasoning?

If your division reviews patterns across supervisors, that happens at another level. Your own job is to bring a clean process. No check here proves that your ratings are fair, and a good process can still miss things. Treat each answer as something to improve next time.

### Leader move: The right to respond

**Control:** You control how a person experiences using their right to respond.
**Failure:** Do not ask someone to withdraw a response. Do not say you are disappointed that they filed one.
**Next:** Add one sentence to your review meetings: "You can sign, respond in writing or appeal. Any of those is fine, and it won't change how I work with you." Then make sure that is true.

### Scenario

**Context:** A case consultant receives a lower rating than last year. You have dates and examples, and you discussed them during the year. The consultant files a written response that says the rating is unfair and that you favor staff who socialize with you. It does not mention any protected trait. Your manager reads it and tells you, "I'd keep an eye on that one." A stretch assignment is coming up, and the consultant meets the criteria.
**Prompt:** What do you do next?

**Option (recommended):** Treat the response as a normal right. Consider whether the claim about socializing has anything to it. Apply your criteria to the stretch assignment as usual, and if the consultant raises discrimination at any point, report it immediately.
**Response:** This is the strongest move. It keeps the consultant's access to the assignment tied to the criteria and takes the concern seriously as a question about your process. You can also ask your manager what "keep an eye on" means, and ask Human Resources if it seems to mean closer scrutiny.

**Option:** Skip the consultant for the stretch assignment for now. Things are tense, and you can tell them it is to take pressure off.
**Response:** Being passed over right after filing a response could look like a penalty, even though the kind explanation is sincere. Let the criteria decide.

**Option:** Ask them to meet with you and withdraw the response, since you discussed all of this during the year.
**Response:** The contract gives them the right to respond. Asking them to withdraw it could feel like pressure. If you want to discuss it, listen and do not ask for anything.

**Option:** Start keeping closer notes on the consultant's work, as your manager suggested.
**Response:** Increasing scrutiny is one of the EEOC's examples of retaliation where someone has engaged in protected activity. This response may or may not be protected, but the habit is unwise either way. Keep the same level of notes you keep on everyone.

### Knowledge check

**Question:** Under the MAPE contract, what does an employee's signature on an appraisal mean?
**Options:**
- The employee agrees with the rating.
- The employee waives the right to appeal.
- It does not indicate acceptance or rejection of the appraisal. (correct)
- The rating has been approved by the union.

**Correct answer:** It does not indicate acceptance or rejection of the appraisal.
**Feedback if correct:** Correct, under Article 6, Section 3. The employee may still respond in writing within 30 calendar days of receipt, and may appeal under Administrative Procedure 20.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Check Article 6, Section 3. Signing does not indicate acceptance or rejection. The employee keeps the right to respond and appeal.

## Job aid

**Title:** From rating to what follows
**Subtitle:** Use before, during and after a review cycle.

**Purpose:** To help you write a rating from evidence and follow it through to the decisions it affects.

**Remember:**
- Equal ratings do not guarantee equal results. Watch the handoff.
- Same headings and same amount of evidence for everyone.
- Keep the ADA, FMLA, the Employee Assistance Program and health details off the form.
- A response or an appeal is a right. It is not a mark against anyone.
- These contract terms come from MAPE 2025-2027. Check other agreements directly.

**Section: All year**
- One or two lines per person after each one-to-one.
- Include invisible work, good news and problems.
- Raise problems when they happen, so the rating is no surprise.

**Section: Before you submit**
- Swap check: same words for the same behavior?
- Seen-it check: can you name a date or piece of work?
- Recency check: does your evidence cover the whole period?
- Lower than last year? Check what changed, what happened in between and whether the person has heard this before.
- Any leave, late accommodation, report or grievance in the period? Call Human Resources or the ADA coordinator.

**Section: At the handoff**
- Name the decision, and state its criteria.
- Start from the roster of everyone eligible.
- Compare evidence side by side.
- Check against the ratings, write the reason and get a second look.
- MAPE step increases: written notice before the anniversary date, or the increase shall be granted.

**Section: The meeting and after**
- Ask for their view first. Share evidence and next steps.
- Signing does not mean agreeing. Written response: 30 calendar days from receipt. Appeal: 30 days from the official date of rating. Representative may attend the appeal if they ask.
- Report any discrimination or retaliation claim immediately to Human Resources or the Affirmative Action Officer.
- Keep their assignments and your note-taking the same as for everyone else.

**Do next:** Start an evidence file with the same headings for each person this week.
