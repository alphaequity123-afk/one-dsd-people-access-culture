# sm-facilitator-guide: Running an Optional Live Practice Session

**Subtitle:** A guide for anyone who wants to host a voluntary, self-organized practice session for supervisors and managers. It includes an invitation, an agenda, rehearsal prompts and what to say when things get hard.
**Duration:** 63 minutes of reading across 5 lessons, plus optional sessions of about 90 minutes
**Content type:** practice
**Status:** reviewed
**Last reviewed:** October 31, 2026
**Evidence date:** October 2026

**Course objectives:**
- Set up a practice session with no records, no attendance, no identity-based breakouts and no required disclosure.
- Plan access before you invite anyone.
- Run a rehearsal in which people practice a real decision and get useful feedback.
- Respond to harmful comments, disclosures and disagreement.
- End the session so that people leave with a private plan and nothing is kept.

**Applied next step:** Choose one decision your peers will face soon, such as an accommodation conversation. Book 90 minutes, plan access, and send the invitation in Lesson 2.

## Lesson 1: What this session is

**Summary:** Why people meet to practice, the ground rules, and who should host.
**Minutes:** 12

**Objectives:**
- Explain why practice and feedback are the point.
- State the ground rules in your own words.
- Decide whether you are the right person to host.

**Takeaways:**
- Practice, feedback and peers are the reason to meet.
- There are no records, no attendance, no breakouts by identity and no required disclosure.
- The host shouldn't be anyone's boss in the room, if that can be helped.

### Text: Why meet at all

Self-paced lessons can carry knowledge and first practice. Training that changes what people do appears to need more. Reviews of leadership training favor practice, feedback and spaced sessions, though these come from comparisons across studies, not head-to-head tests. Web and classroom delivery came out equal when the methods were the same, so what matters is the method, not the room. A review of behavior modeling found that transfer was greatest with good and bad examples, scenarios generated by trainees themselves, and goal setting. Research on transfer reports that peer support predicted motivation to use what you learned, and feedback and coaching predicted actually using it. None of this tested a session like this one. No study shows that it will reduce disparities. It's a reasonable way to practice hard conversations before you have them.

### List: Ground rules

- Voluntary. Anyone can come, or skip it, or leave part way, without any explanation.
- No attendance. There's no sign-in and no list of who came. Nobody reports to a supervisor.
- No records. No recording, no transcript, no AI notetakers, no minutes and no scoring. Nothing is collected.
- No identity-based breakouts. Pairs and small groups form at random or by the decision people want to practice. As of October 2026, the EEOC says separating employees by protected characteristic can be unlawful even with identical programming.
- No required disclosure. Nobody has to share their identity, their health, their bias or their story. Nobody is asked to confess or affirm a belief.
- Disagreement is welcome. It carries no consequence.
- Nobody speaks for a group.
- No real names, cases or complaints. Change the details if you're practicing something real.

### Statement

The session practices behavior. It does not assess anyone, and it does not count toward anything. It isn't required training and does not meet Minn. Stat. 43A.21.

### Tabs: Who should host

#### Tab: A good host

Someone who can keep time, ask questions and stay calm. They don't need to be an expert. Peers can take turns. A person from another unit may be better, because people can speak more freely.

#### Tab: Be careful if

You supervise someone in the room, or you're senior to them. They may not feel free to skip it, disagree or be bad at something in front of you. If you must host, say this openly, and offer a different session.

#### Tab: Not a job for

Anyone asked to host because of their identity or disability. If someone volunteers, fine. Don't assume. If people contribute lived experience to help design something, pay or credit them. Don't ask them for free.

### Text: Where you can get help

If you need to know whether the session can run on work time, ask your manager or Human Resources. If your group includes MAPE-represented staff, note that Article 6 of the 2025-2027 contract addresses training. Check other contracts and labor relations as needed. This course doesn't describe AFSCME, MMA or Managerial Plan terms. This is general information as of October 2026, not legal advice.

### Leader move: Invite in a way that makes "no" easy

**Control:** You control how the invitation sounds. If it comes from a boss, it feels like an expectation.
**Failure:** A manager says, "I'd really like to see all of you there." From then on, it's mandatory in all but name.
**Next:** Send the invitation through a peer, or word it so that nobody needs to reply and nobody will be asked.

### Scenario

**Context:** You've offered to run a practice session for supervisors in your section. A manager thanks you, and asks you to send a list of who comes, so that she can recognize them at her staff meeting and encourage the others.
**Prompt:** What do you do next?

**Option:** Send the list, since it's meant as praise.
**Response:** It's still an attendance list given to a manager. Those who didn't come get noticed as well. It breaks the ground rules and turns the session into something people watch.

**Option:** Agree, and just leave out anyone who asks not to be listed.
**Response:** That still tells the manager who came, and makes people ask you for special treatment. There shouldn't be a list at all.

**Option (recommended):** Say you don't keep a list and won't, and explain why. Offer a different way she can help: free up time, pay for access, and tell supervisors that it's optional and she won't ask.
**Response:** This keeps the promise and gives the manager a real way to support her people. It also shows that the rule applies to everyone.

### Knowledge check

**Question:** Which plan for grouping people fits these ground rules?
**Options:**
- Separate rooms for staff of color and white staff, so people feel comfortable.
- A group for disabled supervisors, and one for everyone else.
- Random pairs, or pairs chosen by which decision people want to practice.
- Groups chosen by the host, based on what the host knows about each person.

**Correct answer:** Random pairs, or pairs chosen by which decision people want to practice.
**Feedback if correct:** Right. It's open to everyone, it asks nobody to identify themselves and it focuses on the work.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Look for the answer that doesn't sort people by identity. The EEOC position as of October 2026 warns against separating employees by protected characteristic.

### Transfer prompt

**Prompt:** Think about who could host in your area. Finish this plan: "If I host a session for ____, then I'll deal with the fact that ____ is in the room by ____."

## Lesson 2: Access and the invitation

**Summary:** Plan access first, describe the content specifically and send an invitation that asks for nothing.
**Minutes:** 13

**Objectives:**
- Plan communication and physical access ahead of time.
- Handle access requests without creating a record of who needs what.
- Write an invitation that describes the content honestly.

**Takeaways:**
- Book access by default, so that nobody has to ask or reveal anything.
- Ask the person what works. Don't assume one method works for everyone.
- Say specifically what will happen in the session, so that people can decide for themselves.

### List: Access to plan by default

- Live captioning by a CART provider. Automated captions can help, but don't assume they will be accurate enough for a fast group discussion.
- Sign language interpreters. Book early, and give them the materials and the names of any special terms ahead of time.
- Materials sent ahead in accessible formats, including tagged documents and plain text. Everything should work with a screen reader.
- Describe out loud anything shown on screen or on a wall.
- A room that works for wheelchairs and other mobility devices, with clear paths and seating choices. For online sessions, check that the platform works with screen readers and that interpreters can be pinned or spotlighted.
- Breaks. For a 90-minute session, offer at least one. People should be free to move, stand or step out.
- One person speaks at a time. Say your name before speaking. Allow time for interpretation before moving on.

### Accordion: Deaf, DeafBlind and hard of hearing colleagues

#### Item: Ask, and don't assume

Hearing loss and Deaf experience vary a lot. Some people use ASL, some use captions, some use hearing technology, some use several. DeafBlind people use a range of methods, and may have their own support providers or preferred interpreters. Ask each person what works, and honor it as far as you can.

#### Item: In the room

Speak to the person, not the interpreter. Don't ask the interpreter for their opinion or ask them to take part. Keep pairs and small groups practical: an interpreter can only be in one place, so plan the number of interpreters around the breakouts.

#### Item: Rehearsals

A person using an interpreter should get the same practice, with the same time and same feedback. Don't make them the case study. Do not have anyone simulate being Deaf, blind or disabled. Disability simulations don't work on the data and they aren't part of these sessions.

#### Item: Whom to ask

Ask your ADA coordinator how interpreters and CART get arranged and who pays. Published sources on this were thin, so listen to the people who use these services and those who provide them.

### Text: Handling access requests without a record

Booking access by default means that fewer people have to ask. When someone asks for something else, thank them, ask what would work and do it. Keep only what you need to arrange it, and delete it after the session. Do not tell others who asked. Do not ask why. If someone needs something beyond what you can provide, go to your ADA coordinator without naming them, unless they want you to.

### Text: Invitation, ready to use

"**Optional practice session: [the decision, such as 'the first conversation when someone asks for an accommodation'].** [Date, time, place or link.] 90 minutes.

What we'll do: practice this conversation in pairs, using made-up situations or ones you bring with the details changed. You'll get feedback from peers. You can choose to observe instead.

What we will cover: [be specific, such as 'accommodation requests, medical privacy and what to say when you don't know the answer'].

This is optional. There's no sign-in, no list and no notes, and nobody will be told who came. You don't need to reply. You don't have to share anything personal. It doesn't count as required training. Please don't bring real names or open cases.

Access: CART and ASL interpreters are booked. Materials will come out by [date], and they're available here: [link]. If there's anything else that would help you take part, tell [host's name] by [date]. You don't have to say why. We delete access requests after the session.

If you need to step out at any time, go ahead."

### Text: Why be specific

Generic warnings don't help anyone decide. People should know what's going to be discussed. If the session involves a report of harassment, say so in the invitation. Let people pause, leave or choose a different path without penalty and without any record. That's a design judgment from the research for this course. It hasn't been tested, but it does fit the rest of this app.

### Leader move: Spend money on access before anything else

**Control:** If you control any budget at all, you control whether access gets paid for.
**Failure:** Everyone assumes someone else will book CART. It is requested a day before, and a colleague is asked to take notes for the Deaf participant.
**Next:** Find out now who arranges and pays for interpreters and CART. Do this before you pick a date.

### Scenario

**Context:** You've sent an invitation for a session next week. A colleague who's hard of hearing, and who has never told you about it directly, is on the invitation list. A coworker says you should ask that person if they'll need anything, or else offer to have someone sit next to them and take notes. You have not booked CART because you didn't think you'd need it.
**Prompt:** What do you do next?

**Option:** Ask the colleague privately if they need CART, and explain that you heard they're hard of hearing.
**Response:** This reveals that people have been talking about them, and it makes their access depend on disclosure. It would be better if the session simply had CART.

**Option (recommended):** Book CART now for the session, for anyone who may benefit, and update the invitation to say so. Invite anyone to contact you about anything else. Don't single out the colleague.
**Response:** This gives access without asking for disclosure. It's useful for many people, including anyone who processes text better or who's in a noisy room.

**Option:** Arrange for a coworker to take notes next to them.
**Response:** Notes aren't access to a live discussion, and this singles them out. It also takes a coworker out of the session and assumes something nobody asked for.

**Option:** Do nothing unless they ask.
**Response:** It puts the work on the person who needs access, and a late request may be impossible to fill. Planning ahead is the host's job.

### Knowledge check

**Question:** Someone emails you to ask for materials in a different format. What do you do with the request after the session?
**Options:**
- Keep it in a file so you know for next time.
- Share it with the group, so that people can help.
- Delete it, unless the person asks you to remember.
- Forward it to their supervisor.

**Correct answer:** Delete it, unless the person asks you to remember.
**Feedback if correct:** Right. Keep what you need to arrange access, and no more. The person can tell you again next time, or ask you to remember.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Look for the option that keeps no record. Access requests can reveal health information and shouldn't be passed around.

### Transfer prompt

**Prompt:** Finish this plan: "If I'm going to host a session on ____, then by ____ I will find out from ____ how to book CART and interpreters, and I will send materials by ____."

## Lesson 3: Running the session

**Summary:** A 90-minute agenda, rehearsal prompts for each decision, and how to give feedback.
**Minutes:** 14

**Objectives:**
- Run the 90-minute agenda.
- Set up rehearsal by role, with no one playing a disability or an identity.
- Give feedback that's specific and about behavior.

**Takeaways:**
- Most of the time goes to practice, not to talking about practice.
- Play job roles. Do not play identities or disabilities.
- Show a good and a poor example, and let people bring their own situations with details changed.

### Text: Opening script, ready to use

"Welcome. This is optional, so step out whenever you need to. We don't keep a list or any notes. Nothing goes to anyone's supervisor. You don't have to share anything personal, and please don't use real names or open cases. If you're a supervisor or manager and you hear about harassment or discrimination, you still have a duty to report it, including here, so keep that in mind. I'm not grading anyone. You can practice, observe or swap roles. Disagreement is fine."

### List: Agenda, 90 minutes

- 10 minutes: Opening script, and how the session works.
- 10 minutes: Show two examples, one poor and one good, of the same conversation. The host and a volunteer can read them. Ask, "What changed?"
- 10 minutes: Have people write what they'd actually say, first sentence only. Keep it private unless they want to share.
- 15 minutes: First rehearsal, in threes. One plays the supervisor, one plays the other role, one observes. Then feedback.
- 5 minutes: Break.
- 15 minutes: Swap roles. Second rehearsal with a harder version.
- 10 minutes: Third rehearsal, or bring your own, with details changed.
- 10 minutes: Whole group: What was harder than you thought? What would you need from your manager?
- 5 minutes: Private if-then plan. Close. Clear the walls.

### Accordion: Rehearsal prompts, ready to use

#### Item: Accommodation

Role: a staff member says, "Since my treatment started, I can't get through the afternoon in this office. Could I come in earlier?" You're the supervisor. Practice: respond without asking for a diagnosis, ask what would help, explain the next step and say when you'll come back to them. Harder version: you've already got a schedule rule, and two others have asked for the same shift.

#### Item: A report of harm

Role: a staff member says a senior colleague keeps mocking their accent, and asks you not to do anything about it. You're the supervisor. Practice: thank them, explain that you must report, say what happens next and avoid promising secrecy. Harder version: they're angry with you for reporting.

#### Item: Assignments

Role: two staff members want the same high-profile assignment. Practice: explain the criteria and your decision to the one you didn't choose. Then run it again with the people swapped, and see if your reasons still hold. Harder version: the person you didn't choose asks if it's because of who they are.

#### Item: Feedback

Role: you've got to tell a consultant that their summary left out two open items, which led to a wrong approval. Practice: say what happened, why it mattered and what's next. Harder version: the consultant says you only criticize them and not others.

#### Item: A review

Role: you're preparing to tell someone they've got a middle rating, when they expected more. Practice: use evidence from the whole year, and say what the top level would need. Harder version: they've got a recent accommodation, and they think it's related.

#### Item: Corrective action

Role: you're preparing for a first meeting about a repeated missed deadline. Practice: state the expectation, what you saw and what happens next, and respond when the person asks for a union representative. Harder version: the person says they recently filed a complaint and this is retaliation.

#### Item: A manager and a supervisor

Role: you're the manager. A supervisor says, "I told them to get a doctor's note first." Practice: ask questions from Backing Your Supervisors, and move the request along. Harder version: the supervisor says Human Resources is too slow.

#### Item: Disagreement

Role: a supervisor says at a team meeting, "I think all this equity stuff has gone too far." You're their peer or manager. Practice: listen, ask what they've seen and focus on what the duty requires them to do, with no demand that they change their view. Harder version: someone on the team is upset by it.

### List: Rules for rehearsal

- Play job roles only, such as supervisor, staff member or panel chair.
- Do not ask anyone to play a disability, a race, an accent, a religion or any other identity. Do not put on voices.
- Let people choose whether to practice or to observe.
- Stop any rehearsal if someone asks to. No explanation needed.
- If someone brings their own situation, have them change names and details first, so nobody could be recognized.
- Do not ask anyone to be the example of how a group experiences something.

### Tabs: Feedback that helps

#### Tab: For the observer

Say what you heard, as close to the words as possible. "You said, 'What would help?' right away, and the person relaxed." Then one thing to try next.

#### Tab: What to avoid

Comments on the person's character, or guesses at their beliefs. Anything like "You seem biased." Scores of any kind. A rehearsal is feedback only, and nothing gets written down or sent to anyone.

#### Tab: For the person practicing

Speak first. "What would you do differently?" Then listen. You can ignore any advice that won't fit you.

#### Tab: For the host

Walk around or visit rooms and listen. Ask, "What's the first sentence you'd try?" Keep to the time. Make sure people swap roles. The strongest learning usually comes from trying it twice.

### Leader move: Protect the practice time

**Control:** You control the clock.
**Failure:** Discussion is more comfortable than practice, so talk fills the time. People go home having talked about accommodation, without ever having said a sentence aloud.
**Next:** When a discussion runs long, say, "Let's try it. Who'll play the supervisor?"

### Scenario

**Context:** In a rehearsal on accommodation, someone suggests that a participant who uses a wheelchair should play the employee, because it would be more realistic. Another person offers to put on a blindfold to play a blind employee, "so we really get it." The participant who uses a wheelchair hasn't said anything.
**Prompt:** What do you do next?

**Option:** Ask the participant in the wheelchair whether they'd be willing, since it's their choice.
**Response:** Asking in front of the group makes it hard to say no. It also makes them the example, which is not what they came for.

**Option (recommended):** Say, "We play job roles here, and no one plays a disability or stands in for a group. Everyone gets the same practice." Assign roles at random. Skip the blindfold and move on.
**Response:** It stops both ideas quickly, and it doesn't put the participant on the spot. Disability simulations aren't supported by the data, and these sessions don't use them.

**Option:** Let the blindfold idea go ahead, because the person means well.
**Response:** Meaning well isn't enough. Research doesn't support simulation as effective, and it can make disability look like a performance.

### Knowledge check

**Question:** Which is the best observer feedback?
**Options:**
- "You came across as a bit biased."
- "You said 'What would help you do the job?' before anything else, and then you stopped and listened. Next time, say when you'll get back to them."
- "I'd give that a seven out of ten."
- "Great job."

**Correct answer:** "You said 'What would help you do the job?' before anything else, and then you stopped and listened. Next time, say when you'll get back to them."
**Feedback if correct:** Right. It names what was said and offers one thing to try.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Look for the answer that quotes behavior and offers a next step. Scores and judgments about character don't belong in these sessions.

### Transfer prompt

**Prompt:** Choose the prompt closest to what your peers face. Finish this plan: "If I run a session on ____, then I will use ____ as the harder version, and I'll make sure people try it aloud at least twice."

## Lesson 4: When things get hard

**Summary:** What to say when someone says something harmful, discloses something, disagrees, gets upset or dominates.
**Minutes:** 13

**Objectives:**
- Respond to a harmful comment without shaming or ignoring it.
- Respond to a disclosure, within the limits of the reporting duty.
- Make room for disagreement without penalty.

**Takeaways:**
- Have your words ready beforehand.
- Address the behavior and go back to the job.
- If someone describes harassment or discrimination, the reporting duty applies to supervisors and managers in the room.

### Text: What the setting says, as of October 2026

Reported cases suggest that what happens around a training matters, as well as what's in it. *Young* (10th Cir., 2026) reportedly rejected a hostile-environment claim based on a single training, though the court said race-based rhetoric could matter. *Diemert* (9th Cir., September 2026) reportedly sent a human services employee's claim to trial, based on trainings plus about ten incidents over five years, including manager comments. Neither binds Minnesota, and these opinions weren't read for this course. The EEOC says that objecting to training can be protected activity, given a specific basis for believing it violates Title VII. So you don't need to be afraid of disagreement. Deal with it fairly, and never let it cost anyone. In your sessions, avoid generalizing about any group, including majority groups, and stop others from doing so.

### Accordion: What to say, ready to use

#### Item: A generalization about a group

"Let's stay away from statements about what any group is like. What's the decision in front of you, and what would you do?" If it was harmful to someone in the room, you can add, "That's not how we're going to talk here." Then go on. Follow up privately afterward, if appropriate.

#### Item: A comment aimed at someone in the room

"Stop there. That's not okay here. We don't talk about each other that way." Check privately with the person who was targeted, and let them decide what they want. If what happened may be harassment or discrimination, and you're a supervisor or manager, report it.

#### Item: Someone says, "Tell us how your people feel about this"

"Nobody here speaks for a group, and nobody's going to be asked to. What would any of us do as supervisors?" Then move on, without making the person respond.

#### Item: Someone describes harassment or discrimination on their team

Before they go further: "I'd like to hear this, and I want to be straight with you. If you tell me, I have a duty to report it to Human Resources or the Affirmative Action Officer, and others here may too." Offer to step out with them. After the session, make the report if you've got the duty. The report goes to those offices. It isn't a record of the session.

#### Item: Someone shares a health condition or personal story

Thank them, keep going and don't press. Remind everyone, "What people share stays here." Be honest that you can't guarantee it, since it's a group. Don't follow up in front of others.

#### Item: Disagreement

"Thank you for saying so. What have you seen that makes you think that?" Then, "Whatever we each think, here's the duty, and here's the decision. What would you do?" You're not asking anyone to agree. Do not repeat what they said to anyone else, and don't let it follow them.

#### Item: Someone is upset or leaves

Let them. Nobody needs to explain. Don't send anyone after them, unless it's plainly needed. Afterward, one quiet message is enough: "No need to reply. I'm glad to talk if you'd like." Don't make it about the session.

#### Item: A senior person dominates

"Let's hear from someone who hasn't spoken yet." Or give the senior person a role as an observer. If you can, talk to them before the session about what their presence does to the room.

#### Item: Someone presses others to confess or affirm a belief

"We don't ask anyone to affirm a belief or confess anything here. We practice what to do."

### List: Before each session, decide

- What will I say if someone makes a harmful comment?
- Who could I step out with if someone is upset?
- Am I someone with a reporting duty, and do I know whom to call?
- Do I have anyone to talk to after a hard session?

### Leader move: Respond quickly and briefly

**Control:** You control what happens in the first ten seconds after a comment.
**Failure:** A host freezes, or else gives a five-minute lecture. Either way, the person who was harmed carries it, and the group learns not to speak.
**Next:** Practice one short sentence aloud before you host. It should stop the comment and go back to the job.

### Scenario

**Context:** During a rehearsal debrief, a participant says, "Honestly, I think this whole topic treats people like me as the problem, and I've been told as much at past trainings." Someone else rolls their eyes and says, "This is exactly what we are here to get past." The room goes quiet. Both are supervisors.
**Prompt:** What do you do next?

**Option:** Tell the first person that this isn't the right place for that view, and go back to the rehearsal.
**Response:** You'd be punishing disagreement in the very place you said it's welcome. It may also confirm what they've just said.

**Option (recommended):** Thank the first person, and ask what happened at those trainings that made them feel that way. Tell the second person that nobody in this room is a problem to get past. Then bring it back to a decision: "Whatever we think about the topic, what do you want a supervisor to do in this situation?"
**Response:** This takes the concern seriously, stops the dismissive comment and treats both as colleagues. It doesn't ask anyone to change their view, and nothing about it follows them out of the room.

**Option:** Stop the session, because it's gotten too tense.
**Response:** Tension doesn't mean the session has failed. Ending it teaches people that disagreement is dangerous. You can slow down and keep going.

**Option:** Take the first person aside at the break to explain why the topic matters.
**Response:** Asking what they've experienced might be useful. Trying to convert them isn't. The session teaches behavior, not belief.

### Knowledge check

**Question:** A participant starts to describe ongoing harassment on their team. You are a manager and are hosting. What should you do?
**Options:**
- Listen, keep it confidential, since this session has no records.
- Tell them before they go further that you have a duty to report, offer to step out, and report it to Human Resources or the Affirmative Action Officer afterward.
- Ask the group for advice on how to handle it.
- Write up what they said and send it to their supervisor.

**Correct answer:** Tell them before they go further that you have a duty to report, offer to step out, and report it to Human Resources or the Affirmative Action Officer afterward.
**Feedback if correct:** Right. Being honest up front lets them choose. The no-records rule doesn't change the policy duty, and the report goes only to those offices.
**Feedback if incorrect:** The session's rules don't suspend the reporting duty under Policies 1436 and 1329. Look for the answer that's honest about the duty and sends the report to the right place.

### Transfer prompt

**Prompt:** Finish this plan: "If someone in my session says something that harms another person, then my first sentence will be ____. If someone begins to describe harassment, then I'll say ____."

## Lesson 5: Closing, and what happens afterward

**Summary:** How to finish so that people have a plan, nothing is kept and the practice can continue.
**Minutes:** 11

**Objectives:**
- Close with a private if-then plan.
- Leave no record.
- Improve the next session without tracking anyone.

**Takeaways:**
- The if-then plan belongs to the person who wrote it. Nobody collects it.
- Clear the room, the chat and any shared documents.
- Ask for usefulness out loud, or not at all. Don't report it as success.

### Text: Why an if-then plan

An if-then plan names a real upcoming situation and what you'll do when it comes. In general behavior research, these plans have shown strong effects on reaching goals, though they haven't been tested for training transfer in what was reviewed for this curriculum. They cost nothing and they might help. They should stay private. Some participants may want to tell a peer, and that's fine, but it's their choice.

### Text: Closing script, ready to use

"Take two minutes. Think of one real situation coming up, and write: 'If this happens, then I will do this.' Keep it. Don't use names. I won't collect it. If you want to, tell someone here what you're trying, and check in with them later. Thanks for practicing. Apart from any report a duty requires, nothing from today goes anywhere. I'll clear the walls and chat. If anything today raises something you want to talk about, I'm available, and your ADA coordinator, Human Resources or your union can help with the specifics."

### List: Clearing up

- Take down all flip charts and wipe the boards.
- For online sessions, turn off recording and transcription before you start. Delete the chat at the end, if your platform allows. Check that no AI notetaker joined.
- Delete any list of access requests and any scheduling list of who said they'd come.
- If you use a shared document for prompts, keep it free of names, and clear anything people typed.
- Do not write a report of who came or what was said. If someone asks, say the session happened and what the topic was.

### Accordion: Improving next time, without tracking anyone

#### Item: Ask out loud

"What's one thing to keep, and one to change?" Don't write down who said what. If you ask about reactions at all, ask about usefulness, not about how much people liked it.

#### Item: Do not use it as evidence

Reactions don't tell you whether anyone learned anything. Don't report attendance or satisfaction as progress, and don't claim that the session changed anything. If you're asked, say what you did and that it hasn't been measured.

#### Item: Keep the next one close to real work

Pick the next topic based on what's coming up, such as a review cycle, a reorganization or a budget change. Spaced practice is better supported than a single long event.

#### Item: Rotate the host

Peers may run it next time. That keeps it from depending on one person, and it helps with your own load, too.

#### Item: Report a harmful scenario

If a scenario here or in a course seemed harmful or wrong, let the people who maintain the materials know. Don't include any details about who was in the session.

### Text: What you can honestly say

You can say, "A few of us have been practicing accommodation conversations. It's optional and we keep no records." You can't say, "Our supervisors have been trained", because you don't know who came. You also can't say it satisfies a requirement, because it doesn't. Don't suggest the problem has been handled. Lab research suggests that fairness signals like that can make people less willing to see real problems.

### Leader move: Keep the promise even when it costs you

**Control:** You control whether "no records" is true after the session, when someone important asks.
**Failure:** The host keeps a small private note, just in case. A few months later, someone asks for it and it's handed over. The next session is empty.
**Next:** Before you host, decide what you'll say if a senior leader asks who came. Practice saying, "I don't know. I didn't keep track, and I told people I wouldn't."

### Scenario

**Context:** A few weeks after your session, a division leader tells you that the division is writing its annual report on equity training. They ask if you could provide attendance figures and a sentence saying supervisors have completed live practice on accommodation.
**Prompt:** What do you do next?

**Option:** Estimate the number from memory, and write the sentence.
**Response:** That creates the record you promised not to keep. The number would be unverifiable, and "completed" suggests a requirement and a result that do not exist.

**Option:** Refuse to say anything about it.
**Response:** You can say something honest. Saying nothing may suggest that the session is secret, when it isn't. It's simply not tracked.

**Option (recommended):** Explain that it's a voluntary session with no attendance records, so there are no figures. Offer a true description: optional peer practice sessions on these topics are available. Point out that it shouldn't be listed as training completed, and that required training is tracked through the state's own process.
**Response:** It's accurate and it protects the promise. It also keeps the report from overstating what happened.

### Knowledge check

**Question:** What happens to the if-then plans people write at the end?
**Options:**
- The host collects them and follows up in a month.
- They go to each person's supervisor.
- The person keeps their own, and nobody collects them.
- They are typed into the app for tracking.

**Correct answer:** The person keeps their own, and nobody collects them.
**Feedback if correct:** Right. Private plans fit the ground rules. If someone wants to tell a peer, that's their choice.
**Feedback if incorrect:** Look for the option that keeps the plan with the person. This session keeps no records, and nothing goes to a supervisor.

### Transfer prompt

**Prompt:** Finish this plan: "If someone asks me who came or what was said, then I'll say ____. And if I'm asked to describe the session in a report, then I'll say ____."

## Job aid

**Title:** Hosting an optional practice session
**Subtitle:** One page for the day

**Purpose:** Help any host run a voluntary 90-minute practice session that respects people's privacy and gets them practicing out loud.

**Remember:**
- Voluntary. No attendance. No records. No identity-based breakouts. No required disclosure.
- Play job roles only. No disability simulations.
- Disagreement carries no consequence. Nobody speaks for a group.
- The reporting duty under Policies 1436 and 1329 still applies to supervisors and managers.

### Before

- Book CART and interpreters by default. Ask your ADA coordinator how.
- Send the invitation, specific about the content. No replies needed.
- Send materials ahead in accessible formats.
- Prepare your first sentences for a harmful comment and for a disclosure.
- Turn off recording, transcription and automated notetakers.

### During

- Opening script.
- A poor example, a good example, and then "What changed?"
- Rehearse in threes, with random pairings. Swap roles. Try it twice.
- Feedback: what you heard, and one thing to try.
- Keep the clock. Take a break.

### Hard moments

- Generalization: "Let's stay away from statements about what any group is like. What's the decision?"
- Asked to speak for a group: "Nobody here speaks for a group."
- Harassment described: "I have a duty to report. Let's step out."
- Disagreement: "Thank you. What have you seen? Whatever we think, what would you do?"

### After

- Private if-then plan. Nobody collects it.
- Clear walls, chat and shared documents.
- Delete access requests and any scheduling list.
- Don't report attendance or say "trained".

**Do next:** Choose one decision, book access, send the invitation.
