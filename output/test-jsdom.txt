What it is not   It is not the same as transformational leadership.  It is not treating a structural explanation as already proven.  It is not strong language without workable change.     The common scenario: “Who gets the visible work?”   Read the fictional scenario  A supervisor repeatedly gives high-visibility assignments to employees they describe as “polished,” “confident” and “ready to represent the team.”  A Black employee points out that they receive a lot of technical and behind-the-scenes work but fewer chances to present to leadership. These responses illustrate tendencies and choices, not scripts that predict how anyone would act.    A stronger version  “We need to examine how ‘polished’ became a selection criterion, who has benefited from that definition, and whether access to preparation and sponsorship has been equitable.”  The leader turns the conflict into an inquiry into structure. Possible actions include replacing vague judgments with job-related criteria, making opportunities visible, providing equitable preparation and support, clarifying who decides and how decisions are reviewed, and handling the reported conduct through the appropriate channels.    A harmful version  “This is obviously systemic racism, and anyone who questions that is part of the problem.”  It may name a real concern, but it treats a finding as proven before inquiry, shuts out people who could help, and offers no workable change. The manager mentions the opening in a hallway conversation and offers it to a staff member they describe as “a natural fit.”  Staff who work an evening schedule, a staff member who works fully remote, and a staff member returning from medical leave never hear about it. One of them, a Hmong American employee with eight years in the unit, asks the manager how the choice was made. The employee adds that acting roles have often led to permanent promotions in this unit.   Separate matters the leader must address   How the opportunity was announced, and who could not have known about it.  What criteria were used, and whether they were job-related.  The history of who has received acting roles and later promotions.  Whether the selection followed the applicable human resources and selection process.  Support for people who want to prepare for future acting roles.   What goes through an authorized process  Acting assignments and promotions may be covered by human resources rules, bargaining agreements and selection procedures. The manager should check with the person or area responsible for human resources before changing or defending the decision. A team conversation can improve future practice, but it cannot replace that process.  How different approaches might respond    A harmful response:  Autocratic or directive leadership   “I needed someone fast and I picked the person I knew could do it. That&#x27;s my call.”  The manager owns the decision but uses authority to close the question. “Someone I knew” is the very pattern being asked about.    A stronger response:  Bureaucratic or procedural leadership   “You&#x27;re right to ask. I&#x27;m going to check with human resources about what process applies to acting roles. Going forward, I&#x27;ll post every acting opportunity in one place, with the criteria and a deadline everyone can meet.”  The manager uses process to make the opportunity visible and reviewable. Checking the applicable process protects both the employee and the person selected.    A harmful response:  Servant leadership   “I&#x27;m sorry you missed this one. Let&#x27;s make a development plan so you&#x27;re ready next time.”  Support is offered before anyone checks whether the employee was ever given a fair chance. Readiness was not the problem; awareness and access were.    A stronger response:  Transformative or equity-centered leadership   “Let&#x27;s look at the last five years of acting roles: who got them, how they were offered, and who was later promoted. If informal offers have shaped who advances, we need to change that.”  The leader looks at the pattern behind one decision. The review uses records the unit already has and does not presume the answer.    A stronger response:  Transactional leadership   “Every acting role will be posted for at least five working days, with criteria. We will track who applies and who is selected, and review it every six months.”  This makes the commitment concrete and measurable. It works best together with the review of past patterns.    A combined response   Thank the employee and confirm there will be no adverse treatment for asking.  Check the applicable human resources process before deciding anything about the current assignment.  Review the history of acting roles and promotions with the authorized partners.  Post future opportunities in one place, with job-related criteria and a deadline that works for every schedule.  Offer preparation to anyone who wants it, without assuming who needs it. Accessible meetings and accommodation routing  The all-staff meeting   A division holds a hybrid all-staff meeting. A Deaf staff member requested American Sign Language interpreting two weeks earlier. The interpreters were not booked. Live captions are turned off because “they slow down the platform.” A manager suggests sending meeting notes afterward.  In the same week, a different staff member tells their supervisor they are having trouble with long video meetings because of a disability and asks whether they could join some meetings by phone or get materials in writing. The supervisor is unsure what to do and asks the team what they think.   Separate matters the leader must address   The missed interpreting request, which is an access failure for a request already made.  Captions, which are a basic access feature for many people.  The accommodation conversation, which belongs in the reasonable accommodation process, not a team discussion.  The privacy of the staff member who disclosed a disability.  Meeting practices that create the same barriers again and again.   What goes through an authorized process  Reasonable accommodation requests follow the agency&#x27;s established process. A supervisor should not put the request to coworkers or make assumptions about whether a disability is real. Connect the person promptly with the designated decision-maker, explain who needs the information to handle the request, and protect it from unnecessary disclosure. Supporting information belongs in the authorized process. Meanwhile, address access barriers within your authority; a team vote does not decide an individual request.  How different approaches might respond    A harmful response:  Democratic or participative leadership   “Let&#x27;s ask the team whether we should allow phone-in options for meetings.”  The supervisor turns one person&#x27;s accommodation request into a group decision and exposes a private disclosure. Participation is the wrong tool here.    A stronger response:  Bureaucratic or procedural leadership   “Thank you for telling me. I&#x27;ll connect you with the accommodation process today and keep what you&#x27;ve shared private. In the meantime, I&#x27;ll send written materials ahead of our meetings for everyone.”  The supervisor routes the request correctly, protects privacy and makes an immediate change that helps everyone without singling anyone out.    A stronger response:  Autocratic or directive leadership   “From now on, captions are on for every meeting we host, and interpreter requests are confirmed in writing within two working days. If they can&#x27;t be confirmed, the meeting moves.”  Directive authority is used well: it sets a clear access floor and makes the consequence of a missed request fall on the meeting, not on the Deaf staff member.    A harmful response:  Laissez-faire leadership   “The notes will go out after. Let me know if anything else comes up.”  The leader leaves the barrier in place and puts the work of following up on the person who was excluded.    A stronger response:  Inclusive leadership   “You should have had full access to this meeting, and you didn&#x27;t. I&#x27;m sorry. Before the next meeting, I&#x27;ll confirm interpreting with you, and we&#x27;ll make sure the recording is captioned and interpreted.”  The leader names the exclusion plainly, apologizes without excuses and commits to a specific fix with a date.    A combined response   Acknowledge the missed interpreting request directly to the Deaf staff member and fix it for the next meeting.  Turn on captions as the default for every meeting.  Route the second staff member&#x27;s request to the accommodation process the same day, and keep it private.  Share materials ahead of time and in accessible formats for everyone.  Assign one person or area responsible for confirming access requests, with a deadline.  Check back with both staff members on whether the changes worked.   To discuss   Why is a team vote the wrong way to handle an accommodation request?  Which access features could become the default so fewer people need to ask?  Who is responsible for confirming interpreting in your work, and by when?       Community input that conflicts with leadership preferences  The listening sessions   A program is redesigning how families learn about a new support option. Leadership prefers a short online survey and the original launch date. In listening sessions, Somali and Latino families, and several disability advocates, ask for in-person sessions in the evening, with interpreters, and for the notice to be rewritten in plain language before launch.  Meeting their requests would delay the launch by six weeks. A senior leader says, “We heard them, but we can&#x27;t let a few voices hold up the whole program.”   Separate matters the leader must address   Whether the community input can still change the decision, or whether the decision was already made. What goes through an authorized process  The Aging and Disability Services Administration (ADSA) Outreach and Engagement Guide helps staff plan engagement, access and reporting back. Any Tribal consultation or coordination process that applies must also be followed.  How different approaches might respond    A harmful response:  Charismatic leadership   “I know these families. I&#x27;ve worked with them for years. Trust me, they&#x27;ll be glad we launched on time.”  The leader&#x27;s personal relationship replaces what families actually said. Their input is overruled by the leader&#x27;s confidence.    A stronger response:  Democratic or participative leadership   “Here is what we heard, and here is what we can change. We can rewrite the notice in plain language and add two evening sessions with interpreters. The launch date can move three weeks, not six. We will come back to you in a month with what happened.”  The leader is honest about limits, shows how input changed the decision and commits to reporting back.    A harmful response:  Transactional leadership   “We held the required number of listening sessions. That box is checked.”  Completion is measured, not influence. The sessions happened, but nothing changed because of them.    A stronger response:  Transformative or equity-centered leadership   “If our process only works for families who can fill out an online survey in English, the process is the barrier. Let&#x27;s ask what it would take for these families to shape the next phase from the start.”  The leader turns one conflict into a change in how the program engages from now on.    A stronger response:  Shared or distributed leadership   “Let&#x27;s invite two of the community members to join the design group, with pay for their time, and give the group authority over the notice wording.”  Community members get real authority, not only a listening session. Paying for their expertise recognizes it as work.    A combined response   Tell participants plainly what can and cannot change before asking for input.  Use the Equity Analysis Toolkit to compare the effects of launching now with the effects of a short delay.  Make the changes that are possible, and explain the ones that are not.  Offer accessible, interpreted options and compensation for community expertise where appropriate.  Report back to participants on what their input changed, by an agreed date.   To discuss   When is a delay the more equitable choice?  How do you tell people honestly what their input can change?
