Compare options and tradeoffs

Once the decision is clear and you have begun gathering relevant perspectives, compare more than one practical option. Include the current approach when it is a meaningful alternative. A comparison helps you see what each choice makes easier, what it may make harder and what you still need to learn.

For the reminder example, consider three possibilities: keep mail as it is; replace mail with text only; or offer more than one method with a usable route for help. These are options for analysis, not a claim that every option is permitted, approved or ready to implement.

Before recommending any of them, confirm the requirements, decision authority and resources. Where additional information or review is needed, mark that need rather than presenting a preference as an approved plan.

A text message might be convenient for someone who can receive and use it. Mail might be more accessible or private for another person in a particular situation. Multiple methods may support choice but require staff time, reliable contact information and a plan to maintain each option.

These possibilities need inquiry. Do not assign a communication preference to someone based on identity, disability, language, income or age. Ask what the available evidence and appropriate engagement reveal, and identify whose experience is still missing.

Consider burdens as well as benefits. Does an option ask a person to obtain a device, navigate a difficult process, share information they would rather keep private or find help on their own? Does it increase effort for staff or partners in a way that needs planning?

Feasibility includes more than whether a method can be purchased or launched. Check whether the information can be delivered accessibly, whether language assistance and disability-related needs can be addressed, and whether the relevant offices can support the proposed process.

Also consider timing, resources, maintenance, privacy and what happens when a message is not received. A backup route matters. A choice can be technically possible and still leave a person without a usable next step.

Do not invent costs, response rates or access needs to complete the comparison. Mark them as unknown and identify how the responsible people can learn more. A sound recommendation can include an uncertainty and a next question.

The Equity Analysis Toolkit's decision workspace can help organize alternatives, benefits, burdens, risks and evidence. Use the official tool and any required process alongside it. Follow the workspace's own instructions when saving to My Work or downloading notes; do not assume another worksheet saves information the same way.

For a recommendation, explain why an option is being considered, what it would require and what could still go wrong. If people affected have not yet had a meaningful opportunity to inform an open choice, identify that gap and the next appropriate step.

The aim is not to find a perfect answer in isolation. It is to make the reasoning clear enough for the people who hold the decision to weigh evidence, responsibilities and alternatives.

In the activity below, compare proposed approaches to the reminder decision. Select the response that gives a reviewer the most useful basis for further work. Read the feedback after choosing, and compare the reasoning across the options.

If you are using your own example, name at least two possible approaches and one possible benefit, burden or question for each. Keep the note general and free of confidential details.

In the next lesson, we will bring the decision, information, alternatives and remaining questions together in a recommendation. The goal is a clear request for the appropriate review, not a statement that the change has already been approved.
