Learn from results and reconsider

The original goal was not simply to send a reminder. It was to help a person receive information they can understand and use to take the next step. That purpose should guide how the team learns from an authorized change.

A count of reminders sent tells you that an activity occurred. It does not by itself show that messages were received, understood, accessible or useful. Those are different questions, and each may need a different source of information.

For your own work, return to the purpose you named at the beginning. What experience or result did the change aim to improve? What would you need to learn before saying that it helped, and for whom?

Consider a process question: were reminders prepared and sent using the authorized method? Consider an experience question: could people understand the message and find help? Consider an outcome question: did people have a usable opportunity to take the intended next step?

You may not have answers to all three. Identify what is available, the period it covers, who is responsible for it and whether you are authorized to use it. Where a starting point or comparison has not been established, record that as a gap instead of creating a number.

The evaluation-plan worksheet can help connect a question, source, responsible role, review point and possible next decision. Its notes are not automatically saved; copy or download them if you want to keep a general working note. Follow the instructions shown in the worksheet.

Suppose a team later sees a higher response count. That could be worth exploring, but the count alone cannot establish that a particular communication change caused it or that everyone could participate. Timing, outreach, the number of people contacted and other conditions could also matter.

Look at both numbers and people's accounts where those sources are appropriate and authorized. Ask who may be missing from the information and what the evidence cannot show. Do not infer an individual's identity or treat a small group as anonymous without the responsible data owner's guidance.

Protect private information. Agree reporting, access and disclosure rules with the responsible office before gathering or sharing data. Do not paste names, appointment details, case information, health information or personnel records into learning notes.

Identify the role responsible for reviewing the information, the proposed time to return to it and the decision that the review could inform. These are plans to confirm, not commitments to invent on someone else's behalf.

For the reminder example, the team might agree to look at delivery and assistance questions after an authorized change, while also seeking appropriate feedback about whether the message was usable. If a help route remains unclear or an authorized method is not working, the team can bring that concern back to the responsible reviewers.

Share what can appropriately be shared with people who contributed: what was learned, what changed, what did not change and what remains unresolved. Report-back is part of respectful participation, not a promise to accept every suggestion.

In the activity below, consider what a change in the reminder count can and cannot establish. Choose a response, then read the feedback and compare the reasoning. The activity is a learning aid; it does not make a conclusion about an actual service.

For your own example, write one process question, one question about experience, a possible information source and the role that could review it. If any source, comparison or review date is not yet agreed, mark it as a question to resolve.

You began by defining a decision and its purpose. You considered the appropriate depth, requirements, affected people, evidence and alternatives. You prepared a recommendation for the responsible review, planned an authorized change and identified how to learn from the result.

Return to the existing toolkit, policy, engagement resources and evaluation plan whenever they are useful. The point is not to complete every form. It is to make a responsible decision more understandable, inclusive and open to improvement through the people and processes that actually govern the work.
