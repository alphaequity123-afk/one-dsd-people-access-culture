Identify requirements and decision roles

Before comparing options, make the decision and its boundaries clear. What is being considered? What needs to stay in place? Who can recommend a change, who reviews it, and who can approve it? Those questions protect good ideas from being mistaken for decisions already made.

In our continuing example, a team is considering how to send appointment reminders. The team has not decided to remove mail. Its task is to examine options for reminders, not to change an individual's eligibility or the appointment rules. The people responsible for those matters remain responsible for them.

Your own role may be to prepare a question, provide information, review a draft, coordinate input, or make a recommendation. A useful analysis makes those contributions visible without implying that one person holds every responsibility.

Look for the sources that actually govern the work. These might include applicable law, current agency policy, service requirements, delegated procedures or a contract. A learning example, a summary or a proposed improvement is not automatically a governing requirement.

The published DHS Equity Policy and the official Equity Analysis Toolkit are available from the existing toolkit and policy pages. Confirm the current policy, instructions and local review process for the decision you are working on. Where a required full analysis applies, a brief pause does not replace it.

Do not settle a question of legal or policy interpretation simply because a source seems relevant. Record what you found, where you found it, what remains uncertain and the office that can interpret or confirm it. Separate a requirement that is verified from one that still needs review.

Some parts of a proposal may be required; others may be choices. In the reminder example, the team should confirm what information must be communicated, whether a particular method is required, what consent or privacy rules apply and who is responsible for approving any change.

Until the responsible people confirm those points, they are questions, not facts. There may be more than one way to meet a requirement. Ask which options can still be considered rather than assuming that a constraint makes every other choice impossible.

The same distinction matters in a hiring process, a contract or a notice. Preserve binding requirements, and keep any proposed change clearly identified as a proposal until the appropriate decision is made.

Identify roles rather than putting one person in charge of everything. Who owns the work? Who understands the governing requirements? Who can arrange meaningful participation? Who will review accessibility, privacy or communications when those subjects arise? Who can make the final decision?

Formal processes, including those for civil rights, Tribal consultation, labor relations or other matters when applicable, remain in place. An equity analysis can inform them; it does not replace them. Ask the responsible office which processes apply rather than assuming they all apply to every example.

The requirements crosswalk can help you keep the source, the work it affects, the evidence and the reviewer together. Its notes support preparation; completing a worksheet is not itself an approval.

In the activity below, consider what to do when the reminder team is unsure whether a text-only approach meets the applicable requirements. Select a response, then read the feedback and compare the reasoning. You may revisit your choice.

For practice, use general information. Do not include a person's appointment details, health information or other confidential records.

For your own work example, identify one governing source, one part of the decision that remains open and one question that needs a responsible review. If you do not know a source or a role, it is useful to write that down as unknown.
