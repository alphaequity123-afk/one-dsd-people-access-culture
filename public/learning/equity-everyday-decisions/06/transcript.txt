Prepare a recommendation for review

A useful recommendation helps another person understand the decision, the people affected, the evidence and the options. It also makes open questions visible. Its purpose is to support the responsible review, not to make uncertainty disappear.

In the reminder example, a draft might explain why the team is considering a change, what is currently known, which communication methods are being compared and what the team still needs to learn. It should identify the relevant requirements and the people or offices responsible for reviewing the proposal.

If participation, privacy, accessibility or feasibility questions remain open, name them. Do not write that people prefer a method unless the information supports that statement. A recommendation can responsibly ask for further review or a limited, authorized test.

Begin with the purpose and the choice that is still open. Then describe who may benefit or experience a burden and where the information came from. Distinguish what people actually shared, what existing evidence shows and what the team is considering as a possibility.

Summarize the alternatives, including the current approach when relevant. Describe the tradeoffs and the practical support each option would need. Explain why the proposed direction deserves consideration, and describe any remaining access, privacy, resource or implementation questions.

If an option appears to reduce one barrier but might create another, say so. Reviewers need an honest account of the tradeoffs, including those that may be difficult to resolve.

Identify the decision owner and any required reviewers. Depending on the work, this may include program leadership, policy, communications, accessibility, privacy, civil rights, Tribal consultation, labor relations, contracting or other responsible offices. Confirm what applies instead of treating the list as an automatic requirement for every decision.

Use the current official Equity Analysis Toolkit and any required agency process. The One DSD resources can help you prepare and practice; they do not substitute for required approvals. A completed learning activity or worksheet does not authorize a change.

If you cannot identify the appropriate reviewer, record the question and seek help from a supervisor or the responsible office. You can make a meaningful contribution by preparing a clear question even when someone else owns the final decision.

Use words that match the actual status of the work. A proposed approach is proposed. An item waiting for review is not approved. A plan to ask people about an option is not evidence that people have already supported it.

For the reminder example, a suitable next step might be to ask the responsible program and communication reviewers to examine requirements, participation and available methods before a decision. The appropriate next step in an actual case depends on the current process and the people authorized to act.

Keep learning notes free of personal and confidential details. For real work, use the approved records and review channels. Follow each tool's instructions about whether a draft is saved, downloaded or retained only during the visit.

In the activity below, choose the recommendation that best communicates what is known, what is still open and what review is needed. Read the feedback after choosing. You can compare the alternatives and revise your answer.

For your own example, draft a short recommendation with a proposed action, the reason for considering it, one important uncertainty and the role that should review it.

The next lesson begins after the appropriate people have authorized a change. Until then, keep the recommendation and any required review separate from implementation. That distinction helps the work remain accountable and understandable.
