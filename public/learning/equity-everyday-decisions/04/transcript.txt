Learn from people and evidence

An equity analysis asks more than whether a change sounds reasonable to the people proposing it. It asks how the decision may be experienced by people who use, carry out or are otherwise affected by the work.

In the reminder example, the team can begin by asking which methods people can receive and use, whether the information is understandable and whether there is a usable way to ask for help. These are questions to explore. They are not conclusions about a person because of their age, disability, language, location or community.

If you are using your own example, identify the people affected by the decision and the people who help carry it out. Their perspectives may be different, and no single person represents every experience.

The team may know that it currently sends reminders by mail and is considering another method. It may know what information appears on a public reminder. It does not automatically know whether a particular person has a phone, reads texts, shares a device, understands a message or prefers mail.

A response count can be useful, but it does not explain a person's reason for responding or not responding. Keep observations, interpretations and questions separate. For example: the team recorded that a reminder was sent; it still needs to learn whether the person received and could use it.

Look for existing information that you are authorized to use. Describe its source, period and limits. If information is missing, say so. Do not fill a gap with a confident-sounding guess.

Ask which choices can still be influenced before seeking input. Explain the purpose of the question, the options under consideration and any known limits. Use the established engagement process and involve the people or offices responsible for arranging participation.

Consider what makes participation usable: clear language, accessible materials, appropriate language assistance, timing, a choice of ways to respond and support where needed. The right arrangements depend on the people and the work. Ask rather than assume.

Be clear about what happens next. Who will review the input? How will people hear what was considered, what changed or did not change, and why? Meaningful participation includes a way to close that loop, even when every suggestion cannot be adopted.

An individual learner does not need to collect sensitive information to practice. Keep learning notes general and leave out names, appointment details, case information, health information and personnel records.

For actual work, use approved sources, permissions and privacy practices. Ask the responsible data owner about access, reporting and small groups before using or sharing information. The goal is to learn enough to make a better decision, not to gather more detail than the work calls for.

When evidence is incomplete, say what it can and cannot support. A few experiences can reveal important questions without proving how every person experiences a service. Missing perspectives are a reason to plan further inquiry, not a reason to pretend the analysis is complete.

In the activity below, consider what the reminder team can reasonably conclude from its current information. Choose a response and then read the feedback. Compare the options and notice the difference between an observation and an assumption.

You can pause here and make a brief note about your own example. What do you know, where did it come from, whose perspective is missing and who can help you seek it appropriately?

The next step is to compare options, not to treat the first attractive idea as the only choice. Bring the information you have, identify what remains uncertain, and keep the people affected in view as you consider the tradeoffs.
