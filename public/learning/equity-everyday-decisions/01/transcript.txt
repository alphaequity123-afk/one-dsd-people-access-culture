Start with a decision and the people it affects

Welcome. Equity becomes practical when we look closely at a decision: what it is meant to accomplish, who may experience it, and what could make that experience more or less fair. You do not have to solve every problem at once. You can begin with one question in your own work.

This learning is self-directed. You can pause, revisit a section and use the activities at your own pace. You may work with the continuing example or choose a non-sensitive piece of your own work. Participation in this learning path is voluntary. Follow the policies, required analyses and training that apply to your work separately.

The lessons are a companion to the existing Equity Analysis Toolkit and other One DSD resources. They help you prepare and practice. They do not replace the official tool, required review, or the judgment of the people and offices responsible for a decision.

Consider an appointment reminder. A team is thinking about replacing mailed reminders with text messages only. The team hopes a text might be a useful way to reach people. No change has been approved, and the team has not yet established which options people can use or prefer.

At first, this can look like a question about how to send a message. For the person receiving it, the question is larger: Will I receive the reminder in a way I can use? Will I understand it? Can I act on it or ask for help? Those questions bring the intended experience into view.

There may be benefits to texting. There may also be concerns about access, language, shared phones, privacy, disability-related needs, or a change in contact information. Treat these as questions to investigate, not assumptions about any person or community.

A clear decision statement names the choice under consideration and the purpose it is supposed to serve. For this example, the question is whether and how to offer appointment reminders, including whether a text-only approach should replace mailed reminders.

Notice what that statement does not decide. It does not change eligibility, appointment requirements or an individual's services. It also does not assume that a new communication method has already been authorized. Those boundaries help the right people review the right question.

If your work involves a form, a meeting process, a contract, a hiring practice or a service handoff, the same habit applies. State what may change, what is staying outside the proposal, and who has authority to decide. If a boundary is uncertain, write the question down rather than guessing.

The people affected may include those receiving reminders, staff who prepare them, people who provide communication support, and partners responsible for the service. Their roles and perspectives differ. A convenient process for one person may require more effort from another.

Equity does not mean assuming that a particular group will respond in a particular way. Ask what could affect access and whose experience would help you understand it. Be attentive to differences without turning a person's identity into a prediction about their needs.

You may not have direct contact with the people affected. Your contribution might be to examine the proposal, notice a missing question, consult the appropriate colleagues, or help bring existing information into the review. Good work begins within your role and makes room for the knowledge of others.

In the activity below, consider how to frame the proposed reminder change before anyone settles on a method. Choose the response you think creates the best starting point. After you choose, read the feedback and compare the reasoning. You can change your answer and try again.

There is no need to enter personal information. The activity is for practice; it does not make a decision for an actual person or approve a change.

If you would like, write a brief note about a decision you can examine safely. Name its purpose, the choice that is still open, the people who may be affected and a question about your authority or next review. You can also stay with the reminder example throughout the series.

Keep names, case details, health information, personnel records and other confidential information out of learning notes. Follow the instructions beside any tool you use to understand whether its notes are saved, downloaded or kept only for the current visit.

Next, we will consider how much review the decision needs. A useful first question is not simply how quickly a decision can be made. It is what the work requires and what could happen if an important perspective is missed.
