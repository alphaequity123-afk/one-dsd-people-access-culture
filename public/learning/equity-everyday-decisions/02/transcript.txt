Choose the right depth of review

Some decisions are routine and contained. Others can change a policy, service, budget, opportunity or process for many people. Equity analysis should be useful at either scale, but the amount of review and the people involved can differ.

Begin by checking the current DHS equity policy, the official Equity Analysis Toolkit and the procedures that apply to your work. The published DHS policy requires a formal equity analysis for actions with prolonged or long-term impact that are scheduled for approval by a supervisor, manager, director, assistant or deputy commissioner, or commissioner.

The policy says those actions must be accompanied by the DHS Equity Analysis or DHS Equity Scan in the toolkit, or a customized tool developed in consultation with an equity director. Confirm the current policy and user guide with the responsible office.

The brief Equity Pause in this application is an additional learning aid; do not assume it is the official DHS Equity Scan or that it satisfies a required process.

If a full analysis or another review is required, follow that requirement. A quick check does not replace a required full analysis, and a short deadline does not by itself decide what is sufficient.

The choices in this lesson help you think about a starting point. They are not a separate approval rule. When the right path is unclear, identify the question and ask the responsible person or office.

An Equity Pause is a brief way to examine a routine decision before it is settled. You might ask who could benefit, who could face an obstacle, what you know, what you do not know and whether someone else should be involved.

For example, a team changing the time of an internal meeting might use a short check to consider access, schedules and participation. If a barrier appears, the response may require more inquiry or another person's review. The value of the pause is the question it opens, not the speed with which a form is finished.

A brief check does not establish that every person has been reached or that a change is equitable. It also does not authorize someone to make a decision outside their responsibilities. Use the appropriate process when a requirement or an emerging concern calls for more.

An analysis gives you room to examine the decision, the people affected, evidence, alternatives, participation, implementation and follow-up. It is especially useful when the proposed change could alter how people reach information, services or opportunities.

Return to the reminder example. Replacing mailed reminders with text-only messages could change the way people receive information and act on an appointment. Before choosing a direction, the team needs to understand the applicable requirements, seek relevant experience and compare options. The official tool and responsible reviewers guide what the work requires.

An analysis does not have to begin with a perfect data set. It can identify what is known, what is uncertain and how to learn more. Be honest about those limits, particularly when an alternative could create a new burden while removing another.

Sometimes a concern points beyond the immediate decision. A reminder may be only one part of an appointment process that also includes scheduling, language support, transportation information, rescheduling and follow-up. If an obstacle appears across several steps, a more sustained review may be needed.

That review can involve the people affected and the offices responsible for the process while meaningful choices remain open. A staff member can help by describing what they observed, bringing forward evidence and identifying where the question needs to go. They need not claim authority over the entire process.

More review is not automatically better if it delays an urgent response without purpose. Follow any applicable immediate procedure, address what you are authorized to address, and identify the appropriate review for the broader question.

In the activity below, consider the proposed text-only reminder change. Choose the most responsible next step when you do not yet know the applicable review requirement or how people would experience the change. After choosing, read the feedback. You can compare all three responses.

The practice is about preparing for a decision, not declaring a final result. Do not enter information about an actual person's appointment or services.

For your own example, note the proposed change, who may be affected, the likely consequences if access differs, and the policy or procedure you need to check. Identify what you know, what you need to confirm and who can help determine the required review.

The existing analysis tools offer a quick check, a standard analysis and an in-depth review. Their labels help you find support; current requirements and responsible review determine what your work must include.
