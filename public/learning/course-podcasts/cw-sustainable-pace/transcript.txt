Running on Empty, Quietly

Kelly: Here's someone a lot of us know. A case manager, two years with a full caseload, and really good at it. Lately they answer email at ten at night, skip lunch and feel flat even after a good outcome for a family.

John: Nothing's on fire. The work is getting done. So is there actually a problem?

Kelly: That's the trap. The early signs are quiet. Feeling flat after a win is one of them. The World Health Organization describes burnout as a result of workplace stress that hasn't been managed: exhaustion, growing distance from the work and feeling less effective.

John: So it's about the conditions at work, not a personal failing.

Kelly: Right. And that matters, because it means some of the fixes are things a team can change together.

John: Okay, practical moment. It's 9:45 at night. A colleague emails a non-urgent question about next week's schedule. I'm going to answer it. It takes thirty seconds.

Kelly: It does. And it also tells your colleague that late-night answers are normal here. Next week, they'll feel they should answer at ten, too.

John: Huh. So the thirty seconds isn't really the cost.

Kelly: No. One small protection is to schedule the reply for the morning, or simply answer it at nine. The question still gets answered. The norm doesn't spread.

John: What about when it's more than email? Caseload up by a third since spring, two reports late, and I haven't said anything to my supervisor.

Kelly: That's the moment to raise it, while it's two reports and not ten. Bring facts and a question: here's what's changed, here's what's slipping, which of these should I put first?

John: That's much easier to say than, I'm not coping.

Kelly: And it's more useful to a supervisor. They can actually decide something.

John: Last one. A colleague has seemed withdrawn for a few weeks and left early twice without saying goodbye. I don't want to pry.

Kelly: You don't have to diagnose anything. A private, simple check-in works: I've noticed you seem a bit quieter lately. How are you doing? Then listen, and let them decide how much to share.

John: And if they're really struggling?

Kelly: Know where support lives. Minnesota's state Employee Assistance Program is free, voluntary and confidential, for state employees and the people they live with. And if someone may be in crisis, the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline answers by call, text or chat, any time.

John: So the course isn't about fixing anyone. It's about noticing early, and knowing the next step.
