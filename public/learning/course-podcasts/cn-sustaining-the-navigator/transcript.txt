Carrying It

Kelly: Navigators hear hard stories all day, often from their own community. That has a name: secondary traumatic stress. The National Child Traumatic Stress Network describes it as distress that comes from hearing about someone else's trauma.

John: What does it look like?

Kelly: Exhaustion, trouble sleeping, hopelessness, getting cynical, avoiding the people you serve. And the risk goes up with things that fit this work: heavy caseloads, isolation, a lot of empathy and feeling unprepared.

John: And then there's the phone. Community members calling at night, on weekends, at the grocery store.

Kelly: Boundary strain. Saying no can feel like letting your own people down. But a kind boundary protects the relationship. Something like, I want to help with this. I'm off now, so let's talk tomorrow at nine. If it's an emergency, call this number.

John: Is this mostly on the navigator, then? Self-care?

Kelly: Most of it is on the organization. The fact sheet names balanced caseloads, flexible scheduling, ongoing training and supervision. Nobody can self-care their way out of a caseload that doubled.

John: So if I supervise a navigator who's gone quiet and is missing follow-ups?

Kelly: Meet privately, say what you've noticed without labeling, ask how they're carrying it and look at the caseload together. And make sure they know about confidential help, like the Employee Assistance Program.

John: Notice early, share the load and set boundaries kindly. That's how the work lasts.
