Who Helps at Home?

Kelly: Picture an assessment. The assessor asks who helps the person at home, and the person's mother says, family takes care of family. What gets written down?

John: Probably something like, daily help covered by family. It sounds clear. It sounds like a strong family.

Kelly: And it might be. But in this case the mother works nights. The family didn't want to look like they couldn't cope in front of someone from the county. So the clear answer was true and incomplete at the same time.

John: And if nobody catches that, the plan has a hole in it on exactly the nights that matter.

Kelly: That's where a cultural navigator can help. A navigator is a trusted go-between, often someone from the person's community, who helps a family and a system understand each other. In this case the navigator simply asked the mother whether she wanted to mention her schedule.

John: So the navigator didn't answer for the family.

Kelly: No, and that matters. The family still speaks for itself. The navigator explains context, goes along to meetings, connects people to resources, follows up, and tells the agency where a process keeps tripping people up.

John: Is there evidence this works, or is it just a nice idea?

Kelly: There's some good evidence when navigation is tied to a specific step. A 2021 randomized trial of family navigation for young children who screened positive for autism found that more families got a diagnostic answer within a year. The difference was largest for Hispanic families. The authors also found results varied by clinic, so it's promising, not magic.

John: Here's a question I hear a lot. If the navigator speaks the family's language, can we skip the interpreter?

Kelly: Book both. The interpreter carries the words accurately in both directions. The navigator helps the family make sense of the process. If the navigator is interpreting, nobody is doing the navigating.

John: And the version where we ask the bilingual coworker to just come along on a few visits?

Kelly: That's navigation work without the job. It adds work nobody counts, and it can leave that coworker caught between their community and their role. If the need is real, treat it as real work, and tell someone who can fix the pattern.

John: So for someone listening who isn't a navigator, what's the one thing to do?

Kelly: When an answer could mean more than one thing, check before you write it down. Ask about specific hours and tasks. And if a navigator might help, ask the person whether they'd like one. It's their choice, and what they share is their choice too.

John: Check the answer, offer the choice, and give everyone a clear role. I can do that on Monday.
