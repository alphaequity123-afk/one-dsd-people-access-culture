Learn with people affected by the work

Imagine a team revises the service-planning notice and asks for feedback only after the new version is approved and distributed. People may offer useful observations, but they have little opportunity to influence the original decision. Participation has arrived too late.

Standards twelve and thirteen address understanding community assets and needs and partnering with communities in the design, implementation and evaluation of practice. For your individual work, the question is how you can support an appropriate process for learning with people affected, within your responsibilities.

This lesson does not require you to organize a group or personally contact service recipients. You can complete every activity independently. Where engagement is needed, identify the established route and the people responsible for it.

Before asking for input, describe the decision. What is open to change? What requirements or constraints must remain? How will input be considered, who will decide, and when will people hear what happened?

For the notice, the team may be able to change the wording, layout and explanation of assistance, while a required time limit remains fixed. Say that clearly. Asking people to choose something that is not actually open can damage trust. A constrained decision can still benefit from meaningful participation when the boundaries are honest.

Also ask whose experience is missing. People who readily respond to an online survey may not represent those who had difficulty accessing the original information. A single advisor cannot be assumed to speak for every person with a disability, every language community or every family.

Consider the format, language, communication support, timing, location or technology, and the effort required. An invitation that is inaccessible can reproduce the very problem the project hopes to address. Offer appropriate ways to participate through the approved engagement process.

Respect a person's choice not to participate. Avoid making access to services appear dependent on providing feedback. Explain what information is requested, how it will be used and any limits on confidentiality. Follow applicable privacy and records requirements. If compensation or reimbursement is available, use the established rules; do not promise a payment you cannot authorize.

Participation should make room for the person themselves, including self-advocates and their chosen supports. Do not automatically substitute the views of professionals or family members for the person's perspective. At the same time, different perspectives can reveal different parts of an experience when their roles are clear.

Community assessment includes strengths. Ask what already works, which communication approaches are trusted, and what people would want preserved. A redesign can unintentionally remove a feature that some people depend on.

In our example, a clear telephone option may be valuable even if a new online response form is introduced. Improving one route does not necessarily justify removing another. Look for choices that support participation rather than assuming one channel will work for everyone.

When feedback differs, describe the difference rather than forcing agreement. One person may prefer a brief notice, while another needs more explanation. A concise main message with accessible supporting detail might address both needs, but the proposed solution still needs checking.

After input is considered, explain what changed, what did not change and why. Be specific without exposing personal information. If a suggestion cannot be adopted, acknowledge it and explain the relevant constraint or next step. Silence can leave people unsure whether their contribution mattered.

Partnership continues into implementation and evaluation. People can help identify whether the revised notice actually improves understanding or whether a new barrier has appeared. That is different from using a consultation only to endorse a finished product.

For your work example, write the decision that needs input, the perspectives that may be missing, an accessible way to obtain that input, and the role responsible for arranging it. Then describe how people would hear back.

If you lack authority to initiate engagement, your action might be a recommendation to the responsible person. It can still be precise: ask for input before approval, identify the access considerations, and explain how the input could improve the decision.

In the practice activity, choose an approach that gives people a real opportunity to influence the work while being honest about constraints. The aim is not simply to count participants. It is to improve the decision and the experience through meaningful involvement.

Next, we will consider how to tell whether the change is helping. Your plan for participation will be part of that evidence.
