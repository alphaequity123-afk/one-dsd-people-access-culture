Host 1: Here is the work this part helps you prepare: Separate an observation from an assumption; write a respectful question

Host 1: Begin with what you know.

Host 2: I have an example. How do I describe a problem without quietly turning my first guess into a fact?

Host 1: In the first lesson, you chose a work example and described what someone needs to do with it. Now we will examine what might get in the way. This lesson connects the principal standard with ongoing workforce learning and understanding community needs.

Host 2: Start with an observation you could describe without assigning a motive. For our sample notice, the observation is that some people have not responded. Saying that they do not care is an interpretation. Saying that they cannot understand the notice is also an interpretation until we have more information.

Host 1: A respectful inquiry begins by acknowledging what we do not know. We can say, where our role permits contact, that we want to check whether the person received the information and whether another way of explaining or responding would help. We do not need to begin by asking the person to defend their behavior.

Host 1: Look at the whole encounter.

Host 1: Could we follow the steps from receiving the notice to responding, instead of looking only at the final response count?

Host 2: Imagine the steps involved in responding. The person must receive the notice, recognize its purpose, understand the options, decide what they want to do, and use an available response method. A difficulty at any one step can look like a lack of interest from the other end.

Host 1: Possible barriers include unfamiliar terminology, information in a format someone cannot access, no interpreter when needed, an inconvenient appointment, transportation, unreliable technology, or previous experiences that make the process difficult to trust. These are possibilities to investigate, not explanations to assign automatically.

Host 2: Also look for strengths and supports. A person may already have an effective communication method, a trusted support person they choose, or a clear preference about how to receive information. A community may have organizations with relevant knowledge and established relationships. Understanding assets as well as barriers is part of the community assessment described in standard twelve.

Host 1: Culture is a starting point for curiosity.

Host 2: Where does cultural context help us, and where could a generalization get in the way?

Host 1: Culture can shape communication, expectations and experiences. It does not determine every person's preferences. People within the same community differ, and a person's preferences can change with the situation.

Host 2: Avoid turning general information about a community into a rule about an individual. Instead of deciding that a family will want a particular person to speak, ask whom the person would like involved and how they want to participate. Instead of interpreting silence as agreement, offer time and a way to ask questions. Instead of treating an accent as evidence of understanding or misunderstanding, check the clarity of the information itself.

Host 1: Cultural humility involves examining your own assumptions as well. Familiar procedures can feel straightforward to the people who use them every day. They may be entirely unfamiliar to someone encountering them for the first time. Recognizing that difference is an opportunity to improve the explanation.

Host 1: Practice a useful question.

Host 1: What would a more useful opening question sound like?

Host 2: Consider these two openings. The first asks, why have you failed to return the form? The second explains the purpose of the form, asks whether the person received it, and offers help identifying a usable way to respond. The second opening provides information and creates room for the person's explanation.

Host 1: That does not mean ignoring a deadline or a required step. It means explaining the requirement clearly while asking what support is needed. Do not promise an extension or exception that you cannot authorize. If an adjustment needs approval, explain what you can do next and who will review it.

Host 2: In the practice activity, separate what was observed from what was assumed. Then choose a question that invites the person's perspective without requiring private information that is not needed for the task.

Host 1: Apply inquiry to your own role.

Host 2: What if my role is behind the scenes, or I supervise the people doing this work?

Host 1: You may not have direct contact with people receiving services. If you work on policy, data, contracts or communications, inquiry still matters. Ask how the process was tested, whose experience informed it, which access needs were considered, and where feedback is recorded. Consult the appropriate people rather than inventing an answer or contacting someone outside your responsibilities.

Host 2: A supervisor can help staff discuss uncertainty without shaming them for asking questions. A manager can look for repeated barriers across encounters. A director can ask whether resources and accountability support the response. Regular learning, addressed in standard four, should help people practice these decisions rather than merely acknowledge that they read a policy.

Host 1: If there is no direct access to the people affected, do not fill the gap by guessing. Name the gap and identify who can help. Existing feedback, approved engagement processes, accessibility reviews or a discussion with the responsible program area may provide a starting point.
