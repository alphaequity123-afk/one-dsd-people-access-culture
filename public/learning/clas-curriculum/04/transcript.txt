Make information accessible and actionable

You have considered the person's experience and the path for communication assistance. Now take a closer look at the information. Standard eight addresses understandable digital and print materials and signage in languages used by people served. The principal standard keeps the person's communication needs at the center.

We will use five questions: can the person find it, access it, understand it, use it and act on it? These questions are a practical review aid. They do not replace technical accessibility testing, translation review or applicable agency requirements. They help you recognize where further work is needed.

Choose a public-facing or non-sensitive example for this activity. If you are reviewing a real work document, keep it in the appropriate work location rather than placing personal information in your learning notes.

First, can the person find the information? A statement about assistance may exist somewhere on a website but be absent from the notice where the person needs it. A link may use an internal document name that gives no clue about its purpose. A telephone option may be difficult to locate among several unrelated numbers.

Next, can the person access it? Consider format and method, not only whether the file opens on your computer. An image of text may be unreadable to assistive technology. A video without accurate captions or a useful transcript may exclude people. A form that requires a mouse can block keyboard users. A digital-only instruction may be unusable without reliable access to a suitable device or connection.

Do not assume you can determine every accessibility issue by looking at the screen. Use the established review process and involve the appropriate accessibility support. If you create documents, use meaningful headings, descriptive links, readable structure and the approved tools for checking the result. Check the document that people will actually receive, including any exported version.

Look at our sample notice. It asks the person to attend a service-planning meeting, but the opening sentence is filled with acronyms and internal references. The reader may not know what the meeting is for, whether they have a choice, or what they need to do next.

A clearer structure begins with purpose and action. Explain why the person is receiving the notice. Identify the next step, the relevant date and a way to ask questions or request assistance. Explain necessary terms when they first appear. Keep essential rights, choices and requirements intact; plain language is not permission to remove important meaning.

Translation and plain language solve related but different problems. A translated document can still be difficult to understand if the source is unnecessarily complicated. An easy-to-read English version does not meet every language need. Use the appropriate qualified review and approved process for the actual audience and purpose. Do not send confidential information into an unapproved translation service.

Now ask whether the person can use the information to make a decision or complete a step. A notice might say to contact the department without naming a usable route. A form might explain what is needed but provide no way to ask for help. A deadline might be stated without explaining what action must be completed by that date.

Make the next step concrete. In our learning example, a useful notice would explain the meeting's purpose, how to confirm or discuss the appointment, how to request communication assistance, and where to ask questions. Use actual approved contact details in real work. We are not inventing a new contact or process here.

Check that the proposed change is within your authority. You may be able to improve a draft, but a regulated notice or official form may require review before use. Preserve the required content and route the suggested revision to the person responsible.

In the activity, compare three proposed revisions to the notice. Think about what each option would mean for someone who needs to understand the information and act on it. Consider clarity, accessibility, required meaning and a way to request assistance. Choose the strongest revision, then read the feedback.

Then apply the five questions to your example. You do not have to solve every issue today. Identify one change you can propose or make, one item that needs specialist review, and one way to check whether the revised information is more usable.

Where appropriate, feedback from people who use the information can reveal issues that an internal review misses. That feedback should be accessible and voluntary, with a clear explanation of how it will be used. We will develop that approach in the community-partnership lesson.

Save a short description of the improvement in your own notes: the barrier, the proposed change, the necessary review and the expected benefit. Avoid claiming that a document is fully accessible simply because it passed one automated check or received a positive comment.

The goal is a more dependable experience. In the next lesson, we will connect your proposed improvement to the roles, resources and decisions that make it possible to sustain.
