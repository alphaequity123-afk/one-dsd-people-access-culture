Notice the lens I bring

Welcome. In this first part, we will look at something you already know: how you usually approach a conversation, a decision, or a piece of work. The habits you rely on have helped you get things done. They can also shape what you notice and what you expect from other people.

This learning is voluntary. You can pause, return to a section, follow the example with us, or think about a process you know. If you choose to make notes, keep names, case details, and other private information out of them. You do not have to share a personal story to take part.

Across these lessons we will follow one work example. A division team is revising how people hear about and respond to a planning opportunity. There is a purpose and a decision deadline. The team has not yet settled on the best way to communicate, invite input, or explain what happened afterward.

You can imagine yourself helping with a notice, a meeting, a recommendation, or another part of that work.

Imagine that the team usually shares a long draft the morning of a meeting and asks for reactions before the meeting ends. Some people find the discussion useful. Others may need a different way to review or respond. At this point, we do not know who prefers what or why.

You might be comfortable thinking out loud. You might prefer to read first, ask questions afterward, or have time to consult someone. A preference can change with the task, the amount of information, the people involved, and the time available. It is not a permanent label for you or anyone else.

The useful question is not whether one style is right. Ask what the work needs, what the current routine assumes, and which arrangements would give people a usable opportunity to contribute. A format can be familiar to the team without being required for the decision.

Suppose a participant says they would like more time before commenting. What do you know? You know they asked for more time. You may also know the decision date. You do not yet know why the person asked, what they might contribute, or whether an adjustment is possible.

An assumption might sound like, “They are not interested,” or “They are not prepared.” Another assumption could be, “Everyone who prefers written comments thinks the same way.” Each moves beyond the information you have. A more useful next step is to ask what would help and check what the team can offer.

This is also a chance to notice your own reaction. Perhaps you feel pressure because the deadline is close. That feeling is worth noticing, and it does not have to settle the question. You can acknowledge the constraint and still look for an option that respects the person's participation.

The team may have some choices and not others. A decision date, a review step, an accessibility requirement, or a policy may need confirmation. Within those limits, staff may have discretion over how clearly they invite comments or how people can ask for support. Check rather than assume which is which.

Roles matter too. A person who drafts an invitation may be able to suggest a change but not authorize a new deadline. A supervisor or another responsible office may need to decide. The person affected is the best source for what would make their participation workable; their preference still needs an honest response about what is possible and what must be checked.

The goal is not to judge a colleague's intent. A team can mean to welcome everyone and still have a routine that is easier for some people to use than others. Looking at the effect gives you a practical place to begin.

Return to the planning invitation. A participant asks for more time to review the draft. The decision date is approaching, and you have not yet confirmed whether the comment period can change. What would you do first?

Pause here if you would like to consider your response. Choose an answer in the activity, then compare the reasoning. You can revisit the choices and change your answer. The activity is practice; the actual requirements and decision authority belong to the responsible people and offices.

Choose one small routine in your own work, or stay with the planning example. Name the step, what it is meant to accomplish, and one expectation it makes about the person using it. Then write a question that could help you check that expectation.

For example: “We usually ask for comments in a live meeting. What other response routes could fit the decision schedule, and whom should we ask before choosing?” You do not need to name a person or describe a private situation. The point is to make the next conversation more useful.
