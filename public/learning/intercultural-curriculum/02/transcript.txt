Use context without making a profile

In the last part, we noticed how a familiar routine can carry an assumption. Now we will add context without turning that context into a prediction about a person.

The Minnesota Communities collection brings together history, place, contributions, experiences, and questions for work. The material can help you prepare, notice a possible access barrier, and find ideas for respectful questions. It does not tell you what an individual believes, what language they use, whom they trust, or what they want you to do.

You may explore a brief that is relevant to your work or simply follow the example. You are not expected to read the entire collection. Each person and each situation deserves attention in its own right.

Imagine that you are helping prepare the planning invitation. You open a community brief before discussing the invitation with people affected by it. A passage describes a historical experience or a possible obstacle to using a public service. That gives you a reason to ask whether the current process creates a similar barrier. It does not establish that the person you will meet has encountered it.

Look at where a statement comes from and when it was written. A statistic may describe a particular place, period, definition, or group of respondents. A book offers an author's perspective. A community account may describe a real experience without representing every person in that community. If the source or its date is unclear, record the uncertainty and seek a current, appropriate source before repeating the claim.

You do not have to turn every unknown into a question for the person receiving a service. Ask only what helps with the work. Do not ask someone to explain their community, faith, immigration history, or a painful experience simply to satisfy your curiosity.

Suppose a brief discusses relationships and support. A rushed conclusion would be, “This person will want their family to decide.” A better question is, “Is there anyone you would like involved, and how would you like them to participate?” The question leaves room for a relative, a friend, a professional supporter, or no one else.

The same habit applies to language. A person's name, appearance, community, or country of origin does not tell you which language or communication method they prefer. Make it easy for everyone to request support, and ask what works for the individual when that information is needed.

There is variety within every community, and people may identify with more than one community or with none of the labels in a directory. Let the person describe what matters to them. When the brief raises a question about the way your team works, examine the process as well as the conversation.

Culture is not the only possible explanation for a barrier. A short response period, inaccessible document, missing interpretation, unreliable technology, transportation, workload, or unclear decision process may also affect participation. Start with what you can observe and the information people are willing to share; do not use a cultural label as a shortcut.

In the planning example, the team might learn that a notice is difficult to use. The useful follow-up is to ask what made it difficult, what would help, and who can review a change. Explain what is open to change and how feedback will be considered. If an answer is not yet known, say so and identify a next step.

For substantive questions concerning Native American individuals, communities, or Tribal Nations, follow this program's standing deference to the Office of Indian Affairs and the DHS offices responsible for Tribal relations and consultation. A general reading or listening activity does not replace a Nation's authority or government-to-government consultation. Seek the appropriate guidance rather than inventing an answer.

A colleague reads that some people may value involving trusted supporters. The colleague proposes inviting a participant's relatives to the planning discussion before asking the participant. What would you do first?

Pause and choose an answer in the activity. Then read the explanation. Notice the difference between preparing an inclusive option and deciding for someone who should be involved.

Choose one sentence or idea from an available brief, or use the example you just heard. Write down what it helps you notice, one question you could ask about the actual work, and one thing you do not yet know. Keep any note general and free of identifying or confidential information.

You can follow the source links or an existing reading for deeper context. Treat the source as a starting point, not a substitute for listening. Next we will use the same approach to make communication and participation more accessible.
