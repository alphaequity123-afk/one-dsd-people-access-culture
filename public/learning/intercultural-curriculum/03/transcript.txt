Make communication and access usable

Our planning team is ready to improve the invitation. The goal is not just to send a message. It is for people to receive information they can use, understand what they are being asked, and have a workable way to respond.

Think about the whole exchange: the invitation, the material someone needs to review, the conversation, the way they can ask for help, and the follow-up. A change to one step may not solve a barrier in another. The person using the process can tell you what is and is not working for them.

This lesson is voluntary and individual. You may use the shared example or a general process from your work. Keep any notes free of names, case details, health information, and other confidential information.

The team has prepared a written notice in English and is considering what other support to offer. Rather than assigning a need from someone's name, background, age, disability, or the community brief you read, make the request process easy to find and ask the person what works for this communication.

An open question could be, “What language or communication format would help you take part?” Depending on the situation, another question might be, “Would you like an interpreter or another communication support, and how should we arrange it?” Ask about a practical need; do not demand a personal history as the price of access.

Listen to the answer and check that you understood it. If an arrangement is already documented in an approved process, use it appropriately so the person does not have to repeat the same request unnecessarily. If you cannot arrange a requested support yourself, explain whom you will contact and when the person can expect an update.

Language assistance and disability access can overlap, but they are not identical. Someone may ask for an interpreter, a translated document, an accessible electronic file, a different way to communicate in a meeting, or more time with a communication device. The right arrangement depends on the person, the information, and the situation.

The National CLAS Standards call for language assistance at no cost to individuals in health and health care, notice of available assistance, and competent people providing it. The Department of Justice's effective-communication guidance describes aids and services for people with communication disabilities and the importance of the person's usual method and the context.

Apply the relevant agency procedures and current requirements to your work; ask the responsible office when you are unsure. A short learning activity does not decide a legal obligation for every situation.

Do not assume that a relative, a child, or a bilingual colleague should interpret. Follow the applicable process for qualified assistance and privacy. If a person invites a supporter, ask what role they want that person to have; a supporter's presence does not automatically replace the person's own participation.

Return to the planning opportunity. A person tells the team that the current materials do not work with their communication method. Start by asking what would make the materials and conversation usable. Confirm what support is needed, who can arrange it, and when it will be ready. Check both the document and the meeting, not just one of them.

Make the next step clear. The team might provide an accessible version, arrange a qualified service, offer a usable way to send a response, or consult the appropriate accessibility or language-access contact. Which steps fit depends on the actual request and applicable procedures. Do not promise an arrangement you have not confirmed; do not leave the person to solve the problem alone.

Afterward, ask whether the arrangement worked and what remains difficult. Record only what is needed and allowed by the applicable process. A useful record helps the next step happen; it is not a collection of personal cultural stories.

A participant says the projected captions at a planning meeting are not usable for them. The meeting organizer has not asked what alternative would work. What would you do next?

Pause and choose an answer in the activity. Then compare the explanations. Consider what you know about the participant's request and what you would need to check before deciding.

Choose an invitation, notice, meeting, or form you can influence, or continue with the example. Write down how someone can request language or communication support; what must happen before they participate; and who can help if you cannot resolve the request. Add one way to check whether the arrangement actually worked.

Use the existing access checklist and CLAS learning when you need more detail, and confirm current agency procedures. Next we will focus on the person's voice and on the relationships they choose to include in a decision.
