Look at the process as well as the encounter

A good conversation can reveal a barrier. The next step is to ask whether the process itself makes that barrier more likely. In our continuing example, a team is revising how people hear about a planning opportunity and how they can respond. The purpose and deadline are known, while the choice of communication and participation methods is still open.

Imagine tracing the experience from beginning to end. A person receives or discovers an invitation, works out what it means, considers whether they can participate, sends a response, and learns what happened next. Each step is connected. If the invitation is hard to understand, a convenient meeting time alone may not make the opportunity usable.

If someone can attend but cannot influence the question, attendance alone is not meaningful participation.

You do not need to know everything about a community before you begin. Start with the process you can see. Describe what it asks people to do, what is known about how it works, and whose experience has not yet been heard.

A community brief may help you prepare a better question; it cannot tell you whether a particular person received a notice or why they did not respond.

Suppose the current proposal offers one quick live meeting as the only way to give feedback. List what a participant would need to do: receive the invitation, understand it, find time, use the meeting format, and contribute while the meeting is taking place. Then consider what remains unknown. Do people need another language or format?

Could work schedules, caregiving, transportation, technology, or the time needed to think affect participation? Which conditions are supported by evidence, and which are questions to ask?

Avoid assuming that any barrier applies to a person because of a community label. A live meeting may work well for some people and poorly for others within the same community. Ask people affected what would help, and make sure input reaches beyond the people the team already knows. Pay attention to who has the authority, time, and resources to change the process.

Also examine the team's side of the process. What will happen to responses, who will review them, and what can still change? Adding a new way to respond is useful only if it is usable and the team can consider the input within the actual decision process. Name limits honestly instead of promising a choice that is not available.

A second response route might include an accessible written option, a telephone option, a supported conversation, or another method people identify as useful. These are possibilities, not automatic requirements or a promise that all can be offered immediately. Check accessibility, language assistance, privacy, the deadline and the program's current procedures before deciding what to recommend.

Compare the alternatives against the purpose of the opportunity. Who can use each option? What extra effort does it place on participants and staff? What evidence do you have, and what would you need to learn? If a change requires approval, bring a clear proposal to the responsible person.

Do not treat a community description as a substitute for a required equity analysis or as authority to change a policy.

The team has proposed one quick live meeting as the only way to provide feedback. Before the invitation is finalized, what would you do? Choose one of the three responses on this page, then explore the feedback. You can pause to think about access, the decision deadline and what is still open to change.

Consider what each option assumes and what it would leave the team able to learn. The feedback becomes available after you choose.

Choose a notice, form, meeting, or decision route from your own work. You may also continue with the planning invitation. In a private working file, draw a short sequence of steps. For each step, note one question: what does a person need in order to use this step, and what evidence would tell us whether it works?

Keep the note general. Do not include names, case details, health information, or other protected information in these learning notes. Identify an uncertainty, a person or office with relevant responsibility, and one possible improvement to discuss. If you do not have authority to make a change, your useful action may be to bring a well-formed question to the person who does.
