Act, listen and learn

You have followed one question through the learning: how can a notice, conversation, meeting, or program decision work better for the people it affects? You have examined your own expectations, used community context to prepare questions, considered communication and access, and looked at both the person's choices and the process around them.

Our planning opportunity is still an example for you to work through. The team has a purpose and a deadline; which methods are available, what people prefer, and which changes will be approved depend on the actual situation. Do not treat a possible improvement as something that has already happened. Describe the proposal, the open questions and the review it needs.

A useful plan can be small. It might begin with one accessible invitation, a clearer way to respond, or a question for the person responsible for the decision. Your contribution may be to test an idea within your role, or to prepare evidence and bring it to a colleague with the authority to decide. Either way, make the next step clear and realistic.

Start by naming the purpose: what should be easier or more equitable, and for whom? Describe the process being considered and the people who may experience it. List what you know, where the information came from, and what you still need to learn. Consider the effect of the current approach alongside at least one feasible alternative.

Next, identify how people can participate, what communication and access arrangements need to be confirmed, and what information may be shared. State any possible burden or unintended effect you want to examine. For example, a new online response option could help some people while creating more work for someone who cannot use it.

Do not assume a person's preferences from a name, language, disability, or community description; ask and follow the appropriate process.

Name who can review or authorize the proposed step, what decision is still open and when the team will return to it. If the work may have Tribal implications, follow the standing direction to contact the designated DHS Tribal relations or consultation office and the Office of Indian Affairs as appropriate.

An individual learning activity or a general engagement event does not replace that process or any required analysis.

Once an authorized change is in use, look for evidence that relates to its purpose. A count of invitations, attendance or responses can tell you about activity. It does not by itself show that people understood the material, could use the format, had influence, or experienced the process as more accessible.

Ask what information can be collected and shared under current rules, whose perspective is missing, and how to protect privacy. Consider whether a small number could identify someone. You might compare participation routes, review general comments, or ask whether materials were understandable, but do not collect information just because it is available. Choose measures that are appropriate to the work and the people involved.

Be honest about limits. A positive count may reflect several factors, and one response will not describe every person's experience. Where evidence is incomplete, say what remains uncertain and identify the next responsible question. Then return to the people who contributed with an appropriate account of what happened and what can still be considered.

Imagine that attendance at a planning session increased. Participants also say that the materials were hard to use and they do not know how their comments were considered. What would you recommend next? Choose one of the three responses on the page, then explore the feedback. Pause while you compare the attendance figure with the experience people reported.

Think about what each option would do with the information available. Once you make a choice, the feedback will explain its effects.

For your own practice, write a general plan with these prompts: purpose; decision or process; people affected; what is known; what remains unknown; communication and access; possible options and burdens; participation and appropriate partners; responsible review; the next step; and a date or occasion to check back. You can adapt the plan to the work you actually do.

Keep the learning note free of names, case details, health information, religious affiliation, immigration status, personnel information and other protected information. If you need to work with a real person's information, use the approved systems and procedures for that work. Follow the page's actual instructions about whether a note is saved or needs to be downloaded; the learning materials do not change your organization's records requirements.

A plan is not complete simply because the boxes are filled in. Revisit it when a person offers a different experience, when the decision changes, or when a responsible office provides further guidance. If the evidence shows a new barrier, explain it and take the next step within your role.

The community briefs remain available when you need background and better questions. The intercultural learning journey offers further reflection. The CLAS materials support attention to communication and access, while the Equity Analysis Toolkit and community engagement resources help connect learning to a decision and follow-through. Use a brief as context, never as an answer about an individual.

Before you leave, choose one next step you can take or one question you can bring to the right person. It does not have to solve the whole problem. A careful question, a usable invitation, a clear explanation, or an honest return conversation can make the next decision more thoughtful and more responsive to the people it affects.
