Put the person's voice and choices first

Welcome back. So far, you have considered the lens you bring, used community context to prepare questions, and looked at communication and access. Now let us bring those ideas into one conversation: how do we make room for the person who will live with the decision?

Our continuing example is a planning opportunity. A division team knows what the opportunity is for and when a decision is due. It is still working out how to explain the invitation, how people can respond, and what the responsible decision maker can consider. No single format or personal preference has been decided in advance.

A person-centered approach does not mean the staff member can promise every requested change. It means the person has a meaningful chance to understand the opportunity, say what matters to them, and take part in ways that work for them. Your role is to make the process clear, listen, and follow through on what you can do.

When a requirement or decision belongs to another office, say so and identify the right route.

Imagine a participant says they would like a trusted supporter to join a conversation and would also like some private time before responding. The supporter might be a relative, friend, advocate, or another person the participant chooses. Their relationship alone does not tell you what role the participant wants them to have.

You might begin: “Would you like your supporter here for the whole discussion, or for part of it? What would you like to talk through privately?” Give the person time to answer in their preferred way. Speak to them directly, provide appropriate communication support, and avoid directing every question to the supporter merely because that person is present.

Ask what the supporter is invited to do. They may help the person prepare questions, provide information the person wants shared, or offer another kind of support. Do not assume they make the decision. If a legal representative or another formal arrangement may affect the process, follow the applicable procedure and seek the appropriate guidance. Explain the roles that actually apply without guessing from a relationship or label.

A good invitation makes it easier to decide whether and how to take part. Explain the purpose in plain language, the time available, what is still open to influence, and how a person can respond. Ask what language, format, communication support, timing, or other access arrangements would be helpful. Arrange support through current procedures and check whether it works for the person.

When the participant asks for private time, discuss the available options and the timing honestly. For example, the person might want a break during a meeting or time afterward to consider an accessible copy of the information. Check which arrangements are available and whether there is a decision deadline.

If you cannot offer the requested timing, explain the constraint and seek an appropriate alternative rather than presenting the limitation as the person's fault.

Be clear about privacy without making promises you cannot keep. Explain how information will be used and any applicable limits, using current guidance. For this learning activity, you do not need to record a participant's name, health information, case details, or reasons for choosing support. A general note such as “check the available private response options” is enough.

Consider the participant who wants a trusted supporter at the conversation and private time before responding. Three possible responses are available on this page. Choose the one you would take first, then explore the feedback. You can pause the video while you decide.

Consider what each response would mean for the participant and the planning process. Once you make a choice, the feedback will explain its effects.

Think of a meeting, notice, or planning conversation connected to your work. Where does the process let a person say whom they want involved? Where could it make private thinking time or another response route clearer? If you are not responsible for the process, identify a question to bring to someone who is.

Write a general action rather than personal information. You might note: “Ask how the person wants a supporter involved, confirm available private response options, and explain the decision deadline.” If a question involves a specific case or protected information, use your organization's approved channels instead of these learning notes.

The existing CLAS learning and planning worksheet offer ways to consider communication, access and participation together. The access checks in the Library can help you examine whether a meeting, information, or response route can actually be used. These resources support your judgment; follow current program procedures and the person's stated needs.

In the next part, we will step back from one conversation and look at the process itself. A respectful interaction matters, and so does the way the notice, deadline, meeting, and response options work together for people who encounter them.
