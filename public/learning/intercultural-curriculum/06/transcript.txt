Build relationships and seek the right review

A relationship is more than an invitation sent once. In our continuing planning example, the team is considering how people will hear about an opportunity and how they can respond. Before contacting anyone, be clear about the purpose, what the team can still change, who makes the decision, and when a response is useful. These details help people decide whether and how they want to participate.

It is also important to say what you do not yet know. If a decision has already been made, do not invite input as though every option remains open. If the team can change the wording of an invitation but not the statutory deadline, explain that difference. When you are unsure what can be changed, check with the responsible person before making a promise.

Participation in this learning is individual and voluntary. The workplace action you consider may involve responsibilities, partners or review beyond your role. Use the learning to prepare a thoughtful question and the appropriate next step, not to assume authority you do not have.

Ask how people would like to receive information and what would make it possible to respond. Depending on the work, that may involve language assistance, accessible materials, a workable time, another way to contribute, or support for participating. Check what current guidance permits and what arrangements are available. Do not assume that everyone reached through the same organization shares one preference.

A community organization may offer valuable knowledge and relationships. Ask what role it wants and can take, and recognize that one partner cannot speak for everyone. Where appropriate and under the applicable process, plan for compensation, accessible participation and the time people need. Explain how their input will be considered and how the team expects to report back.

If you cannot make a commitment yourself, identify who can answer before presenting it as settled.

Protect information appropriately. Explain the purpose of a request, how information will be used and any applicable limits, based on current procedures. For this lesson, your own notes can remain general. You do not need to record anyone's name, case information, health details, immigration status or other private information.

There is an important distinction between an individual's choices, community engagement and government-to-government consultation. A person can tell you what support or communication works for them. Community engagement can help a team understand a range of experiences and shape an open decision. Neither automatically fulfills a government-to-government obligation.

The existing One DSD direction is to defer to the Office of Indian Affairs and the DHS offices responsible for Tribal relations and consultation for substantive matters concerning Native American individuals, communities or Tribal Nations. If a proposed action may have Tribal implications, contact the appropriate designated office or liaison before planning engagement or consultation, and follow its guidance and the applicable process.

Each Nation is a distinct sovereign government; a general community brief is not Nation-specific guidance.

You do not need to decide here exactly which consultation process applies. Recognize the potential implication, identify the responsible office, and ask how to proceed. Do not present a general listening session, an invitation to a community organization, or a brief you have read as a substitute for the designated process.

Consider what each option would mean for the team’s next step and the responsible offices. The feedback becomes available after you choose.

Return to your work question. Write a general plan with five items: the purpose; what people may influence; the responsible decision maker; the support needed for participation; and how the team will respond after hearing from people. Add any question you need to take to a designated office or other responsible partner.

If you do not know whether compensation, an interpreter, a particular format, or another arrangement is available, mark it as something to confirm. Do not put someone's private circumstances in your learning note. The value of your plan is that it makes responsibilities and unanswered questions visible before an invitation goes out.

A relationship also needs follow-through. After a decision, tell participants what was considered, what changed, what did not change and why, as appropriate to the process. If some information cannot be shared, explain what you can say and where questions can go. People should not have to guess whether their contribution reached anyone.

The community engagement and connections resources offer planning questions about purpose, influence and reporting back. The Minnesota Communities collection provides background for preparing questions, while the CLAS learning and the Equity Analysis Toolkit offer ways to examine access and a decision together. Use the standing Tribal deference and current designated guidance wherever they apply.

In the final part, we will bring the work together. You will prepare a small action and review plan, then consider how to tell whether a change helped the people it was intended to reach.
