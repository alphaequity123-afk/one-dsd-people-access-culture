Unconscious Bias in Everyday Decisions

One DSD People, Access and Culture

Foundation learning for all staff and supervisors

A first impression can influence a decision before you notice the assumptions behind it. These activities help you distinguish what you observed from what you inferred, compare options consistently, and reconsider a familiar explanation. Unconscious and implicit bias are overlapping terms. This companion supports practice; it does not diagnose beliefs or require an Implicit Association Test.

For all staff, including supervisors and managers. You may use the practice cases, reflect privately, discuss an example, or pass. No one needs to disclose personal experiences, identities, or assessment results.

What you will practice

1 Separate the observation from the story

An observation describes what happened. An interpretation gives it meaning. 'A person spoke loudly' is different from 'the person was hostile.' Loudness may reflect frustration, a poor connection, hearing needs, a familiar speaking style, or something else. The point is to check the meaning relevant to the exchange, rather than choose a cultural explanation in advance.

Automatic associations can influence attention and interpretation. That does not mean every quick judgment is wrong or that anyone can know someone's private beliefs from a single action. A useful pause makes room for evidence and another plausible account.

Explore the example

Practice example: During a phone conversation about a program notice, a staff member hears a raised voice and writes 'aggressive' in a draft note. The caller later explains that the connection was poor and they were trying to be heard. The original observation did not establish aggression.

Try it

Rewrite the note using only observable details relevant to the work. Add a neutral question, such as 'Can you hear me clearly, and what would you like me to explain about the notice?' Identify what new information would support or change your interpretation. Do not place a cultural or psychological label on the caller.

Check your reasoning

Which note stays closest to the evidence?

A. The caller's culture makes them aggressive.

B. The caller spoke loudly; the reason was not yet clear.

C. The caller has an unconscious bias.

Answer B. The second note describes an observation and its uncertainty. Neither a cultural explanation nor a diagnosis follows from speaking volume.

Reflect if useful: Which words in my notes describe a person's actions, and which assign a motive?

2 Check familiar shortcuts

Affinity bias can make familiarity feel like evidence of suitability. Confirmation bias can lead people to favor information that supports an existing view. A halo effect occurs when one favorable quality spills over into judgments about unrelated qualities. Perception can also be shaped by stereotypes that influence what people notice. These concepts help you examine a decision; they are not labels to assign to colleagues.

Before comparing people or proposals, identify what the task actually requires. Ask the same core questions of each option. Look for information that could change your preferred explanation, and distinguish a relevant qualification from personal comfort.

Explore the example

Practice example: A colleague favors a proposal from a familiar team because its previous presentation was polished. A less familiar team provides clearer evidence about the current question. Presentation skill may matter for some work, but it does not establish the quality of this analysis.

Try it

List three criteria relevant to the decision. Use them to compare both proposals. Identify which impression could be an affinity or halo effect, and what evidence would justify a different choice. Keep the judgment about the proposals rather than the character of either team.

Check your reasoning

What would best improve this comparison?

A. Select the familiar team to avoid uncertainty.

B. Select the unfamiliar team regardless of its evidence.

C. Compare both proposals against the same relevant criteria and examine contrary evidence.

Answer C. The third response tests the reasoning. Reversing a preference without examining the work simply replaces one shortcut with another.

Reflect if useful: What evidence would make me reconsider a choice that already feels right?

3 Notice when an assumption becomes a routine

A label can travel through a process. If a person is described as difficult, later staff may approach them defensively before reviewing the actual request. Repeated assumptions can influence whose concerns are heard and how much explanation they receive. Reviewing the routine helps the team move beyond asking whether one individual meant well.

A complaint history does not establish the merits of a new request or the person's character. Attend to the current question, relevant facts, and any actual safety requirements. Keep complaint handling and formal decisions within their appropriate processes.

Explore the example

Practice example: A staff member reviewing a service inquiry sees that the person previously challenged a decision. A colleague suggests avoiding contact because the person is 'always difficult.' The file does not describe a current safety concern. The staff member still needs to understand and respond to the inquiry.

Try it

Separate relevant information from a judgment about the person. Draft an opening that explains the purpose of contact and invites the person's current question. Identify where a formal complaint should be directed if that is what the person wants. The exercise does not ask you to resolve a real complaint.

Check your reasoning

How should the previous complaint affect this inquiry?

A. Use relevant facts without assuming the current request lacks merit or that the person is unsafe.

B. Treat it as proof that the person should receive less help.

C. Promise the requested outcome to prevent another complaint.

Answer A. The first response preserves a fair examination of the current inquiry. It neither penalizes the person for a complaint nor promises an outcome before review.

Reflect if useful: Where could an unsupported label keep being repeated in a process I use?

4 Review the decision and repair the record

Recognizing a shortcut is a beginning. A practical response might correct an inaccurate description, compare evidence more consistently, or make an opportunity clearer. If your action affected someone, acknowledge what happened without asking them to comfort you or accept an apology.

Learning about bias does not establish that bias has disappeared. Research on changing implicit measures also does not justify treating a test score as a reliable verdict about a person's future conduct. Examine the decision and its effects, and make room for feedback that challenges your preferred account.

Explore the example

Practice example: A team discovers that 'did not engage' in its meeting notes meant a colleague did not speak immediately. The colleague had sent written comments that were overlooked. The team corrects the summary, considers the comments, and agrees to review both spoken and written contributions before recording a decision.

Try it

Draft a brief correction naming the inaccurate statement, the evidence that changes it, and the practical repair. Identify how the team could check whether written contributions are considered in later decisions. Use the practice case without collecting individual bias scores or personal histories.

Check your reasoning

Which statement is supported after this correction?

A. Everyone on the team is now free from bias.

B. The team corrected an unsupported description and changed how it considers input.

C. Written comments are always more useful than spoken comments.

Answer B. The second statement describes what the example establishes. It does not turn one correction into a claim about everyone's beliefs or all future decisions.

Reflect if useful: What would help me correct an assumption promptly and without becoming defensive?
