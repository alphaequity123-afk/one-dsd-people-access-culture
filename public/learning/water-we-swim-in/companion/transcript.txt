The Water We Swim In: A Two-Voice Companion

Host 1: Welcome. This episode is a companion to The Water We Swim In, a six-module course from People, Access and Culture about Minnesota's majority culture and how it shapes public work. We will follow one planning meeting and walk through each module in order. As you listen, consider this question: which of my everyday habits at work feel like plain good manners to me, and might look like unwritten rules to someone else?

Host 2: Let's start with the meeting.

Host 1: A case manager who grew up in a small Minnesota town is meeting with a family who moved to the state two years ago. Their adult son receives disability services, and it is time to review his plan. A newer colleague, who moved here from another part of the country, is sitting in to learn. The meeting is polite and pleasant. At the end, the case manager feels it went well. The family agrees to everything. The newer colleague is not so sure.

Host 2: Why not?

Host 1: We will get there. The first module is called "The water we swim in."

Host 2: The title comes from a story, right?

Host 1: A novelist told it in a commencement speech. Two young fish meet an older fish, who asks how the water is. Later, one young fish asks the other, "What is water?" The point is that the most obvious realities are often the hardest to see. The module says most training on culture asks staff to learn about other people's cultures. That matters. But the culture a person grew up in rarely feels like a culture at all. It feels like normal.

Host 2: And for many people in Minnesota's public agencies, that normal is mainstream Minnesota culture.

Host 1: Yes. The module is careful about three things throughout. Patterns are not people: a pattern across a state describes an average, not how any particular coworker or family thinks. Minnesota culture is plural: Minnesotans of every background share in it and change it. And evidence varies in strength: some points rest on large surveys, others on interviews, essays or humor, and the course says which is which.

Host 2: So the case manager is not being criticized for being Minnesotan.

Host 1: Not at all. Seeing your own culture is not the same as criticizing it. The course says plainly that Minnesota's majority culture has real gifts, and later modules look at them with respect.

Host 2: Module two. "Minnesota nice, decoded."

Host 1: This one is affectionate and honest. It starts with what is good, because the warm side is real. The course notes that in 2023, about 40 percent of Minnesotans sixteen and older said they had volunteered in the past year, third among the states.

Host 2: That is a real strength.

Host 1: It is. Then the module looks at the code. It draws on a well-known humor book from the 1980s about how Minnesotans talk. In it, "That's different" works as a blanket reply on neutral ground. A guest must refuse food, and the host must insist. And an offer of help is refused before it is accepted.

Host 2: I recognize all of those.

Host 1: Many people will. Now back to the meeting. The case manager asks, "Does this plan look okay to you?" The father says, "Yes, it's fine." The case manager hears agreement.

Host 2: And the newer colleague?

Host 1: She noticed the father hesitated, looked at his wife, and then said yes. She is not sure "fine" meant fine.

Host 2: So the case manager is reading the family through her own code.

Host 1: Possibly. In her code, if someone disagrees, they will find a gentle way to say so. In the family's code, disagreeing with an official in a meeting might be impolite, or risky. The module also describes the guarded side: how "nice" can avoid conflict, and how newcomers often find Minnesotans friendly but hard to befriend.

Host 2: There is a line in the course about directions.

Host 1: A newcomer told a reporter that Minnesotans will give you directions to anywhere except their own house. It gets a laugh. And it points to something that matters in this meeting: politeness is not the same as an open door.

Host 2: Module three. "Where it comes from."

Host 1: This module traces mainstream Minnesota culture to its roots in settlement, faith, farm life, climate and civic tradition. It opens with a funeral lunch in a church basement. Folding chairs, coffee in a big urn, at least three kinds of hotdish. Nobody sent a sign-up sheet. People just knew what to bring.

Host 2: That is a very specific picture.

Host 1: And the point is that it has a history. The module traces habits like keeping your head down, pooling resources through cooperatives, and seeing government as a shared project, to particular communities and the problems they faced. So the habits we bring to work feel natural, and they also came from somewhere.

Host 2: How does that apply to our meeting?

Host 1: The case manager's habit of not pressing, of letting people offer concerns in their own time, comes from a culture where that is respectful. It is a good habit in many settings. But it assumes the other person shares the same understanding of when and how to speak up.

Host 2: Module four. "Seen from the other side."

Host 1: This module listens to how Minnesota's majority culture is experienced by Minnesotans of color, immigrants and newcomers. It says a culture looks one way from the inside and another from the outside. People inside see kindness. People arriving from elsewhere, or people the culture has kept at a distance, may see something more mixed.

Host 2: The course includes voices from people who stand partly outside the majority.

Host 1: It does, and it is careful to say none of them speaks for a whole community. It also faces parts of the state's racial history that the majority culture has not always talked about. The goal, in the module's words, is not to feel bad about home. It is to see home more completely.

Host 2: What does the family's experience look like from their side of the table?

Host 1: We do not know exactly, and we should not assume. But we can imagine some possibilities. They are in an official setting, in their second language, with someone who controls their son's services. They have been told the meeting is a review. They may not know what can change, or whether asking for more would put the current services at risk.

Host 2: So "fine" might mean "we don't want to cause problems."

Host 1: It might. Or it might mean fine. The point is that the case manager does not yet know.

Host 2: Module five. "Culture in the system we run."

Host 1: This module looks at how majority-culture values are built into disability policy and practice. It says disability services are one of the institutions Minnesotans built, and they carry our habits, including ideas about what help looks like, who should decide, and what a good life is.

Host 2: The module also covers history.

Host 1: It includes a content note, because it describes abuse, neglect and deaths in state institutions where people with disabilities were placed. That history still shapes how some people view public agencies today. Then it turns to the values that disabled people and families fought to write into Minnesota law: informed choice, person-centered planning, self-determination and the most integrated setting.

Host 2: Those are strong protections.

Host 1: They are, and the module honors them. It also notes that they carry assumptions that match the majority culture: that the individual is the main decision-maker, and that independence is a central goal.

Host 2: And some families see it differently.

Host 1: The module draws on particular studies of how some families in Minnesota describe disability, care and decision-making. It is careful to say the examples come from particular studies at particular times and do not describe every member of any community. For some families, decisions are made together, or with elders or faith leaders, rather than by one individual.

Host 2: In our meeting, the plan asks the son what he wants. He looks at his parents.

Host 1: The case manager might read that as the parents overriding his choice. It might be. Or it might be how this family makes decisions together, by the son's own preference. The law still requires that his choices count. The skill is asking in a way that finds out, rather than assuming either answer.

Host 2: Module six. "Seeing across difference."

Host 1: The last module opens with hotdish again. New Minnesotans picked it up and changed it, and the old Minnesotans ate it. The module is about that kind of change. It offers lenses for reflection, including a model from an intercultural researcher with six ways people meet cultural difference. It asks you to use those lenses as mirrors for your own habits, not as labels for other people.

Host 2: Mirrors, not labels. That is important.

Host 1: The module also has a section called "Whose same?" Treating everyone the same can mean treating everyone the way the majority culture expects. And a section called "Averages, not people," repeating the caution from the first module.

Host 2: And a section on what is worth keeping.

Host 1: Yes. Fairness and civic commitment. Follow-through and patience, shown in cooperative traditions that pool resources and keep talking until a fair deal emerges. And care: politeness, helpfulness, and a reluctance to embarrass people. The module asks you to choose which strengths to carry forward on purpose.

Host 2: So what does the case manager do differently next time?

Host 1: She keeps her warmth and patience. Those are strengths. And she adds a few habits. She explains at the start what can change in the plan and that asking will not put services at risk. She asks open questions instead of yes-or-no ones: "What would you change if you could?" She offers a follow-up call, so the family can raise concerns outside the meeting. She arranges an interpreter, even though the father speaks English, and lets the family choose whether to use one. And she asks the son, and the family, how they like to make decisions.

Host 2: And the newer colleague?

Host 1: She mentions what she noticed, gently. The case manager could have felt defensive. Instead she says, "I didn't see that. Thank you." That may be the most important moment in the whole story.

Host 2: Back to the question.

Host 1: Which of your everyday habits at work feel like plain good manners to you, and might look like unwritten rules to someone else? For the case manager, it was the gentle "Does this look okay?" that only works if everyone shares the code.

Host 2: Here is a next step. In your next meeting with a person or family whose background differs from yours, replace one yes-or-no question with an open one, and tell them plainly what can change and what cannot. Then, afterward, ask a colleague what they noticed that you did not.

Host 1: Thanks for listening. The Water We Swim In is in your People, Access and Culture learning library. It ends with questions for reflection, and those are yours to keep.
