Where it comes from

Host 1: Welcome to this module of The Water We Swim In. This one is called Where it comes from. It asks a simple question about the habits we bring to work. Where did they come from?

Host 2: And it starts in a church basement. A funeral lunch in a small Minnesota town, with long tables, folding chairs, coffee in a big urn and at least three kinds of hotdish.

Host 1: Nobody sent a sign-up sheet. People just knew what to bring. I have been to that lunch more times than I can count.

Host 2: The module points out that hotdish has a history. The Minnesota Historical Society's online encyclopedia traces the earliest known recipe to a 1930 cookbook from a Lutheran church women's society in Mankato.

Host 1: After World War Two it moved into home kitchens, served at showers and funerals as a way to show care. So something that feels like just food grew out of particular churches and particular immigrants. The module asks us to look at our work habits the same way.

Host 2: It begins with a helpful idea. A culture is partly a set of answers that earlier people worked out to the problems they faced.

Host 1: And it is honest about where the story begins. It begins with land. The encyclopedia describes the state's origins as primarily about the taking of land from Native people. The settlement story that follows began on land that was taken.

Host 2: Then come the settlers. By 1917, Germans were the state's largest single ethnic group. Hundreds of thousands of Norwegians arrived, often whole families who settled near one another because they feared extreme isolation. And more Swedes settled in Minnesota than in any other state.

Host 1: What struck me is the role of the church. It was school, social club and aid society, and later a social service agency.

Host 2: That's a good way to see it. It helps explain why so much help in Minnesota still flows through organizations.

Host 1: The module also describes a time when the majority culture learned to keep its head down. During World War One, a state commission watched German Americans closely. Swedish Lutheran churches moved to English services in the 1920s and 1930s, partly under pressure to Americanize.

Host 2: And it is careful here. It asks whether some of today's caution about standing out was learned in those years. Then it says plainly that no source answers that question.

Host 1: Many people point to a Scandinavian source instead. In a 2009 Minnesota Public Radio essay, the playwright Syl Jones tied Minnesota nice to the Jante law. Those are the unwritten rules of a fictional town in a 1930s novel, and they begin, "Don't think that you are special."

Host 2: The module reminds us that is one writer's essay, not a study. It is worth knowing because so many people find it familiar.

Host 1: It also names who the usual story leaves out. The Dakota and Ojibwe people whose land this was. Black Minnesotans of the Rondo neighborhood in Saint Paul and of north Minneapolis. And Mexican farmworkers, about seven thousand of whom worked the sugar beet fields as early as 1928.

Host 2: A culture's picture of itself is a selection. I keep coming back to that line.

Host 1: So where did Minnesota nice come from? A University of Saint Thomas sociologist, Lisa Waldner, told Minnesota Public Radio in 2019 that there is no definitive documentation.

Host 2: But she offered two likely threads. Northern European settlers clustered by group, so people lived side by side without mixing much. In her words, they "co-existed, but you didn't intermingle."

Host 1: The second thread is the farm. If your plow broke at harvest, you might need your neighbor. So politeness won out over hostility.

Host 2: Which sets up a real tension. There is a strong ethic of handling your own problems, right alongside a practical dependence on the people nearby.

Host 1: Winter is part of it too. The humorist Howard Mohr connected Minnesota sociability to a stoicism born of outlasting brutal winters. The module is clear that this is humor from a close observer, not research.

Host 2: And a University of Minnesota researcher, Grace Vieth, has linked newcomers' isolation partly to long winters, when people stay with the friends they already have. That is worth remembering when someone new joins your team in November.

Host 1: Then there are the cooperatives. In 1890 the Danish American community at Clarks Grove founded one of the state's first cooperative creameries. For decades it was also a community center, with talks, meetings and school recitals.

Host 2: By 1928, nearly half of all the cooperative creameries in the United States were in Minnesota. Pooling resources, sharing profits and losses, and meeting to decide. Those habits have deep roots here.

Host 1: The module then turns to government. The political scientist Daniel Elazar called Minnesota the archetypical example of a moralistic political culture. Politics is a shared search for the good society, and government is a positive instrument for the general welfare.

Host 2: The module asks us to treat that as a mirror, not a label. It is one scholar's classification, and it describes a state's public life, not any person in it.

Host 1: I appreciated that Elazar named a tension that matters for a large agency like the Minnesota Department of Human Services. He said this culture has a major difficulty with bureaucracy. And where merit systems exist, they tend to be rigidly maintained.

Host 2: The history behind it is real, though. The Farmer-Labor Party grew out of farmers and workers organizing. The 1967 state civil rights law passed with support from both parties. And the 1971 Minnesota Miracle shifted most school costs to the state.

Host 1: In 2024, about seventy-six percent of eligible Minnesotans voted, second in the nation. But the module also cites a 2025 column arguing that Minnesota exceptionalism was a myth. Easy consensus is part of the inheritance, but not a promise.

Host 2: Then it names the values under the surface. Each one is a strength, and each one can hide something. Self-reliance comes first, with a twist. Help here is often organized through institutions, and given more easily than it is asked for.

Host 1: Then treating everyone the same. The question is whether fairness means identical treatment, or treatment that gets people to the same place.

Host 2: Modesty and privacy are next. A culture that keeps its own troubles private may find it awkward to ask others about theirs. And so much of our work depends on asking.

Host 1: Right. And last, consensus and trust in process. Trust in institutions is a strength. It can also be a blind spot with people whose own or family history gives them reason to trust less.

Host 2: The module closes with a point I want to underline. None of these values belongs to every Minnesotan. They are inherited defaults, and knowing their history lets you choose when to lean on them and when to set them aside.

Host 1: There is a good example in the module. A new colleague who came from a state agency in the South is puzzled. Nobody takes credit, a coworker turned down help twice, and a small change took four meetings.

Host 2: The easy answer is that this is just how professional teams work. But that treats local habits as the universal standard. A better answer names the roots, then asks how her old team handled these things.
