Host 1: Minnesota built a system of state institutions for people with disabilities. Faribault State School and Hospital began in 1879. The Colony for Epileptics opened at Cambridge in 1925. Over time these places shifted from education to lifelong custodial care, and by the 1960s they were overcrowded and understaffed.

Host 2: Then there are the eugenics laws. A 1917 law let county probate judges commit people judged feeble minded without their families' consent. Being poor or behaving unusually could count as a sign. A 1925 law authorized sterilization.

Host 1: At least 2,204 Minnesotans were sterilized between 1925 and 1945, 77% of them women, and the true number is unknown, because no central board kept records.

Host 2: What struck me is that the people behind this saw themselves as reformers. The module's lesson is not that reform is dangerous. It is that confidence in our own good intentions is not a safeguard.

Host 1: That's a hard one to sit with, especially for people who chose this work to help. But families pushed back. The parents of a resident at Cambridge brought a class action, and in 1974 a judge ruled that residents had a constitutional right to treatment.

Host 2: Parents also pressed for the Developmental Disabilities Waiver and helped negotiate closing the state institutions. And in 1994, disability self-advocates formed Remembering With Dignity to give named stones to around 13,000 people buried in numbered or unmarked graves.

Host 1: The module asks us to notice the arc. Institutions run for people, then parents suing on their behalf, then self-advocates speaking for themselves. The question moved from how do we care for people, to who decides.

Host 2: And for a culture proud of helping, sharing control can be uncomfortable. But that shift is what today's policy rests on.

Host 1: Which brings us to the law. Minnesota law says adults with disabilities, and children with support from their families, may make informed choices about services. That information must be given in a culturally and linguistically appropriate way, and the person's cultural needs must be identified.

Host 2: The licensing law for home and community-based services calls for person-centered planning. It asks what is important to the person as well as for the person, and it calls for self-determination and the most integrated setting.

Host 1: These are strong protections, won by disabled people and families. And the module points out that they also carry majority-culture assumptions. The individual is the main decision-maker. Independence is a central goal. The most integrated setting is usually best.

Host 2: Right. Seeing those as cultural values, not simply as correct, does not weaken them. The law itself names the tension when it asks us to identify cultural needs.
