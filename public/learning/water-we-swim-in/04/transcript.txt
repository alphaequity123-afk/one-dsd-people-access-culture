Seen from the other side

Host 1: Welcome to this module of The Water We Swim In, part of the People, Access and Culture learning program at the Minnesota Department of Human Services. This one is called Seen from the other side. It asks how Minnesota's majority culture looks to people who stand partly outside it.

Host 2: And it starts with a joke. A newcomer from Fargo was warned that Minnesotans will give you directions to anywhere except their own house. Most of us laugh, because we know that person. Friendly, helpful, means well.

Host 1: Right. And the newcomer may still drive away feeling like a guest. A culture looks one way from inside and another way from outside.

Host 2: I like how the module frames the goal. It isn't about feeling bad about home. It's about seeing home more completely.

Host 1: The first section gathers voices from people who stand partly outside the majority, and it's careful to say none of them speaks for a whole community. One that stayed with me is Arthur McWatt, a St. Paul native and retired social studies teacher, speaking in a 2002 public radio documentary.

Host 2: He described employers who accepted a Black applicant's application and then threw it away. He called it discrimination done in a more subtle way than in the South. And he said Black Minnesotans developed an antenna for insincerity, because racism here often came with a smile and a polite voice.

Host 1: He also said Black Minnesotans generally felt safer here than in the South. The module keeps both of those things in view.

Host 2: That balance runs all through it. A General Mills executive put it neatly. A job gets newcomers the nine to five. The harder part is the five to nine.

Host 1: The evenings, the weekends, the friendships. A 2012 public radio series found professionals of color felt accepted but not really understood. Yet the same story included a refugee who said Minnesotans welcomed him and gave him a job before he spoke English well.

Host 2: And the module simply says both experiences are real. It doesn't ask us to choose one story.

Host 1: There are voices from inside the majority, too. A sociologist who grew up in Stillwater in the 1970s wrote that her family taught her people are people. By not talking about race, she didn't really see it. She now sees that kind of colorblindness as discounting the experiences of people of color.

Host 2: That's a hard one, because it comes from a good place. Another sociologist, Lisa Waldner at the University of St. Thomas, described the idea that being polite to everyone means there are no issues around race. She didn't call it malicious. She called it a form of denial.

Host 1: Then there's the finding every public agency should sit with. Immigrant and refugee participants in a 2021 Twin Cities planning effort on belonging described over-engagement. They were asked for input again and again, and never saw it in the final product.

Host 2: That one lands for us. We ask communities for input all the time. The real question is whether people can see what their input changed.

Host 1: Exactly. The module sums it up well. Kindness and avoidance can look the same from inside, and very different from outside.

Host 2: The next section names what Samuel Myers Junior at the University of Minnesota calls the Minnesota Paradox. Minnesota is one of the best places to live in America. Yet by racial gaps in income, homeownership, test scores and more, African Americans are worse off here than in virtually every other state.

Host 1: And there's a nuance worth holding. A 2020 report noted that many indicators for Black Minnesotans aren't worse than elsewhere. The gaps are wide partly because white Minnesotans do better than average.

Host 2: Myers also names a habit. In the 1990s, leaders blamed rising problems on migrants from Chicago, Detroit and Gary. The problem was placed outside, so it wasn't Minnesota.

Host 1: The module calls that a mirror, not a verdict. The question is whether any of us has reached for that move ourselves. When something goes wrong, do we look outside first?

Host 2: Then the module turns to history that sat out of view. Starting in 1910, Minneapolis developers wrote racial covenants into property deeds. By the 1930s most new developments in the Twin Cities carried them. The legislature banned them in 1953.

Host 1: They sat quietly in county deed books. Today the University of Minnesota's Mapping Prejudice project maps them with community volunteers. And a 2019 state law lets homeowners formally discharge a covenant.

Host 2: Then there's Rondo. It was the center of Black life in St. Paul, with its own clubs, a credit union and a cooperative. Interstate 94 cut it in half, and six hundred families lost their homes.

Host 1: What struck me is the comparison with Merriam Park, a mostly white, professional neighborhood. Well-connected residents there won several changes. Rondo didn't have that political clout.

Host 2: And the lesson for anyone in public work is pointed. A process can be open to everyone and still respond most to people who already know how it works.

Host 1: The module also covers the 1920 lynchings in Duluth, with a content note so readers can pause. Three Black circus workers were killed by a mob. Their deaths were mostly forgotten until 1991. The long forgetting, the module says, is part of the cultural story too.

Host 2: Then 2020. After George Floyd was killed by a Minneapolis police officer, the Minnesota Department of Human Rights investigated the city and its police department. Its main conclusion is the part this course cares about most.

Host 1: Right. The department found the pattern was caused primarily by an organizational culture. Without real culture change, it said, new policies and trainings would be meaningless.

Host 2: The module is clear that the report concerns one police department and doesn't describe DHS. But the idea applies to any public institution. Culture can outlast policy. It's shaped by the tone leaders set, how norms are taught, and what happens when they're broken.

Host 1: The last section looks at repair. There's the covenant discharge law, the 2020 pardon of Max Mason, Duluth's memorial work, and Rondo Days, held since 1983. In state government, a 2023 executive order continued the state's equity councils and directed agencies to carry out a long-range equity plan.

Host 2: And the module doesn't pretend everyone agrees. A policy fellow at the Center of the American Experiment argued that equity means guaranteeing equal outcomes, which she saw as discrimination. The Minnesota State chancellor defended equity work, saying Minnesota needs everybody in the workforce.

Host 1: You don't have to pick a side. The module asks us to notice that people of good faith disagree, and that the habit of treating everyone the same sits right at the center of that disagreement.

Host 2: So what do we take from all this? For me, it's that my view of a meeting or a letter is only one view. People farther from the center may be seeing something I can't.

Host 1: That's a good way to put it. Here's the one thing to try this week. Ask a colleague, partner or family member from outside your own background how a recent meeting, letter or process looked to them.

Host 2: Then listen without explaining what you meant. That last part is the hard part. Thanks for spending this time with us. The full module has the sources and much more detail, so please take time to read it.
