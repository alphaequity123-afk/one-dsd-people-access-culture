People, Access and Culture in Practice: A Disability Services Division Example

A person opens a letter about support at

home. A new colleague begins work.

A team prepares a recommendation.

Each moment raises the same question:

will this decision make it easier for people

to participate and get what they need?

People, Access and Culture connects those

moments. These illustrative situations draw on

Disability Services Division work. They show how

the Equity Outcome Map can guide practice across

DHS, with One DSD as a worked example.

The map describes four intended outcomes:

centering equity for DHS, centering equity for

Minnesotans, a continuous learning cycle, and

centering community.

Its six guiding principles describe how to

pursue that work.

The Aging and Disability Services

Administration's six implementation goals

identify areas for action. Outcomes, principles,

and implementation goals play different parts.

A single improvement can support more than one

outcome.

The first three goals are Eliminate Disparities,

Community Engagement, and Hiring and Retention.

The next three are Learning and Development,

Contracts and Procurement, and Communication and

Accessibility.

Together, they connect people's experiences with

decisions about services, relationships,

employees, and resources. Let's follow that

connection into the work.

Community First Services and Supports, or CFSS,

offers support for independence at home and in

the community. Imagine someone reading

information about moving to this program and

wondering what to do next.

The team checks the current official guidance.

Then it asks people who use services what

is unclear, what format they need, and where

they would turn for help.

Using the Equity Analysis Toolkit,

plain-language guidance, and the National CLAS

Standards for culturally and linguistically

appropriate services, the team revises the

information. One DSD's language-access resources

help it explain the next step and where

to get assistance, without promising a decision

it cannot make.

Community Engagement and Communication and

Accessibility support the goal to Eliminate

Disparities. The result question is concrete:

can people understand the next step and obtain

help, including those whose needs the first

version overlooked?

Now consider MnCHOICES. Counties, Tribal

nations, and managed care organizations use it

in assessment and support planning for people

who need long-term services and supports.

The conversation should begin with the person's

preferences and the life they want.

In our example, someone leaves a planning

conversation unsure which supports were

discussed or whom to contact.

The question is how to make that next

step clearer while keeping the person's choices

central.

The team uses person-centered learning and

collaboration resources to clarify the

explanation and handoff. Disability Hub

Minnesota can help people explore options and

find information; eligibility and service

decisions remain with the responsible agencies.

Staff check back: does the person understand

their options and know where to turn?

That connects community participation and

learning with access, rather than counting a

referral as a successful outcome.

A different team is considering a contract for

provider training and technical assistance.

This is an illustrative purchasing decision,

separate from Medicaid provider enrollment or an

individual's services.

Using the contracting learning resources and

Equity Analysis Toolkit, staff examine the

proposed scope, outreach, and accessibility.

Could capable organizations learn about the

opportunity and understand what is needed?

The responsible procurement colleagues review

proposed changes.

Contracts and Procurement becomes a question

about meaningful access and useful work:

does the purchased assistance reach the people

it is intended to support?

Inside DSD, a new colleague needs more than

a list of links. They need clear expectations,

accessible materials, working relationships, and

room to ask questions.

A supervisor uses onboarding and collaboration

resources to plan early conversations and

relevant learning. Hiring and Retention connects

with Learning and Development. Later, they ask

whether the colleague can contribute, find help,

and raise concerns. Finishing a course alone

does not show that workplace conditions or

retention improved.

These same questions belong in policy,

legislative, and bill analysis. Suppose a

proposed waiver change could affect access.

Staff distinguish the proposal from enacted law

and current program guidance before describing

what it would mean.

The policy and legislative course and Equity

Analysis Toolkit help them examine who may

benefit, who may face barriers, and whose

perspective is missing. Their recommendation

explains the evidence, alternatives, and

questions still requiring an answer.

One DHS and One DSD People, Access and

Culture bring relevant learning and practical

resources together. Begin with work you actually

need to do: improve a notice, prepare a

recommendation, strengthen a partnership, or

welcome a colleague.

Choose learning at a useful depth, use the

appropriate tool, and involve the people

affected. The applications support that work;

they do not replace official guidance or the

people responsible for decisions.

For directors, managers, supervisors, and staff,

the connection is practical: choose an intended

outcome, connect the relevant ADSA goal, and

decide what to do next.

Then ask what changed for people, what remains

difficult, and what to learn together.

That is how People, Access and Culture moves

from a shared purpose into everyday practice.
