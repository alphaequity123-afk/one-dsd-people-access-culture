An Equity Pause in ten minutes

Before a routine decision goes out
Many of the decisions that shape people's working days are small ones: a meeting time, a notice, a change to a form. Because they are routine, they rarely get a second look, and that is where barriers slip through. The Equity Pause for a routine decision gives you a short, honest check before a decision goes out. Let's work through one. Imagine you supervise a team in the Disability Services Division, and you plan to move your biweekly team meeting from Tuesday afternoon to Monday at eight in the morning.

Start with the first question
The page lays out Six steps to work through in order. The first, Consider the first question, invites you to browse published questions for a decision like yours, then describe your own decision, identify the people affected, and note what evidence or fuller review it may need. The second step, Consider community context, is marked If relevant. Your meeting is internal, so you might set it aside today, but when a decision reaches particular Minnesota communities, those briefs describe access needs and what not to assume.

Resources, then your pause
The third step, Choose practical resources, points you to the plain-language and language access checklists for anything people will read. Your invitation is short, but its purpose, time and way to join still need to be clear. Then comes the heart of the practice, Write your pause. Download the checklist and write your answers in a tool you normally use, because this page does not save them.

Who it affects differently
Begin with The decision, in one sentence: moving the team meeting to Monday at eight. Next comes Who this affects, and who it affects differently. The help text asks you to think about language, disability, schedule, location, technology, trust, and who might miss it entirely. Thinking it through, you realize that two team members start later on Mondays because of caregiving, one person works a schedule with Mondays off, and a colleague who is Deaf relies on an interpreter who must be booked well ahead.

Who decides, and access
For Who can change this decision, and who you checked with, you note that you set the meeting time as supervisor, and that you asked the team before settling it. Under Access needs you checked, the guidance names plain language, an accessible format, interpretation or translation, timing, and a way to take part or respond that does not require one channel. So you confirm an interpreter for the new time, keep captions on, and plan to send the agenda a day ahead so people can add items in writing.

Alternatives and assumptions
Then comes What you know, and what you do not know yet. You know the Tuesday slot now conflicts with another standing meeting. You do not know whether an early Monday start works for everyone, and that is the question you would ask with five more minutes. For One alternative that would reduce a barrier, you consider Monday at ten instead of eight, choose it, and note why. Under What you chose not to assume, you write that silence after an invitation does not mean the time works for everyone.

Next step and review
The last two questions turn your thinking into follow-through. For Your next step, and who is responsible, you will send the revised invitation and book the interpreter yourself. For When you will look at this again, you choose a date about a month out, after the new time has had an effect. Now move to Review your pause. Confirm the decision is specific, you checked access, you know who can change it, you noted what you did not assume, and you have a next step with a date. If the reach or the stakes are larger than you first thought, move to the Equity Analysis Toolkit.

What a useful result looks like
The page is candid about what makes a pause real. A pause completed after the decision already went out, or one that says no one is affected when no one was asked, still needs work. A useful pause names a specific decision, the people it affects differently, and one thing you changed or kept with a reason. If a question remains, the optional last step, Talk it through, helps you find the right person. Nothing you read or download here goes to your supervisor. Pick one routine decision this week and give it ten minutes.
