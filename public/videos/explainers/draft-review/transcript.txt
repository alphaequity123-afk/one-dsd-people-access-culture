Reviewing a draft for equity, access and plain language

Before it goes out
A notice or form is often the only way a person learns what the Disability Services Division needs from them, and by when. If they cannot read it, use it or trust it, the decision behind it may never reach them fairly. On the Practice page, scroll to Review before it goes out. You will see two guided practices. One is for reviewing someone else's draft, and the other is for checking something you wrote yourself. You will use each of them here, along with the plain language checklist.

What you will end with
Open Reviewing a draft for equity, access, and plain language. The opening tells you what you will have at the end: a review record for one specific draft. It captures what the draft asks of people, who may be excluded, your access and language findings, the sources behind each claim, the changes you suggest and what remains for the owner. Bring a real draft, but keep confidential, predecisional or personal material out of your notes, and work in a tool you already use.

Six steps in order
The path is laid out as Six steps to work through in order. You begin with Examine the draft, recording what it asks people to do, where access or language may create a barrier, and the evidence for each finding. If the draft is aimed at particular Minnesota communities, the second step points you to the relevant brief for naming, language and access. The third step, Choose practical resources, links to the plain-language checklist, the language access checklist and a set of journey and burden questions. Let's open the first of those.

The plain language checklist
The Plain language and accessible documents checklist opens with why this matters. If a person cannot read or use a notice, they may not be able to understand or appeal the decision. Under Use the checklist, aim for sentences of fifteen to twenty words, one idea each. Use everyday words and say who does what by when. Write link text that describes the destination, never click here. Describe important images in alternative text, and make sure error messages on a form explain what to fix. Then run the built-in accessibility checker and ask someone outside the team to describe what the document means to them.

Recording what you find
Back on the practice, the Draft review record shows what a complete review covers. You note what the draft asks people to do, including steps, documents, deadlines and return methods. You consider who may be excluded or burdened, by language, disability, technology, time, trust or place. You label the source of each claim and flag anything cited from memory. You also note what you chose not to assume about who reads it. The record is for browsing and downloading only, so nothing you write is collected or stored here.

A useful result
What a useful result looks like names the common shortcuts. Approved with no findings still needs work. So does a rewrite that changes the meaning without telling the owner, and a reading-level number with no specific change attached. A result is ready when findings are specific and tied to who is affected, sources are labeled, and the owner knows exactly what remains. The final step, Return it to the owner, makes the boundary clear. The author or content owner decides what changes, and your review helps them without approving the draft.

Checking your own writing
When the draft is your own, open Checking your own material for access, assumptions, and tone. It asks you to read in three passes. The first covers access and plain language. The second asks what you assumed people already know, such as deadlines, formats and places stated in full, and dates that avoid major religious observances or offer an alternative. The third pass is tone: invitational rather than punitive, built on strengths and goals rather than deficits. A good result includes a sentence that now tells people how to succeed instead of what happens if they fail.

A second reader
The last step, Ask a colleague to review it, is optional, and it carries a simple truth: a second reader catches what the writer cannot. The draft review path gives that colleague a structure, and the right office can help with language access or accessibility questions. For your next step, choose one notice or form you expect to send soon. Download the checklist from either practice, run a single pass this week, and change one sentence so the person reading it knows exactly how to succeed.
