When a decision belongs to a person

When you need a person
Good preparation can take you a long way, but some decisions are not yours to make alone, and knowing who holds them saves time and protects the people affected. The Support page puts it simply: much of the time, the right resource is enough. When a decision belongs to a person, this page helps you find the supervisor, Equity Director or Specialist, policy owner or office who holds it.

Four ways to begin
Support offers four ways in. Ask is a place to put a question in your own words and get a considered answer, with its sources and its limits, without needing a meeting. The Library holds reviewed checklists, job aids, learning material and practice guidance, searchable by topic. Explore an area of work lets you begin with the decision or task in front of you. And Find the right person matches the work to the supervisor, Equity Director or Specialist, policy owner or office responsible for it. That last one is our focus here.

Tell us about the work
Find the right person asks you to tell it what kind of work you are doing. Under What kind of help do you need, you choose your role in this work, the area of work, and the timing. Timing ranges from planning ahead to work that is already in use and needs prompt attention. One request matters here: describe the work in general terms, and leave out names, complaints and private case details. The page needs only the shape of the work to point you somewhere useful.

Is the work within DSD?
Next, the page asks whether the work sits within the Disability Services Division. You can say it does, that it is outside the division, or that you are not sure. Not being sure is a perfectly reasonable answer, and the page will still help you find the right person or confirm which division owns the work. When your answers are in place, select Show who can help.

Who can help
Your results appear in two parts. First comes a place to start on your own, with timing advice and links to review the area and find reviewed material. Then comes a list called People and offices to consider, with your most likely starting point near the top. For a staff member, that is often your supervisor or manager. Before reaching out, confirm the named contact through the current DHS staff directory, the intranet or your supervisor. Contact details appear only once the responsible office has confirmed them.

One letter, three questions
Further down, a practice example shows why one piece of work can need more than one partner. A team wants a revised appointment reminder to work for the people who receive it. Whether the message is clear is a question for the person responsible for the communication. Whether people can use it calls for language and accessibility support. Whether the process works belongs with the responsible program team. As the page says, the questions help prepare a conversation, and the right person depends on the work.

When another team can help
Some matters belong with another team from the very start. Complaints, investigations, grievances, accommodation decisions, and discipline about a named person go to Employee Culture, Human Resources, or the civil-rights channel. Questions of legal interpretation go to the policy or legal owner. And anything touching a Tribal Nation goes first to the Office of Indian Policy or your Tribal liaison, through the separate government-to-government or Tribal relations process when it applies. Learning on this site can inform those conversations, but it never replaces them.

Nothing is sent
Using this page commits you to nothing. Your choices stay on the page, no one is contacted, and no request is submitted. It simply helps you see who holds the authority you need, so your next conversation starts in the right place. The next time a decision feels bigger than your role, open Find the right person. Answer the three questions in general terms, then reach out to the first person on your list.
