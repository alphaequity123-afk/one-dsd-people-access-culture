Analyzing a decision at the right depth

Match the effort to the stakes
Every week you make decisions that land on other people: a meeting time, a new form, a change in how someone reaches a service. Some deserve ten careful minutes, and a few deserve weeks of shared attention, but it is not always obvious which is which. On the Practice page, the section called Analyze a decision at the right depth helps you decide. Its starting idea is simple and honest: equity analysis scales with the stakes. You can start small and go deeper only when the work calls for it.

The quick check
The first level is the Quick check, which takes about ten minutes. It fits routine decisions, such as a schedule, a template, or a small change to a process. The link, Start an Equity Pause, opens a short guided practice that asks who the decision affects differently, who can change it, which access needs apply, and one alternative you could choose. A quick check is not a formality. If it surfaces a real barrier, or a group that would experience the decision differently, the page tells you plainly to move up to a standard analysis.

The standard analysis
The same three levels appear in the Equity Analysis Toolkit. A Standard analysis takes an hour or more, and it fits decisions that change how people reach services, information, or opportunities. Work through an equity analysis leads to a guided practice for shaping a decision while it can still change. Open the drafting workspace lets you draft and save your analysis as you go. If the barrier turns out to be built into a process or a system, rather than a single choice, an in-depth review is the right next step.

Why depth matters
Further down the toolkit page, a short example shows why depth matters. A team revising a service notice could record that the notice was sent, but that only records an activity. It says nothing about whether people understood the notice or could act on it. A real analysis asks whose next step is unclear, which language or format barriers need attention, and what the people receiving the notice say would help.

The in-depth review
The third level is the In-depth review, which usually takes several sessions. It fits policies, changes to home and community-based services, and barriers that run through an entire process. Here the guidance shifts from what you can do on your own to who needs to be part of the work. The page asks you to involve the people affected and the responsible office before the decision is final, while their perspectives can still shape it.

Choosing a review
On the Equity reviews page, you can choose among three kinds of review. The Policy, process, and service equity review examines who is affected, compares alternatives, and records responsibilities and follow-through. The Home and community-based services equity review explores service access, provider capacity, and the experiences of people using those services. The Structural equity analysis traces a barrier through a process and plans a change with a responsible role and a review date. As the page reminds you, a completed form is a starting point for sound judgment, not proof that a decision is equitable.

When a quick check is not enough
One boundary deserves special attention. When policy at the Department of Human Services requires a full equity analysis, a quick check does not satisfy it, and the section links straight to the policy so you can confirm. The DHS Equity Policy asks staff to use the Equity Analysis Toolkit before work that lasts or needs leadership approval is final. It also says that a short timeline alone does not establish that an equity scan is sufficient. So when a decision will last, needs leadership approval, or reaches further than you expected, treat a quick check as a first look, not the finish line.

Your next step
The best way to build this habit is to try it on something real. Think of one decision you will make this week, perhaps a meeting invitation or a small schedule change, and open the Equity Pause from the Practice page. Ten minutes will tell you either that you can go ahead with confidence or that the decision deserves a deeper look. Either answer is useful, and nothing here scores or ranks you. Start small, stay honest about what you find, and let the stakes decide how far you go.
