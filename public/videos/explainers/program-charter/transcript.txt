The Program charter in five minutes

Why a charter matters
When you bring a real question to this site, it helps to know what the program stands for and where it stops. That is the job of the Program charter. It sets out the principles and responsibilities guiding One DSD People, Access and Culture, a resource for staff of the Disability Services Division. Notice the honest framing near the top: this is a proposed charter. Applicable law, approved Department of Human Services policies and existing decision authority continue to govern official work. The charter organizes the program around those obligations rather than replacing them.

Mission and intended outcomes
The first article states the mission. The program exists to help staff embed equity in their work and their workplace, connecting learning, community knowledge, evidence, practical recommendations, authorized decisions and results. It also defines what operationalizing equity means here: considering equity in how work is designed, decided, resourced, implemented, evaluated and revised. Success is not measured by how many people finish a course. Instead, the charter looks for changes in barriers, access, participation and experience, alongside evidence that someone actually followed through.

Authority and limits
Article two is where the program draws its firmest lines. Applicable law and binding agency requirements come before any program guidance or learning example. Official decisions stay with the people and offices that hold the relevant authority. And there is a rule against substitution: a practice draft is never presented as an approved policy, a completed official review or a demonstrated result. That distinction protects you. Whatever you prepare here can strengthen your work, but it remains clearly labeled as your preparation, not a decision someone else has already made.

Operating principles
The operating principles describe how the work should feel in practice. Equity is considered while decisions are still open and proposals can still change. Before treating a problem as one person's failing, you examine the rules, resources, incentives and decision rights around it. Affected people contribute knowledge and real influence. Accessibility and language access are design conditions, not afterthoughts. Evidence and uncertainty stay visible. One principle deserves special attention: participation shall not become surveillance. Your private reflection and voluntary learning are kept separate from any scoring or monitoring of employees.

From question to action
Article five describes a common path that connects a work question to evidence, analysis, options, review, action and learning from results. The depth of review should match the significance, uncertainty and likely effects of the decision, so a small question does not demand a full study. Your work record should say accurately whether something is a draft, reviewed, decided, implemented or still waiting. The next article is candid about scope as well. Consultation intake and response-time commitments are excluded from the current application, and existing specialist and decision routes stay exactly where they are.

Information and privacy
Later articles carry these commitments into policy, grants and contracts, workforce and culture, measurement, learning, and accessibility. Article twelve speaks directly to how your information is handled. Public learning, private reflection and authorized operational information are kept in separate environments. The program explains where a draft is saved, how you can recover it and what may be lost. It collects only what a stated purpose needs, asks you to leave case and personnel details out of practice, and will not reuse information for an unrelated purpose without proper authority and transparency.

Ask and its limits
Article thirteen is titled Ask and the judgment of responsible people. Ask is a place to put a question in your own words and receive a considered answer with its sources and limits. It can support research, drafting and analysis. It never makes official eligibility, personnel, legal, procurement or other decisions that belong to the people responsible for them. Sources stay in view, and the difference between evidence and interpretation stays clear. A responsible person reviews any suggestion before it is relied on in official work, and nothing is sent or published without explicit authorization.

Stewardship and adoption
The final articles turn the charter on the program itself. Resources carry their source, review and version information, and new capabilities are checked for evidence, accessibility, information handling and boundaries before release. Article sixteen is plain about status: the charter is not treated as adopted until the necessary authorized review takes place. Until then, the priority is strengthening trust, sources, useful work records and the connection between learning and practice. A good next step is to open the article closest to your own work and read it with a current decision in mind.
