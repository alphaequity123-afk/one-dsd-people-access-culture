Equity Outcome Map

An ordinary decision can change someone's

experience of government. A notice or a program

requirement can open a door, or make it

harder to get through. At the Minnesota

Department of Human Services, everyday choices

are part of the equity story.

The Equity Outcome Map for 2025 through 2026

asks how those choices can add up to

something better: services that meet

Minnesotans' needs, and a workplace where people

can contribute and build a career.

Here is how the map took shape.

In fall 2025, the Office of Strategy and

Performance partnered with the Office of

Innovation and Impact and the Office of Employee

Culture. Their inquiry looked across the department.

The question was practical. What is already

helping equity become part of everyday work?

Where does progress get stuck?

Understanding both would matter before deciding

what to strengthen or change.

The team reviewed more than 25 documents,

dashboards, and webpages, and held 34

interviews. Written plans showed one part of the

picture. People's perspectives helped explain

the conditions surrounding that work.

The inquiry unfolded in four connected phases,

moving from an initial picture toward deeper

understanding, and then a chance to shape the work together.

An initial meeting and document review began in

September 2025, establishing a starting point

for understanding the department's equity work.

Interviews and deeper document review followed.

Analysis then brought those materials together,

looking for connections between the work people

described and the barriers they encountered.

In April 2026, the findings moved into

co-creation. The next question became how to

turn what had been learned into a shared direction.

Thirty-four people contributed perspectives from

different parts of DHS, bringing several views into the inquiry.

Eleven participants came from Innovation and

Impact, four from Diversity Recruitment and

Retention, and four from the Office of Employee Culture.

Another nine came from A D S A,

B H A and H S S A,

H C A, O I G, and Operations.

Six more came from Tribal and Urban Indian

Relations, Community Relations, and the C E C

L C, adding relationship and community

perspectives to the picture.

Across this work, six principles provide a

shared compass. Equitably serving all

Minnesotans keeps the purpose in view:

the people affected by the department's decisions.

Transparency and trust, along with mutual

respect and psychological safety, create room to

raise a concern and hear a different perspective.

Continuous learning and accountability connect

reflection with responsibility. Stability and

sustainability keep attention on lasting work.

And the final principle, we are stronger

together, ties those commitments to collaboration.

The interviews also described barriers.

A shared aspiration alone cannot remove the

conditions that get in its way.

Participants identified unclear roles, work

culture, systemic racism, and silos.

Limited access to data, stretched staffing, and

limited time could make it harder to understand a problem or act on it.

They also described superficial community

engagement, turnover, gaps in accountability,

the political climate, and uneven training.

Another concern ran through the picture:

equity could be treated as an add-on, without

a shared understanding of what it means.

The map responds by connecting equity to the work itself.

That connection becomes a theory of change:

how better conditions and different choices

might contribute to better outcomes.

It gives the work a direction to test.

The starting condition is that DHS centers

equity throughout its work and operations,

including policies, contracts, training, and performance plans.

The intended result is that Minnesotans receive

the services they need, without disparities in

access and experience, and can live their

healthiest, fullest lives. These are ambitions

the work must help advance.

The same theory looks inward:

DHS would become an inclusive employer where all

people can build a career.

Neither result is automatic; progress must be examined.

The outcome map puts the pieces together.

Policy and strategy give direction.

Activities turn that direction into work people

can recognize and take part in.

Those activities include workforce and hiring,

learning, data, engagement, integrity, and

collaboration. Communication, meetings,

workgroups, and resources help people carry the

work across organizational boundaries.

The connections point toward four intended

outcomes. How might an activity improve

someone's experience, and what would show whether it does?

First is centering equity for DHS:

making it part of everyday work, sustaining it

through leadership changes, and creating

conditions where staff can bring themselves to

work while building their careers.

Second is centering equity for Minnesotans.

The aim is for public funds to reach

the adults and children who need them most,

while health disparities are reduced or eliminated.

Third is a continuous learning cycle.

Staff would feel safe receiving feedback, and

leaders at every level would hold themselves and

others accountable. Learning would lead to a

response, then further learning.

Fourth is centering community: culturally

responsible engagement, language access, and

community participation built into program

design. The aspiration is for that relationship

to shape the work as it develops.

Consider an illustrative example: a team is

revising a service notice. It could ask who

may have difficulty understanding it, listen to

affected people, and use that learning to improve the notice.

The map identifies support through employee

resource groups, community engagement and

feedback loops, equity forums, retreats, and

Tribal consultations, each serving its own purpose and relationships.

It also includes using data to close workforce

gaps, embedding equity in hiring and

contracting, and building learning through

training and Equity Week.

Practical resources include the Equity Analysis

Toolkit, an integrity tip line, and shared

equity language. The useful next step is to

connect the right resource to a real decision or concern.

The map identifies measures for each outcome,

helping examine progress and where people still experience barriers.

Measures include retention and career

advancement rates, completion and scores for the

Intercultural Development Inventory, use of the

Equity Analysis Toolkit, and decisions

co-created with community. Each offers part of the picture.

Wherever possible, the measures are examined by

race and ethnicity, gender, division, and staff

or manager. Looking beneath an overall result

can reveal differences that would otherwise be easy to miss.

The map brings us back to the everyday

decision, and the person whose experience it may change.

Start with a piece of work in front

of you. Name the outcome it is meant to support.

Consider whose perspective is needed, and how

those people can help shape the decision.

Use the available evidence, listen for what it

misses, and make room to learn.

Then return to the outcome.

Examine what changed, what did not, and what deserves another look.

For directors, managers, supervisors, and staff,

this map is the shared foundation for

equity-related learning and practice in One DHS

and One DSD People, Access and Culture.

The work continues through decisions,

relationships, and follow-through. We are stronger together.
