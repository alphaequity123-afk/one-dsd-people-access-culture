Equity Analysis Toolkit: worked example

Imagine opening a notice about support at home and still not knowing what to do next. A team wants clearer information about Community First Services and Supports, or C F S S. Let's turn that intention into a useful working draft. We are using the practice workspace accompanying the DHS Equity Analysis Toolkit training. Its five fields help organize thinking. They are not the official toolkit sequence, and filling them in does not complete a required review. In The decision and its limits, writing make the notice equitable gives us an intention, but no decision to examine. Instead, describe the choices that are actually open. Our draft says: revise the explanation, format, and route to help, so readers can identify their next step. Confirm current guidance and who can authorize these changes. Eligibility rules are outside this notice revision. That separates communication choices from decisions the team cannot make. In People, evidence, and unanswered questions, everyone benefits is too broad. It conceals differences in who can read, understand, and act. An English-only document or an online-only response can transfer extra work to people already facing barriers. Our draft asks about language and disability access, shared devices, and getting assistance. Review available questions and usability evidence; seek missing perspectives directly. We have not established what people need. Do not infer a person's preferences from their race, language, or disability. In Options and tradeoffs, translate it is a possible action, not a comparison. Consider keeping the existing notice, revising a paper explanation, or offering accessible formats and a supported route to help. Paper can reduce dependence on internet access, but paper alone will not meet every language or disability access need. More formats may improve access, but require review, staff time, and a way to keep each version current. Record who could benefit, who could carry additional burdens, and what remains uncertain. Use the plain-language and language-access resources to identify adjustments, then test them with affected people rather than assuming the proposed solution works. In Participation and next actions, ask the community leaves power and responsibility unclear. Invite people who use services while the wording, formats, and help route can still change. Offer ways to participate that fit people's language, access, and scheduling needs. Our draft assigns a communications lead to coordinate review and report back on what changed, what did not, and why. Confirm decision authority, participation supports, and resources. One meeting or one person cannot establish agreement for every community. In Review responsibilities and reconsideration, count notices sent measures distribution. It does not tell us whether people understood or could act. Ask readers to describe their next step and examine unanswered questions, assistance requests, and people not reached. Name who will review that information, agree a review date, and identify a reason to reconsider. For example, repeated confusion about where to get help would prompt another revision. Assign responsibility and resources for maintaining every format as guidance changes. Together, these notes can inform an equity impact statement: the proposed changes may reduce communication barriers, but evidence and engagement are still needed. The training also addresses alignment and sustainability. Check that recommendations fit current guidance and can be supported over time. Now try a different decision in the existing workspace. A service team is considering replacing mailed appointment reminders with texts. Work through its example, then use the five draft fields to prepare your own analysis. Describe what can change, whose experience is missing, two workable options, how people can influence the decision, and when to revisit it. Explain one tradeoff rather than copying our notice example. Keep practice notes separate from actual work and exclude identifying or confidential details. A useful analysis makes the decision, evidence, participation, and follow-through clear enough for others to question and improve.
