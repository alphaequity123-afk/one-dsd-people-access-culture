HCBS equity policy analysis walkthrough

These guides support independent use by DHS staff, with optional help from the Equity and Inclusion Operations Consultant. The consultant leads One DSD People, Access and Culture. Tool drafts are saved in the current browser on this device and can be downloaded or imported; they are not sent to the program server.

Step-by-step walkthrough in plain language, with a worked example

Disability Services Division · One DSD People, Access and Culture

Before you start

Open the file DSD_HCBS_Equity_Policy_Analysis_Tool.html in any browser. It works without internet and does not send anything anywhere. The screen has three parts. On the left are the eleven steps; a green circle means the step is complete, a gold circle means it is partly done. In the middle is the worksheet for the step you are on. On the right are three tabs: Coach (what the policy says, questions to ask, strong and weak answers, pitfalls), Walkthrough (the same instructions as this document, with the example for the step you are on), and Resources (the policy, the data to ask for, the legal authorities and links).

Four buttons at the top: New analysis, Open draft, Save draft and Load example. A draft is automatically kept in this browser on this device; Save draft, which downloads a small .json file; Open draft brings it back. Save after every step. Load example fills every field with the scenario below so you can see what a complete analysis looks like, then click New analysis to start your own.

Keep case, medical, personnel, complaint and identifying details out of every field. Describe roles and communities, not individuals.

The scenario

Follow one practical example through the steps. DSD proposes a waiver amendment that adds a new remote support service (sensors, two-way video, on-call staff) to the DD and CADI waivers, and lets lead agencies reduce in-person staffing authorizations where remote support is approved. The effective date is targeted for July 1, 2027 and CMS submission for March 1, 2027. The policy lead is a DSD program specialist. Names, places and figures are provided for practice; they do not describe an actual proposal or finding.

Step 0. Set up and triage

Service line, instrument, decision-maker, review track.

How to do this step

1. Type what you are analyzing in one plain sentence. If a family member could not understand it, rewrite it.

2. Choose the instrument. If more than one applies (a waiver amendment that also needs a bulletin), choose the one that goes for approval first; the others are follow-up actions.

3. Tick every service line the change touches, including the ones it touches sideways. If people or providers will move between programs, those programs count.

4. Fill in the unit, the person leading the analysis, the team (by role), the person who will sign, and the dates: the state decision date and the federal or system date.

5. Answer the triage questions. The tool identifies the policy criterion for prolonged or long-term actions scheduled for leadership approval. Confirm the appropriate review with the official User Guide and responsible equity office; the tool does not approve a scan.

6. Save a draft now and after each step. Browser autosave keeps a draft on this device. Download a copy for separate storage.

The example at this step

What is being analyzed. Add a remote support service to the DD and CADI waivers and allow reduced in-person staffing where remote support is approved

Type of action. Waiver amendment or renewal (CADI, DD, BI, CAC; 1915(c))

Service lines touched. DD waiver; CADI waiver; Waiver Reimagine (individual budgets, two-waiver model); 245D licensing and standards; DSP rates and DWRS; Provider enrollment (MPSE) and capacity; Lead agency operations (county and Tribal); Case management

DSD unit. DSD Waiver Policy and Operations

Analyst. Program specialist, waiver policy (analysis lead)

Analysis team. Waiver policy specialist; residential services policy lead; data analyst; a county case management supervisor; a Tribal lead agency representative; two self-advocates from the DD and CADI advisory councils; a 245D provider from Greater Minnesota; Equity and Inclusion Operations Consultant (facilitator)

Decision-maker. DSD Director, with ADSA Assistant Commissioner sign-off

Decision date. 2027-02-05

Prior formal analysis. No

Watch for

If the review track says 'formal analysis', tell the decision-maker today so the February date can hold.

'We already did community engagement last year' is not a prior formal analysis.

Step 1. Describe the action and outcomes

Problem, proposal, federal constraint, state choice.

How to do this step

1. Write the problem for people, not for the agency. 'CMS encourages technology' is not a problem. 'People wait months for overnight staff' is.

2. Describe what actually changes: the service, the rule, the money, the step.

3. Cite the federal floor. Look it up; do not guess. Then list what Minnesota chose above that floor. Those choices are what you will compare alternatives against later.

4. Write outcomes as what would be true for people a year later, with a number you can count and a story you would expect to hear.

The example at this step

Problem, and for whom. People on the DD and CADI waivers who want to live with less staff presence, and people who cannot find overnight or awake staff at all, wait months or accept more supervision than they want. Provider staffing shortages are worst in Greater Minnesota. Some lead agencies have no option to authorize technology-based support, so the choice depends on where a person lives.

What is proposed. Add 'remote support' as a covered service on the DD and CADI waivers: sensors, two-way audio and video, and an on-call responder within a set response time, delivered by a 245D-licensed provider. Allow the lead agency to reduce in-person staffing hours in the support plan where the person chooses remote support and the assessment supports it. New provider standard, new rate line, new CBSM page, new WMS service code.

Federal floor. 42 CFR 441.301(c)(4) (HCBS settings: privacy, choice, community integration) and 441.301(c)(1)–(3) (person-centered planning, including informed choice and the person's consent to any monitoring). The 1915(c) technical guide requires a defined service, provider qualifications and a quality strategy. Nothing in federal rule requires remote support to be offered, requires staffing reductions, or sets the response time.

State design choices. Whether to add the service at all; the response time standard; whether staffing can be reduced and by how much; the consent and revocation process; who can be the responder; whether broadband and equipment are covered; the rate; whether remote support is available on BI and CAC; the transition rules for people already in staffed settings; what guidance lead agencies get.

Intended outcomes. By July 2028, at least 400 people statewide use remote support by choice, with uptake in Greater Minnesota and among American Indian, Black and Hmong participants at or above the statewide rate. No person's in-person hours are reduced without documented informed choice in their language and format. Response-time performance is the same in rural and metro counties. No increase in appeals or in reported harm events among people using the service.

How we will know. Count: uptake by region, race, language and lead agency; staffing-reduction authorizations with and without documented choice; response times; appeals; incident reports. Story: a person in a rural county says they chose remote support, understood it, and can turn it off; a case manager in a small county says they knew how to authorize it.

Watch for

If the federal floor paragraph is empty, Step 8 will have nothing to work with.

Outcomes written as 'implement by July 1' are tasks, not outcomes.

Step 2. Who is affected

Disability populations, communities, lead agencies, providers, workforce.

How to do this step

1. Go down the grid row by row. For each group ask: does this help them, hurt them, both, or do we not know? Pick one. 'Unknown' is honest and becomes work in Step 3.

2. Write how, in a sentence. 'Burden' with no 'how' is a guess.

3. Add rows for the intersections that matter here. Remove rows that truly do not apply.

4. Mark whether each group has been heard yet. 'No' is a plan for Step 4, not a problem.

Step 3. Data and evidence

What MMIS, MnCHOICES, licensing and people's accounts show.

How to do this step

1. List what data you used. Ask the DSD data team and the ADSA Equity Director early; extracts take weeks.

2. Say how you cut the data: by race, language, disability, county or Tribal lead agency, region, provider type. Say what you could not cut.

3. Write what the numbers show in plain sentences with the figures in them. Then add what people have told you that the numbers cannot show.

4. Turn every unknown from Step 2 into a line here: what is needed, who will get it, by when.

The example at this step

Sources. MMIS authorizations and claims for DD and CADI residential and in-home staffing services, FY2024–FY2026; WMS enrollment by lead agency; MnCHOICES assessment bands; MPSE provider enrollment by county and size; DWRS cost report staffing and vacancy data; DEED broadband map; DD and CADI advisory council notes (2026); Ombudsman reports on monitoring complaints; NCI-IDD Minnesota privacy and choice items; Olmstead transition list; two listening sessions (one rural, one Deaf community) held September 2026.

Disaggregation. Authorizations by race, ethnicity, primary language, age, waiver, lead agency and region. Could not disaggregate by Tribal affiliation reliably (field incomplete in MMIS) or by Deaf/hard-of-hearing status (not captured). Provider data by county and size; cultural specificity by self-report only.

Findings. About 3,100 people on DD and CADI have overnight staffing in their plan; 41% of those in Greater Minnesota had at least one unfilled shift in the last quarter versus 17% in the metro. People whose assessments used an interpreter have in-person staffing authorizations 18% lower than others in the same band. Black and American Indian participants are 1.6 times more likely to be in a 24-hour staffed setting at the same band. Zero enrolled providers offer a remote responder with ASL capacity. Nine of the 11 reservations have broadband coverage below 60% of households. Listening sessions: rural families want the option; Deaf participants fear being pushed into it; self-advocates asked who can see the video and who can turn it off.

Gaps and follow-up. Authorization levels by race crossed with staffing reductions: data team extract by 2026-11-15 (owner: data analyst). Tribal affiliation: ask Tribal lead agencies for counts under data agreement by 2026-11-30 (owner: analysis lead with Tribal Liaison). Deaf/HoH prevalence on waivers: estimate from Deaf services and MnCHOICES communication items by 2026-11-30 (owner: data analyst). BI and CAC effect: decision whether to include in phase two by 2026-12-15 (owner: waiver policy lead). CDCS purchase question: policy decision by 2026-12-15 (owner: self-direction lead).

Watch for

'Zero providers' is a finding, not an absence of data.

Statewide averages hid the interpreter gap until the data was cut by language.

Step 4. Engagement

People who use services, families, Tribal Nations, lead agencies, providers.

How to do this step

1. Describe who you engaged by community and role. Never names.

2. Say how and when. 'Before the options were fixed' and 'after the draft was written' are different answers and the record should show which.

3. Decide the Tribal consultation status and route it through the Tribal Liaison. It is not the same as a county call.

4. List internal partners. Then write what changed because of what you heard. If nothing changed, say why and how you told people.

The example at this step

Who was engaged. Self-advocates from the DD and CADI advisory councils; Deaf and DeafBlind participants through a Deaf services partner; rural families through two Greater Minnesota listening sessions; 245D providers of three sizes; DSPs through a provider workforce group; county case managers through MACSSA; Tribal lead agency staff from two nations (informal, pre-consultation); Olmstead Implementation Office.

How. Two listening sessions (one with ASL and CART), one advisory council session on options A/B/C, one provider forum, one case manager forum, an online survey in English, Spanish, Hmong and Somali with plain-language and screen-reader-tested versions.

When. Listening sessions and the options session happened in September and October 2026, before the amendment language was drafted. Provider and case manager forums will repeat after the draft in January 2027.

Tribal consultation. Required and planned; date set

Internal partners. ADSA Equity Director; DSD Equity Team; DHS Tribal Liaison; DHS Licensing (245D); MnCHOICES team; accessibility coordinator and language access; Deaf and Hard of Hearing Services Division; Olmstead Implementation Office; federal relations; MNIT (WMS code).

What changed. Three changes so far. Consent must be given by the person in their language and format, with a right to turn the system off at any time without losing in-person hours for 90 days (from self-advocates and Deaf participants). Staffing reductions cannot be initiated by the lead agency; only the person or their chosen representative can request them (from advisory councils and families). Rural response time standard will be set by region with a provider-reported measure (from Greater Minnesota families). Not changed yet: BI and CAC inclusion, pending data; CDCS purchase, pending policy decision. Both will be reported back with the reason.

How engagement continues. You-said-we-did summary in plain language, ASL video and four languages by December 15, 2026, posted and sent to every participant and lead agency. Advisory councils review the draft amendment in January. Quarterly check-ins with Deaf services and Tribal lead agencies through the first year. Public and Tribal notice period is in addition to this, not instead of it.

Watch for

Formal Tribal consultation has a date in Step 8 routing; the informal conversations here do not satisfy it.

The CMS public notice period is a federal minimum; it is not engagement.

Step 5. Impacts and root causes

Benefits, burdens, mechanism, disparity effect.

How to do this step

1. List the benefits and who gets them. Then the burdens, how severe, and roughly how many people.

2. For each burden, name the mechanism: the specific rule, formula, step, standard or technology that causes it. Then say whether that mechanism is federal floor or state choice.

3. Say how this interacts with the rules around it.

4. Imagine it going wrong. Who would it go wrong for first, and what would you see in month three?

5. Judge the net effect on the gaps you already know about: reduce, hold, widen, or cannot tell.

The example at this step

Benefits. People who want less staff presence gain privacy and independence (likely 300–500 in year one). People in Greater Minnesota with unfilled shifts gain a reliable overnight option. People on the Olmstead transition list whose barrier is staffing may move sooner. DSPs gain on-call roles that may stabilize vacancies. Lead agencies gain an option they currently lack.

Burdens. Deaf, DeafBlind, blind, LEP and some IDD participants may be unable to use the service as designed yet could still face staffing reductions (estimated 400–700 people at risk). Communities with historically lower authorizations risk being steered toward the cheaper option. Small and culturally specific providers face capital and 24/7 staffing costs they cannot meet (an estimated 60 providers serving 1,100 people). Tribal lead agencies receive new work with no authority language or funding. Self-directed participants are excluded from the option.

Root causes. Mechanism 1: the draft allows lead agencies to initiate staffing reductions (state choice; now changed to person-initiated). Mechanism 2: provider standard requires 24/7 in-house responders and owned equipment (state choice; could allow shared responder networks and leased equipment). Mechanism 3: consent and instructions in English PDF only (state choice; accessibility and CLAS obligations apply). Mechanism 4: response-time standard set statewide (state choice; could be regional with a measured floor). Mechanism 5: no Tribal lead agency language (state choice). Mechanism 6: service limited to DD and CADI provider-delivered (state choice). Federal floor contributes only the requirement that monitoring be consented to in the person-centered plan.

Interaction with adjacent rules. HCBS settings rule: cameras in private space require consent and privacy protections. 245D: new service standard and incident reporting. DWRS: new rate line and interaction with existing residential rates when hours drop. Waiver Reimagine budgets: remote support must be costed inside the individual budget bands or people with low bands cannot afford it. EVV: not applicable to this service but responder visits may be. Case management limits: new consent and review work with no added case management time.

Unintended effects. Month three risks: a provider markets remote support to lead agencies as a cost saving and reductions rise fastest in counties with the tightest budgets; a Deaf participant's sensor alert goes to an audio-only responder; a guardian consents and the person did not; a rural response time of 90 minutes is recorded as 'met' because the standard was never set; two culturally specific providers close their residential programs.

Net effect on disparities. Risks widening a disparity

Watch for

'Widen' is the honest answer for the draft as written; Step 8 exists to change it.

Count the people behind each burden; 'some providers' becomes 'about 60 providers serving 1,100 people' when you look.

Step 6. Lead agencies, providers and workforce

County and Tribal variation, provider capacity, DSPs.

How to do this step

1. Describe how the smallest county and a Tribal lead agency would actually do this with the staff and systems they have.

2. Find every place the instrument leaves judgment to a lead agency. For each, say what guidance and examples will keep readings consistent.

3. Describe provider capacity by region and type: who can deliver this, who cannot, who might leave, and who loses access if they do.

4. Describe what happens to DSPs and self-directed workers: pay, hours, training, turnover. Use numbers where you have them.

5. Say what support, time and money implementers get, and whether it is the same for all of them.

The example at this step

Lead agency variation. A county with two case managers covering all waiver participants would receive a new service to explain, a new consent process to document and a new review at each annual meeting, with no added case management hours. Tribal lead agencies in the two nations consulted informally operate under separate agreements that do not mention this service; two have fewer than 50 waiver participants and one case manager. Distance to the nearest provider with responder capacity exceeds 60 miles for 14 counties and 7 reservations.

Discretion points and guidance. Discretion points in the draft: 'where the assessment supports' reduced staffing; 'reasonable response time'; what counts as 'informed choice'; whether a guardian's consent is sufficient; whether equipment is covered. Guidance planned: a CBSM page with three worked examples (person requests reduction; guardian requests and person objects; sensor-only with no video); a consent checklist in plain language and ASL; a regional response-time table; a decision that equipment is covered under the service rate. Training for case managers before the effective date, with a Tribal lead agency session delivered separately.

Provider capacity. Metro: four large providers ready to deliver in year one. Greater Minnesota: one provider in the northwest, none in the southwest and none on reservations. Culturally specific providers: none have responder capacity; 60 serve about 1,100 people in residential settings and report that technology costs and 24/7 staffing are out of reach. Exit risk: if residential hours fall by 20% without a shared-responder option, up to 12 small providers report they would close a program, affecting roughly 150 people who would then have less choice, not more.

Workforce effects. About 2,400 DSPs work overnight shifts on DD and CADI. A 15% shift to on-call would reduce scheduled hours for an estimated 350 DSPs, concentrated in metro large providers, while rural vacancies (currently 41% unfilled shifts) would fall. The responder role has no DWRS wage line; providers propose paying it below the DSP median, which would make the role hard to fill and would lower overall DSP pay. Self-directed workers are unaffected in the draft because CDCS cannot buy the service.

Support for implementers. Draft provides none. Planned mitigations: a two-year start-up grant program weighted to Greater Minnesota and culturally specific providers; a shared responder network contract so small providers can offer the service without 24/7 in-house staff; case manager training with a Tribal-specific session; added case management time coded for consent and review in year one; Tribal lead agency agreement amendment with funding; a provider technical assistance line.

Watch for

A county with two case managers is the design case, not the exception.

If the mitigation depends on a lead agency's own budget, it is not a mitigation.

Step 7. Access, communication and settings

Plain language, Deaf and blind access, LEP, digital, HCBS settings.

How to do this step

1. Walk the path of each person named in the coach panel through the notice, the consent, the device, the responder call and the appeal. Write where it breaks.

2. For disability access, language, non-digital routes and settings, write the standard, the provision, the budget and the owner. If one of those four is missing, the item is not done.

3. Check the settings rule: does the change touch choice of setting, privacy, autonomy or the person-centered plan? Say how.

The example at this step

Barriers. Consent and instructions are English PDFs; responder line is audio-first and English-only; app interfaces are not screen-reader tested; sensor alerts are audible only; equipment setup assumes a smartphone and home broadband; the appeal notice does not explain how to keep in-person hours while appealing.

Disability access. ADA Title II, Section 504 and 508, WCAG 2.1 AA. Provisions: plain-language consent tested with six self-advocates (owner: waiver policy; budget: existing plain-language contract; by 2027-01-31); ASL and tactile interpreting for consent and reviews through the Deaf services contract (owner: DHHSD liaison; budget line added to amendment fiscal note); visual and tactile alert devices required in the provider standard (owner: 245D standard author); screen-reader-tested app as a provider qualification with a 12-month compliance window (owner: accessibility coordinator); braille and large-print consent on request within five days.

Language and culture. Consent, instructions and the you-said-we-did summary translated into Spanish, Hmong, Somali, Oromo and Vietnamese (the languages of 92% of LEP participants on DD and CADI), dialect-checked by community members, published with the English version (owner: language access; budget: $38,000 in fiscal note). Responder line must provide interpreter access within four minutes through the state interpreter contract; rural availability confirmed with the vendor by 2027-01-15 (owner: provider standard author). CLAS Standards 5 through 8 applied; cultural review of in-home camera concerns with Tribal and Somali community members.

Non-digital routes. Non-digital route: sensor-only and phone-line options that do not require a smartphone or broadband, covered at the same rate; equipment and connectivity included in the service so no participant pays; in-person consent meetings available in every lead agency; mail and phone appeal routes stated on every notice. Equal timeliness: same five-day setup standard for all routes (owner: waiver policy).

Settings and person-centered planning. Cameras and sensors are monitoring in a private residence under 42 CFR 441.301(c)(4); consent must be recorded in the person-centered plan with the specific devices, locations, who can view, retention period and the right to revoke. Choice of setting is protected by the rule that reductions are person-initiated and reversible for 90 days. Community integration is expected to improve for people in rural areas who can move to less staffed settings.

Other provisions. Hours: consent meetings available evenings and weekends on request. Location: home or lead agency office. Trusted messengers: self-advocacy organizations, Deaf services partner and Tribal health programs will present the option in their own sessions, funded through engagement contract.

Watch for

Four parts to every provision: standard, provision, budget, owner.

'Sensor-only' is the non-digital route for most people; it has to cost the same.

Step 8. Alternatives, mitigation and routing

Options above the federal floor; formal paths.

How to do this step

1. Write at least two alternatives above the federal floor, including 'do less' and a phased or targeted option. Compare them on the burdens from Steps 5, 6 and 7, cost, feasibility and whether CMS would approve.

2. List the mitigations for burdens that remain under your preferred option. Each one: specific, funded, owned, dated.

3. Tick every formal path the action triggers, then write the status and dates. Contact them before the decision date, not after.

The example at this step

Alternatives. A. Amendment as drafted: lead-agency-initiated reductions, in-house responder standard, statewide response time, English materials. Cheapest to administer; widens disparities; CMS approvable; highest harm risk. B. Amendment with person-initiated reductions, shared responder network, regional response standard, full access provisions, Tribal lead agency language, start-up grants (preferred): adds about $4.1 million over two years; removes mechanisms 1 through 5; CMS approvable; some lead agencies will say it is complex. C. Phase one as a pilot in eight lead agencies (four rural, two Tribal, two metro) with no staffing reductions permitted, then statewide in 2028 after review: lowest risk; delays benefit for rural families by a year; CMS approvable as a limited-geography service. D. Do less: add remote support only as an add-on with no staffing reductions ever: removes the steering risk entirely; loses the budget case that funds the start-up grants. Recommended: B, with C's eight-agency structure used as a six-month early-adopter wave before statewide launch.

Mitigations. Start-up grants weighted to Greater Minnesota and culturally specific providers, $2.4 million, owner: DSD grants manager, RFP by 2027-05-01. Shared responder network contract, $900,000 per year, owner: ADSA procurement, award by 2027-06-15. Responder wage line in DWRS at the DSP median, owner: rates unit, in rate table by 2027-04-01. Case management time for consent and annual review in year one, owner: case management policy, bulletin by 2027-06-01. Tribal lead agency agreement amendment with funding, owner: Tribal Liaison and waiver policy, by 2027-05-15. BI and CAC inclusion decision for phase two, owner: waiver policy lead, by 2027-09-30. CDCS purchase option, owner: self-direction lead, policy decision by 2027-09-30. Disparity watch: quarterly report of staffing reductions by race, language and lead agency with a stop rule if any group's reduction rate exceeds 1.3 times the statewide rate, owner: data analyst.

Formal paths. ADSA Equity Director (advice; approved analysis per policy); DSD Equity Team / ADSA Equity Committee; Tribal consultation (EO 19-24; DHS Tribal Liaison; Tribal lead agencies); Federal relations / Medicaid policy (CMS waiver or SPA approval, public notice); Olmstead Implementation Office (plan goals and measures); Lead agency communication (county and Tribal; MACSSA, AMC, Tribal partners); DHS Licensing (245D) and provider standards; MnCHOICES / assessment and support planning team; Equal Opportunity and Access Division (civil rights, Title VI, ADA, LEP); Accessibility coordinator and language access (508, WCAG, translation, interpreters); Labor relations / HR (DSP and self-directed worker effects; bargaining); Data privacy / records (MGDPA, HIPAA); MNIT / systems (MMIS, MnCHOICES, EVV, portals); ADSA procurement and contracts (grants, RFPs, FMS, vendors); Ombudsman for Mental Health and Developmental Disabilities; advisory councils

Routing status. Equity Director: briefed 2026-10-20, reviewing this record by 2026-12-01. DSD Equity Team: session 2026-11-12. Tribal consultation: formal consultation requested through Tribal Liaison for January 2027, before public notice; Tribal notice will follow federal rule in February. Federal relations: pre-submission call with CMS scheduled 2026-12-08. Olmstead: alignment memo 2026-11-30. Lead agency communication: MACSSA policy committee 2026-12-10; Tribal lead agencies separately in January. Licensing: 245D standard drafting with licensing by 2027-01-15. MnCHOICES: consent fields in support plan by 2027-03-01. EOAD: LEP and ADA review of consent process by 2027-01-31. Accessibility: app qualification standard by 2027-01-31. Labor relations: DSP hour effects reviewed with HR by 2026-12-15. Data privacy: video retention and viewing rules through data practices review by 2027-01-15. MNIT: WMS service code by 2027-02-01. Procurement: responder network RFP scoping started. Ombudsman: briefed 2026-11-05; monitoring complaint data agreement in place.

Watch for

Option A is still on the list so the decision-maker can see what was rejected and why.

Every mitigation has a dollar figure or an owner; most have both.

Step 9. Implementation and accountability

Rollout, measures by lead agency and community, review, sustainability.

How to do this step

1. List the first implementation steps with owners, especially what reaches lead agencies and providers before the effective date.

2. Date the milestones. Set the first review date and how often after that.

3. Name the money and the people. Say what equity learning implementers have or need.

4. Write the measures, disaggregated the same way as Step 3, tied to the waiver quality strategy and the Outcome Map metrics.

5. Say how results get back to leadership, the Equity Director, the Equity Team and the people you engaged.

6. Say what is written into the waiver, the manual, contract language or the performance plan so this survives a change of sponsor.

The example at this step

Implementation steps. 1. Finalize amendment language with person-initiated reductions, access provisions and Tribal lead agency section (waiver policy lead, 2027-01-31). 2. Step 10. Recommendation and record

Verdict, owned recommendations, alignment, output.

How to do this step

1. Choose the verdict. Proceed, proceed with changes, redesign, or do not proceed. If the team disagrees, say so in the summary and let the decision-maker decide.

2. Write one paragraph a commissioner could read in a minute: what, who, so what, recommended action.

3. Write three to six recommendations. Each has an owner, a date and a measure. Those are the action items the record carries.

4. Tick the policy objectives and ADSA goals this advances.

5. Say how and to whom the results go. Then run the quality check; fix anything marked with a dash before you print.

6. Print or save as PDF for the approval package; copy as text for the amendment narrative; save the draft (.json) so the next review starts from here.

The example at this step

Recommendation. Proceed with the changes and mitigations

Summary. Adding remote support to the DD and CADI waivers can give several hundred people more privacy and independence and relieve staffing shortages that are worst in Greater Minnesota. As drafted, it would widen existing disparities: lead agencies could initiate staffing reductions, and the people least able to use the service as designed (Deaf, DeafBlind, blind, LEP and some IDD participants) and the communities with historically lower authorizations would be most exposed; small and culturally specific providers and Tribal lead agencies would bear costs with no support. The analysis team recommends proceeding with the amendment in the revised form: reductions only at the person's request and reversible for 90 days, a shared responder network and start-up grants, regional response standards, full language and disability access provisions, Tribal lead agency authority and funding, and a quarterly disparity stop rule. With those changes the net effect on disparities is expected to be neutral in year one and positive thereafter, and the amendment remains approvable by CMS on the current timeline.

Two shorter scenarios

Shorter scenario: a 245D documentation change

DHS Licensing proposes that 245D providers record a daily progress note for every person served, within 24 hours, in the provider's electronic record. Instrument: licensing standard. Service lines: 245D, DSP rates, provider enrollment. Federal floor: none; this is a state choice. In Step 2, small and culturally specific providers and DSPs are marked burden, high; people served are marked unknown, because better notes could improve care or could take staff time away from them. Step 3 finds that 30% of small providers have no electronic record and that DSP documentation time already averages 40 minutes per shift. Step 6 shows the smallest providers would need software and training with no funding, and that 24 hours is unworkable for a weekend on-call structure. Step 8 compares: as drafted; a 72-hour window with paper allowed and a grant for software; a note only when something changes. Verdict: redesign. The protection (knowing what happened to a person) is kept; the paperwork that only large providers can absorb is removed.

Shorter scenario: a bill analysis

HF 1842 would set a statewide wage floor for DSPs funded through a DWRS adjustment. Instrument: bill analysis. In Step 0 the analyst records the file number, author and committee stage; the review track is formal analysis. Step 1 separates the federal floor (none) from state choices (the floor amount, whether it covers self-directed workers, the phase-in). Step 2 marks DSPs benefit, high; self-directed participants and workers unknown (the bill is silent); small providers both (higher wages, higher costs); people served benefit if vacancies fall. Step 3 uses DWRS cost reports to show rural wages are 11% below metro and that culturally specific providers pay closest to the floor already. Step 6 notes that rate adjustments reach providers months after the wage obligation. Step 8 recommends an amendment covering self-directed workers and a provider cash-flow bridge. Step 10's bill button copies an equity section: position 'support with amendments', communities benefited and burdened, evidence, Tribal consultation status, recommended amendments and fiscal notes for equity provisions, ready to paste into the standard bill analysis.

After the record is produced

Print or save as PDF for the approval package. Copy as text to paste the record into the amendment narrative or a memo. For bills, use the button that copies the equity section for the bill analysis. Save the draft so the next review, the quarterly report and the year-one review start from this record rather than from a blank page. Send the record to the ADSA Equity Director and the DSD Equity Team, and send the you-said-we-did summary to everyone who was engaged.
