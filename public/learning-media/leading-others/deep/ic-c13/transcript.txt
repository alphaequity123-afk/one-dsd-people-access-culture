Designing services that adapt



Picture a notice. It tells a person receiving home and community-based services that a reassessment is coming. It gives a date. Somewhere in the third paragraph, it says the person can ask for a different date. Many people never find that sentence. Some miss the visit. Some lose time with their services while it gets sorted out.

It is easy to think the fix is better writing. Shorter sentences. Friendlier words. A good readability score. Those help. But a readability score measures sentence length. It does not tell you whether a reader can find an option and act on it in time.

Consider what happened when the IRS tested its own notices. Researchers mailed different versions to more than thirty-five thousand people who seemed eligible for a tax credit but had not claimed it. A simpler notice raised the share who claimed from fourteen percent to twenty-three percent. Adding the possible benefit amount raised it to thirty-one percent. Messages meant to make people feel less stigma did nothing at all. Clarity about the action mattered. Reassurance did not.

Now add language. Plain English is not language access. DHS policy says county, Tribal and state servicing agencies must provide appropriate language assistance. No one should be denied access to a DHS program because they do not speak English fluently. The National CLAS Standards call for free language assistance as well.

Who interprets matters too. In one study of audiotaped emergency visits, untrained interpreters, such as family members or staff who happened to speak the language, made consequential errors as often as having no interpreter at all. Trained professional interpreters made far fewer. Those with at least one hundred hours of training made the fewest. A helpful bilingual coworker is generous. That coworker is not a language access plan.

So how do you design a notice that adapts? Start with the decision the reader faces. Ask a few readers to show you what they would do next. Include people who missed the step before. Include people who read the translated versions, and people who use accessible formats like screen readers. Watch where they stop.

Some content will be required. Do not cut it to make a test look better. Ask the person or area responsible what must stay. Then redesign around it. A clear heading. A short summary. A box that says, here is what to do next.

Be honest about one limit. Co-design, involving people in designing the services they use, has rarely been tested for outcomes. One review found it can make materials more relevant. It also found that some participants felt their input was ignored. So the last step matters most. Tell the people who helped what you changed, what you could not change, and why. That is how a service learns to adapt, one tested notice at a time.
