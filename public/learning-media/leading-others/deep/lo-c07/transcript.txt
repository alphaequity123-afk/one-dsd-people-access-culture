Why people stay



Human Services Supervisor: I lost two experienced specialists this year. Both said pay in their exit conversations. I am thinking the retention plan should be a request for higher classifications.

Program Manager: Pay is real, and it does predict leaving. But in the biggest review of turnover research, covering 316 articles, pay was one of the weaker predictors. Job satisfaction, commitment, leadership and something called embeddedness each mattered more.

Human Services Supervisor: Embeddedness. What does that mean in practice?

Program Manager: It is the web that holds someone in the job. Links to colleagues and partners. Fit between the work and their skills. And what they would give up by leaving. One review of about 43,000 people found it predicted staying even after accounting for how satisfied people were.

Human Services Supervisor: So two people could be equally satisfied, and the one with more ties is more likely to stay.

Program Manager: That is the idea. And notice what happened in your unit. One of the people who left had a review partner who left earlier. Did that come up?

Human Services Supervisor: Not in the exit conversation. But now that you mention it, they did seem more isolated after that. And I have a strong specialist right now who has gone quiet. They train every new hire.

Program Manager: Then do not wait for an exit conversation. Exit answers come at a moment when people have little reason to say everything. Pay is easy to name. Talk with your specialist now, while there is time to act.

Human Services Supervisor: What do I ask? I do not want it to sound like I am trying to talk them out of something.

Program Manager: Make it a regular conversation you have with everyone. Ask what they look forward to, who they rely on, and what would make the next year harder. Then pick one thing you control and give a date.

Human Services Supervisor: And if they bring up pay?

Program Manager: Be honest about what you can and cannot change. Raise a real classification question with the people responsible. Then look at load, support and growth. In the child welfare research, stress, burnout and lack of support mattered more than who the worker was. Those are things a supervisor can shape. None of it is guaranteed. It shifts the odds.
