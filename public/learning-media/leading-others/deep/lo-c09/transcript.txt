Managing workload



Policy Analyst: Do you have ten minutes? I need help with the next two weeks. A new data request came in this morning for Friday, and I do not think everything fits.

Human Services Supervisor: Can you squeeze it in? It is for the director's meeting.

Policy Analyst: That was my first instinct too. So I checked how long similar work took last time instead of guessing. The last data summary took about 14 hours with reviews. The training presentation took 20. With the report section, the inbox and my two days of leave, it comes to about 70 hours of work. I have 52.

Human Services Supervisor: Seventy against fifty-two. I would have guessed it was tight, not that far over.

Policy Analyst: Most people underestimate. In one study, students thought their theses would take about 34 days. They took about 55. Looking at the last similar task is what fixes it.

Human Services Supervisor: All right. Is Friday the real deadline?

Policy Analyst: I asked. The meeting is next Thursday, so Friday was a buffer. That helps, but it still does not fit. I have two options. I can deliver the summary Wednesday if inbox response times slow from two days to four this week. Or the data team's manager could assign it to another analyst.

Human Services Supervisor: That is clear. I would rather not slow the inbox. Counties are waiting on answers about the policy change. Let me ask the data team's manager about another analyst.

Policy Analyst: Thank you. Could I hear by tomorrow? If it stays with me, I need to start Thursday.

Human Services Supervisor: Tomorrow morning. And I am noticing this is the third reporting cycle with a collision like this.

Policy Analyst: It is. Two managers approve work separately, so nobody sees the total until it lands on one person.

Human Services Supervisor: Then the fix is not you planning harder. I will ask both managers to agree on one order of priority, or one person who decides when requests collide. Bring this picture to that conversation. It makes the choice visible.
