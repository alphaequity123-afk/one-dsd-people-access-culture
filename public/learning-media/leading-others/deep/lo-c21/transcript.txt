Accessible documents and meetings



A person who uses a screen reader opens an invitation to a public session. The screen reader announces one thing: image. The date, the time, the link and the number to call for accommodations are all inside a picture of a flyer. The person cannot find any of them. A sighted colleague would see a clear, attractive invitation. The author meant well. The barrier was built in at the start, and it can be removed at the start too.

The rules are now specific. The U.S. Department of Justice rule under Title II of the Americans with Disabilities Act sets WCAG 2.1 Level AA as the standard for state and local government web content. That includes new PDFs, Word files and slides. For a state agency, the compliance date is April 26, 2027. Minnesota law already requires state agencies to follow accessibility standards built on WCAG and Section 508. And the ADA duty of effective communication applies today.

Most failures are common and fixable. A 2026 scan of a million home pages found detected failures on almost 96 percent. Low-contrast text was the most common problem, followed by missing descriptions for images, unlabeled form fields and empty links. Use an accessible template. Put key details in ordinary text. Use real heading styles. Name each link for where it goes. Run the checker, then move through the file using only the keyboard.

Plain language is part of access. In an IRS field experiment, people who had not claimed a tax credit received notices. Simpler, clearer notices led to substantial additional claims. Messages meant to reassure people did not. So put the action first. Say what to do, by when, and how to get help. Required legal language can follow, under short headings.

Be honest about limits. In one randomized trial, a plain version improved understanding of one health recommendation by almost 20 points, but barely changed understanding of another. Plain language helps most when the original is hard. Test drafts with a few readers and ask them to tell you, in their own words, what they must do.

Meetings need the same care. Send materials ahead. Arrange captions and interpreters. Offer more than one way to take part. A review of more than 100 studies found captions help many viewers understand and remember. Finally, name one person at the start who will pause the meeting if access fails. When the captions stop, that person speaks up, and no one is left behind.
