Working through conflict



Waiver Operations Lead: I raised a real problem with the new guidance, and the response was that operations always wants exceptions. That was not about the rule. That was about me.

Human Services Supervisor: You are right that the comment was about motives. I should have redirected it in the meeting. Can I separate the strands with you? What is the work question underneath this?

Waiver Operations Lead: Whether the rule applies to plans that were already approved. If it does, about 300 plans stall.

Human Services Supervisor: That is a fair question, and it is answerable. Research on teams separates three kinds of conflict. Disagreement about the work, friction between people, and disputes about who does what. The personal and process kinds are reliably linked to worse performance. Disagreement about the work is more mixed.

Waiver Operations Lead: Mixed how? I thought debate was supposed to be healthy.

Human Services Supervisor: A meta-analysis of 116 studies found no overall link between task conflict and performance. In teams below top management, it was actually negative. It was most harmful when it came bundled with personal friction, which is what happened in that meeting.

Waiver Operations Lead: So the problem is not that I disagreed. It is that the disagreement turned into a fight about who cares more about the rules.

Human Services Supervisor: Yes. One study of 70 leadership teams found that when trust is low, people read a challenge to their idea as a challenge to them. That probably happened on both sides. I suspect the policy specialist heard your concern as an attack on their work.

Waiver Operations Lead: I can see that. What happens now?

Human Services Supervisor: I will route the interpretation question to the person or area responsible for the rule. Then I will bring you and the policy specialist together with a narrow agenda: the question, the evidence, who decides, and by when. Comments about motives stay off the table.

Waiver Operations Lead: And the call-line representative? They raised the same concern and stopped coming to briefings.

Human Services Supervisor: They come back. Their callers will live with the result. The evidence on people being pushed out after disagreeing is thinner, but it points the same way. Losing the person who disagrees usually means losing information the team will need later.
