Diagnosing problems before fixing them



Picture a disability services call line on a Monday morning. Calls about a new authorization letter have doubled. Wait times are up. Two staff members have given callers different answers, and a county partner has noticed. Your manager asks a simple question. Do you need more people?

Most supervisors would say yes, and some would be right. But consider what research on decisions has found. Paul Nutt studied 356 real decisions in organizations across the United States and Canada. About a quarter were made in public agencies. Managers considered more than one option in fewer than one in five of those decisions. When they did, success rose from 56 percent to 70 percent. The most common way to fail was to settle on a solution early and stop looking.

There is a second trap, and it is less obvious. You might think the answer is careful problem-finding: what went wrong, and who did it. Nutt found that this approach worked no better than skipping diagnosis altogether. Problems invite blame. When people feel blamed, they defend themselves instead of helping you find the answer. So start somewhere else. Name the result you want. Callers should get a correct answer on the first call. Now the question is how to close a gap, and everyone can help.

Next, look at real cases. Pull ten recent calls. What did people actually ask? In this example, seven callers read the same paragraph of the letter aloud. Three calls were answered from an older script that no longer matches policy. That is two causes, not one, and both matter.

Notice something else. Your staff had probably known about that paragraph for weeks. In a study of hospital nurses, researchers watched them work around problems ninety-three percent of the time. The nurses kept patients cared for and moved on. They rarely told anyone who could fix the cause. That is not carelessness. It is commitment. But it keeps problems invisible. When a staff member tells you about a pattern, thank them, and act on it.

Be careful, too, about the person nearest the error. Retraining the two specialists who gave conflicting answers feels decisive. Studies of improvement efforts show that managers tend to blame workers rather than the system. A quick dip in errors can make that seem true. Meanwhile, the old script is still on the shared drive.

Finally, choose fixes that change the process. A clearer letter. A single current script. A review of health care investigations found that teams often settle for reminders and posters, and the same error returns. So bring your cases to the person or area responsible for the letter and the script. Keep an interim answer in place for today's callers. And set a date to check whether the calls actually drop. That last step is how you know the diagnosis was right.
