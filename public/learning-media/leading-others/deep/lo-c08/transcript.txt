Preventing burnout



Think of someone on your team who used to light up when they solved a hard case. Lately they arrive early, leave late and answer questions in a few clipped words. It is tempting to think they need to take better care of themselves. The research points somewhere else.

The World Health Organization describes burnout as the result of chronic workplace stress that has not been managed. It has three parts: exhaustion, mental distance from the job, and a sense of not making a difference. It is a work phenomenon, not a medical condition. That tells you where to look first. At the work.

Researchers Christina Maslach and Michael Leiter describe six areas where people and their work can be mismatched. Workload. Control. Reward. Community. Fairness. Values. When the mismatch grows in any of these, burnout becomes more likely. Meta-analyses tell a similar story. Job demands such as conflicting instructions or constant interruptions raise burnout. Job resources such as support and clear authority lower it.

So what helps? In a review of 18 controlled studies with physicians, changes to how work was organized, mostly reducing workload or changing schedules, reduced burnout more than programs aimed at individuals. And a survey of more than 46,000 workers in the United Kingdom found that people who joined resilience training, mindfulness sessions or wellbeing apps appeared no better off than those who did not. That survey cannot prove cause. But together these findings point one way. Change the work first. Then offer individual support as an addition.

In human services there is a second strain. Secondary traumatic stress comes from hearing about or helping with other people's trauma. It can look like intrusive thoughts, avoidance or feeling on edge. In one survey of 992 child welfare workers and supervisors, more than half reported such symptoms in the past week. It is related to burnout, but cutting paperwork will not touch it. What helps is attention to how trauma exposure is spread across the team, and support from colleagues and supervisors. The evidence on specific prevention programs is weak. So offer support, and let people choose how to use it.

One last check. When you relieve one person, ask who picked up the work. Strain that moves has not been solved. Two weeks after any change, look again at hours, breaks and who is carrying the hardest calls. Prevention is not one fix. It is a habit of looking at the work.
