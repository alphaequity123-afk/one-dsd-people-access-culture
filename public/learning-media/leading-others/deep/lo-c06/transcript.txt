Onboarding through the first year



Picture a new employee's first morning. They arrive knowing a lot about Minnesota human services. They do not yet know who approves an exception in your unit, whether they can handle a difficult call, or whether the team will welcome them. Those three questions are the core of onboarding. Researchers call them role clarity, self-efficacy and social acceptance. When Bauer and colleagues pooled 70 samples of new employees, these three explained most of how onboarding connects to later satisfaction, commitment, performance and staying.

So the test of any onboarding activity is simple. Does it help the person understand the role, feel able to do it, or feel accepted? If not, it may be ceremony.

Now think about timing. In one study, researchers checked in with 264 new hires 14 times across their first 90 days. Support from supervisors and coworkers dropped over that period. And the early weeks mattered most. Early support, or early undermining, predicted later outcomes better than any later change. Undermining is often small. It is a correction in front of others, a key email the new person was left off, or a sigh when they ask a basic question. Undermining by a supervisor was the one behavior uniquely linked to people leaving.

That means your effort should come early. Before the first day, make sure equipment and access will work. In a study of 985 new hires, a ready workstation on Day 1 was linked to better adjustment, and meeting the manager on Day 1 was linked to feeling accepted. Name a peer guide, and give that person real time by moving some of their work. Support from experienced colleagues is among the strongest tactics in the research.

Then ask about strengths. In a field experiment with 605 new call-center agents, a one-hour first-day session on each person's best qualities cut turnover. Six months later, the standard-training group's turnover was 47.2 percent higher. Ask what work the person is proud of, and find an early real task that uses it.

Finally, put check-ins on the calendar now: two weeks, 30 days, 90 days, six months and one year. At each, ask about the three questions. Good work at 90 days is encouraging, but it does not mean the person feels part of the team. Ask who they can go to without hesitation.

The evidence here is solid but modest. Most studies are correlational, and few come from public human services. Good onboarding improves the odds. It does not fix a poorly designed job. Watch what happens with each new hire, and improve the plan for the next one.
