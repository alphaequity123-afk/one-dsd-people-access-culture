Curiosity before conclusions



Picture a case note that says, "Family resistant. Refused two contact attempts." It reads like a fact. It is actually a story about character, built from two missed calls. Psychologists have studied this kind of story for decades. People lean toward explaining behavior by who someone is, even when the situation explains it well.

Here is why it happens to good, careful people. Judging another person runs in three quick steps. First, you notice what they did. Second, you infer a trait. Third, you correct for the situation. The first two steps are fast and nearly automatic. The third takes effort. In a classic set of experiments, people who were mentally busy skipped that third step. People who could focus made it. A full queue or a stack of renewals is exactly that kind of busy.

So what helps? Many trainings say to put yourself in the other person's shoes. Researchers tested that advice across 25 experiments. Imagining the other view did not reliably improve accuracy. Sometimes it lowered accuracy while raising confidence. What worked was asking. In the final study, people who asked their partner first got more than twice as many predictions exactly right as people who only imagined.

That points to a simple method, first taught at the University of Minnesota in the 1970s. Describe, Interpret, Evaluate. Describe what a camera could confirm. Two calls, both at ten in the morning on weekdays. Voicemails left in English. A file that lists a different primary language. Then interpret, with more than one possible meaning. Maybe the family works mornings. Maybe they could not understand the message. Maybe they did not know who was calling. Only then evaluate, and hold that evaluation open to correction.

Notice that the best explanations in that list were about the process, not the family. Those are the facts busy minds skip. They are also the facts you can change.

Then ask. Use a qualified interpreter. Offer a few times and channels. Say what will happen with the answer. Asking someone who depends on you for services takes care, because power can make a yes mean very little. A private way to correct the record later helps.

Be honest about the evidence. The research on attribution is strong, but much of it comes from laboratory studies. No controlled study was found that shows the three-step method itself changes workplace outcomes. Its parts rest on firmer ground: describe first, check the situation, and ask directly.

As a leader, you can make this normal for your team. Ask for the observation before the assessment. Treat a judgment word as a cue to ask what was seen. Thank the person who brings back a fact that changes the plan. The next time a quick story forms in your mind, find the one fact you are missing, and go get it.
