Real participation inside the team



A call-line supervisor at DHS needs to change how callback requests are sorted. The usual approach is familiar. Bring a draft to the team meeting. Ask for thoughts. Thank everyone. Move on. It feels inclusive. The research suggests it may do almost nothing.

In 2015, researchers studied participative leadership in two settings: workers in offices and call centers, and factory workers whose output was measured directly. They expected that more participation would mean better performance. Instead they found a threshold. Below a moderate level, participation was not linked to performance at all. Only above it did more participation go with better results. And the effect was strongest when leaders shared a lot of information.

Think about what that means. An occasional 'any thoughts?' may never reach the level where participation starts to matter. Asking without sharing the data leaves people guessing about limits the leader already knows.

A second body of research explains why. A meta-analysis of 72 studies, covering nearly 4,800 groups, looked at how teams share information. Sharing was linked to better performance. But the type of sharing mattered. Sharing what only some members knew was more strongly linked to performance than simply being open. Openness built cohesion. Unique information built results. And left alone, groups tend to talk about what everyone already knows.

So the supervisor changes the plan. Two days before the meeting, they send the call data, the staffing limits and the one rule that cannot change. They say the team is advising, and the decision will be made Friday. They ask each person to bring one pattern they hear on calls that others may not.

In the meeting, they go around in a set order, starting with those who usually speak last. Structure matters here. In the same meta-analysis, structured discussion was linked to more sharing and a bigger payoff from unique information.

Two workers who take most of the Spanish-language calls describe a delay that no report shows. Another worker raises a problem: the new sort could push urgent callbacks behind routine ones. A senior colleague disagrees.

This is the moment that decides whether people keep speaking up. Research on voice finds that raising problems is often rated lower than offering suggestions, even when the warning is right. So the supervisor thanks the worker for the specific point, and asks both people to walk through a real call. The risk turns out to be real. The plan changes.

On Friday, the supervisor sends the final plan with a short note. It names what changed, who raised each point, and why one popular idea was not used. A review of voice research found that people speak up when they believe it will have an impact. The note is the evidence of impact.

The lesson is simple to say and harder to do. Involve people on fewer decisions, and do it fully. Share first. Ask for what only they know. Protect the people who raise problems. And show what changed.
