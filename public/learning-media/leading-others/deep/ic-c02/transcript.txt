Who DHS serves, and who is on your team



Start with a map of Minnesota. Between 2010 and 2020, the share of White residents in Minnesota cities fell from about 81 percent to 73 percent, and every other group grew. You might expect that change to sit in Minneapolis and Saint Paul. It did not. In cities of five to ten thousand people, the White share fell from about 90 percent to 85. In the state's smallest cities, those with fewer than 250 residents, it fell from 94 percent to 89.

Add a few more facts. About 13 percent of Minnesotans speak a language other than English at home. Minnesota law recognizes 11 Tribal governments, and every state agency must consult with each one. Nationally, more than one in four adults has a disability, and the most common type involves cognition.

Why does this matter in a unit that processes contracts or writes policy? Because the words you write reach someone. A provider notice, a renewal letter or a county instruction reaches many kinds of readers. They differ in language, in how they read and in what they expect from government. Population numbers do not tell you what any one person needs. They tell you to design for range: plain language, more than one channel and an easy way to ask for help.

Now turn to your own team. Researchers pooled 30 studies of nearly half a million workers, many in human services. Diversity on its own had mixed effects. What was consistently linked to good outcomes was a climate of inclusion: fair treatment, a voice in decisions and access to information. Headcount is a start. Daily practice decides the result.

Daily practice gets tested in small moments. Suppose a team member asks to adjust their schedule for a religious observance during a deadline week. The tempting reply is to ask whether the observance could shift this year. Do not start there. Start with the work. Ask what adjustment they need and how they would like to handle it. Then plan the coverage. Look for swaps, adjusted hours or a staggered plan. If it may be a formal accommodation, bring in your human resources contact. Since 2023, federal law allows an employer to deny a religious accommodation only for a burden that is substantial in the context of its business.

Keep the reason confidential. If a coworker complains about special treatment, talk with them one-on-one about coverage and fair rules, not about anyone's faith.

The same habit works for names. Ask how a colleague wants their name said, and ask one-on-one. When you get it wrong, correct yourself briefly and keep going.

Who DHS serves and who is on your team are really one question. A team where people can raise a need without risk is the team most likely to notice when a process is shutting someone out.
