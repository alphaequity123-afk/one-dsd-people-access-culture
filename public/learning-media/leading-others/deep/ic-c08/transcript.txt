Patterns of difference, not profiles



Senior Eligibility Worker: I put a lot into that tips sheet. I have taken calls from these communities for twenty years. The patterns are real. New staff walk in blind.

Human Services Supervisor: I believe the patterns are real. I also read a meta-analysis of 558 studies. About 80 percent of the variation in work values was inside countries, not between them.

Senior Eligibility Worker: So you are saying my experience is wrong?

Human Services Supervisor: No. I am saying it describes the range, not the person. Your sheet says some callers say yes to be polite. That is true for some. For the caller last week, nobody knows, because nobody asked.

Senior Eligibility Worker: Fair. The new worker assumed the yes meant no and closed the call. That was not what I meant the sheet to do.

Human Services Supervisor: That is the risk. There are experiments on this. People told to imagine another person's view got no more accurate. Sometimes less. The only thing that helped was asking.

Senior Eligibility Worker: Then what do new staff do with what I know?

Human Services Supervisor: Your knowledge becomes the reason for the question. If some people decide with family, the worker asks who should be involved. If yes can mean many things, the worker does a read-back: what will you do next, and by what date?

Senior Eligibility Worker: And language? Half the confusion I see is really language.

Human Services Supervisor: That is the part with the strongest evidence. A review of 28 studies found professional interpreters beat family members and untrained staff. So the sheet should say: offer a professional interpreter early.

Senior Eligibility Worker: I can rewrite it. Each pattern, then the question it leads to. Then a date, never the word soon.

Human Services Supervisor: Would you lead the practice session with the team? They will listen to you more than to a study.
