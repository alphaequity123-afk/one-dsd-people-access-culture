Why culture matters in leading DHS work



Picture a renewal notice. It is clear, accurate and identical for everyone. One person reads it and calls with a question. Another reads it as a final denial and gives up. A third never opens it, because past letters from government brought bad news. The notice did not change. The reader did. Culture shapes how people read what an agency sends, how they disagree in a meeting and whether they trust the person on the phone.

Most leaders accept this. The harder question is how to get better at working with it. Here the research offers three surprises.

First, people are poor judges of their own skill. In one nursing school, 98 percent of students rated their intercultural competence higher than an assessment did. A broad review of self-assessment found that people have only moderate insight into their abilities. Insight is weakest for complex skills, and this is a complex skill. Feeling comfortable is not the same as being effective.

Second, exposure is not enough. A study followed nearly thirteen hundred college students. Those who studied abroad gained only about two points on an intercultural measure. Students who took classes mainly alongside local students, the most immersive group, barely changed at all. Students who met with a cultural mentor gained more. Contact with difference, without help to make sense of it, can confirm old assumptions as easily as challenge them.

Third, one-time training fades. A review of 260 training studies found that knowledge held up over time, but changes in attitude faded. Training worked better when it built skills, ran over a longer period and connected to other changes in the organization.

What about the models and assessments you may have encountered? They are useful maps. They remind you that people differ in how they make sense of difference, not just in what they know. But a score is one questionnaire on one day. No study found here shows it predicts how someone does their job. Never use a score to label yourself or anyone else.

So what works? Practice with guidance, on real work, again and again. After a hard call or a tense meeting, take ten minutes with a colleague who was there. Ask three questions. What did I assume? What might the other person have experienced? What will I try next time?

Then look at the decisions you control: the channel, the deadline, the meeting time, the script. Ask who each one works for, and who has to do extra work to use it. That is where culture stops being an idea and becomes part of how you lead.
