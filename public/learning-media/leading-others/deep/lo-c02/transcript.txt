Psychological safety in daily work



Human Services Supervisor: I think my unit is pretty open. I tell people all the time that they can bring me anything. But a renewal notice problem reached me through the call line last week, and someone on my team had noticed it days earlier.

Program Consultant: That pattern is common. In one interview study, 34 of 40 employees said they had at least once felt unable to raise something important. The top reasons were fear of looking like a complainer and fear of damaging a relationship.

Human Services Supervisor: But I would never punish someone for raising a problem.

Program Consultant: Most leaders would say that. The question staff ask is different. What happened the last time someone spoke? Were they thanked? Did the issue go anywhere? Did a senior colleague brush it off while the supervisor watched?

Human Services Supervisor: That last one stings. A team lead did dismiss a new worker's question a while back. I did not step in because I trusted the team lead's experience.

Program Consultant: Status matters a lot here. A study of 23 neonatal intensive care units found that physicians felt safer speaking up than nurses, and nurses felt safer than respiratory therapists. Where leaders actively invited input, that gap narrowed. The lowest-status staff gained the most.

Human Services Supervisor: So the new worker watched me side with the senior voice, and drew a conclusion.

Program Consultant: Probably. The good news is that repair is concrete. Go back, say they were right, and ask what else they have noticed. Then build small habits. Ask for problems in every huddle. Ask the newest people first. Report back on what happened to each issue.

Human Services Supervisor: Does safety mean I have to soften my standards? My unit handles eligibility. I cannot have people deciding rules are flexible.

Program Consultant: No. In fact, a study of 126 teams found that high safety can make it easier for a team to agree on an unethical shortcut, when the team already leans that way. Safety amplifies what a team values. The fix is to make it safe to question a shortcut while keeping the standard itself firm.

Human Services Supervisor: So safety and standards work together. People can tell me the standard was missed without fearing blame, but the standard does not move.

Program Consultant: Exactly. One caution: most of this research is correlational. It shows these habits go with learning and better work, not that they guarantee it. But they target the exact reasons people give for staying quiet.
