Owning your development



Think about the last time you grew at work. Not a training you attended, but a time you came out able to do something you could not do before. Chances are it involved real work, some stretch, and someone who told you how it was going.

That pattern has research behind it. A study of 60 managers tracked 225 work experiences. Skill growth rose with challenge, but it leveled off when the challenge got very steep. What kept people learning at the hardest end was feedback.

You may have heard that expertise comes from thousands of hours of practice. A large meta-analysis tested that claim. In games, music and sports, deliberate practice explained a real share of the differences between people. In professions, it explained less than one percent. A careful repeat of the famous violin study found a smaller effect than the original. Practice helps. It is not the whole story.

So what else matters? One answer is what you do with experience after it happens. Across 46 samples, debriefs improved performance by about a quarter on average. And they worked for individuals, not just teams. You can debrief yourself in five minutes. What did I intend? What happened? Why? What will I do next time?

Include what went well. In a study of 98 soldiers on navigation exercises, those who reviewed both successes and failures improved more than those who reviewed only failures. Most people learn from mistakes by habit. Few ask why something worked, so they cannot do it again on purpose.

Then there is initiative. A two-year study of 180 employees asked what proactive people actually do. Three things stood out. They tried new approaches. They learned how decisions get made and who shapes them. And they made their goals known and sought out development. All three were linked to later career progress and satisfaction.

Be realistic about the payoff. Across many studies, proactive personality has only a modest link to salary and promotion. The link to career satisfaction is stronger. In state service, where advancement follows job classes and openings, satisfaction may be the most reliable reward. It is a real one.

One last finding is worth carrying into your next conversation with a supervisor. In a study of 264 professionals, support for development predicted better performance when people saw a career path. When they did not, that same support predicted more leaving. So when you ask for training or a stretch assignment, connect it to something you can see ahead: a role, a kind of work, a direction.

Here is the practice. Choose one skill. Find one piece of real work that stretches it. Ask two people for one specific piece of feedback. Review your work afterward, including what went right. And when support is offered, ask which path it serves. Your development is yours to lead.
