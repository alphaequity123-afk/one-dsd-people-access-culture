When difference feels like a threat



Imagine a unit three months after two teams were merged. The backlog has doubled. Overtime has been cut. At the morning huddle, someone says, "The other team keeps dumping incomplete files on renewals. They just don't care." A few heads nod. The other team goes quiet.

It is easy to hear that comment as a character flaw. The research suggests a different starting point. A meta-analysis of 95 samples found that several kinds of threat were linked to more negative views of other groups. Those threats included competing for resources, worrying about losing a way of doing things and simple anxiety about working with people who differ. A doubled backlog and fewer hours touch all of them.

Scarcity goes deeper than mood. In lab studies, just framing resources as scarce changed how people perceived race and how they shared resources. Nobody in those studies set out to be unfair. That is the point. Pressure can shift judgment quietly, which is why fair, consistent processes matter most when budgets are tight.

So what does a leader do in the next minute? First, name the real pressure. "The backlog is real, and everyone is stretched." Second, turn the motive claim into a question about a step. "Which files? Which fields were missing?" Third, invite the other side's facts. "What does intake see from their end?" Often a mechanism appears, like two teams still using two different checklists.

Now the disagreement is about the work. That matters. Across more than a hundred studies, conflict about people consistently hurt teams. Conflict about the task was mixed, and in one study it helped performance where people felt safe to speak. Your job is not to end disagreement. It is to keep it on the work and make it safe.

Here is a finding many people do not expect. Contact between groups generally reduces prejudice. But when researchers asked why, learning facts about the other group was the weakest route. Lowering anxiety and building empathy did more. So a fact sheet about the other team helps less than regular shared work, such as mixed pairs on hard files, with equal roles.

Be honest about the limits. Much of the threat research is correlational. The scarcity studies are short lab experiments. When reviewers looked only at randomized contact studies, the effects were smaller, especially for racial and ethnic prejudice. Contact helps. It is not a quick fix.

Finally, watch your measures. Side-by-side team error rates invite blame. One shared measure, plus a measure of what people waiting experience, keeps everyone on the same side of the problem. When pressure makes difference feel like a threat, bring the team back to the task, together.
