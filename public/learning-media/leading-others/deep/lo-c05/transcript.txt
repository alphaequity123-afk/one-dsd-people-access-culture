Empathy and emotional intelligence, without the hype



Call-Line Supervisor: I pride myself on reading people. When a worker comes in upset, I can usually tell what is going on and get them back on track.

Human Services Program Manager: That is a common belief. Researchers tested it in 25 experiments. They asked people to imagine another person's perspective and then predict that person's feelings or preferences. Imagining did not improve accuracy. It slightly lowered it, and sometimes raised confidence.

Call-Line Supervisor: So I might be confidently wrong.

Human Services Program Manager: Possibly. What did work was asking. When people got the other person's perspective through conversation, accuracy went up. Imagination uses what you already believe. Asking brings in something new.

Call-Line Supervisor: Last week a worker came in after a hard call. I assumed the caller's anger had rattled her, so I talked about handling angry callers. On her way out she mentioned she was not sure about a phone number she gave.

Human Services Program Manager: That is the pattern. The number was the real concern, and possibly a risk for the caller that night. If you had asked what was hardest about the call, you might have heard it first.

Call-Line Supervisor: What about emotional intelligence training? My team was offered an assessment and workshop.

Human Services Program Manager: Be careful with big claims. A meta-analysis of 58 studies found training raises scores moderately. But effects on actual behavior at work are largely unknown. And one meta-analysis found the link between emotional intelligence and leadership was 0.05 for ability tests, close to zero.

Call-Line Supervisor: So it is mostly hype?

Human Services Program Manager: Not entirely. Emotional skills do seem to matter more in jobs with heavy emotional demands, like your call line. The useful part is specific skills you practice, with feedback. Asking before advising is one of them.

Call-Line Supervisor: My staff hear distress all day. I worry that asking them to be more empathic just wears them out.

Human Services Program Manager: That worry has support. In 11 studies, people avoided empathy because it felt effortful. But the avoidance disappeared when they felt they could empathize effectively. Give your staff concrete questions to ask and show them when their response helped. That makes empathy feel worth the effort.

Call-Line Supervisor: So for my next check-in: ask what was hardest, listen, then separate support for the worker from the steps that protect the caller.

Human Services Program Manager: And name who verifies the fact and who calls back, by when. Empathy and accuracy work together.
