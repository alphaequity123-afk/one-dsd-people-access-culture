Earning and repairing trust



Trust is a willingness to be vulnerable to someone because you expect them to act well. Staff show it when they raise a problem with you, accept an uncertain assignment, or plan around something you promised. Large reviews of the research link trust in leaders to better performance and commitment. And direct supervisors, the people staff work with every day, are an especially important source of that trust.

People decide whether to trust you on three things. Can you do the job? Do you care about them? Do you act on principle? One meta-analysis of 185 studies found that trust built on care predicted others' ratings of performance more strongly than trust built on reliability alone. Competence earns trust. Care earns more.

Sooner or later, every leader breaks trust. A promise slips under a deadline. A decision looks like favoritism. A problem someone raised goes nowhere. What happens next depends first on what people believe went wrong.

In two experiments, participants judged a job candidate accused of a prior error. When the error looked like a competence problem, an apology repaired trust better than a denial. People accept that a capable person can make one mistake. When it looked like an integrity problem, admitting fault was more damaging, because people read one integrity failure as revealing character. Denial helped there, but only when the evidence later showed the person was innocent. When the evidence showed guilt, apology still worked better. So the rule is simple. If you did it, own it.

Owning it is the start. In a trust game with 262 participants, researchers tested apologies and promises separately. An apology alone, with regret but no commitment, made no significant difference to how trust recovered. A promise to change sped recovery. And a consistent run of trustworthy actions did the most.

The same study found one kind of damage that never fully healed. When bad behavior came with deception, trust never fully recovered, even after a promise, an apology and a long run of good behavior. Under pressure, a partial account or a convenient explanation can feel harmless. It can cost trust you will not get back.

Put this together, and repair has a shape. Find out what people think went wrong. Give a full, accurate account. Apologize for the specific failure. Make a specific promise about what will change, and when. Address the cause, not only the symptom. Then keep that promise, especially when keeping it costs you time. That is the moment people use to decide whether your word means anything.

A note on limits. Most trust-repair studies are lab experiments, often with students. They describe how people reason about trust, and that reasoning shows up at work. But no study can tell you exactly how your team will respond. Watch what people do after you act. That is the evidence that counts.
