From vision to plan



A division director announces a priority for the year. People should get waiver services started sooner after their plan is complete. Everyone agrees. And on Monday morning, almost nothing changes. That is the usual fate of a vision. It is not wrong. It just is not yet something anyone can do.

The first step is a goal people can see. Decades of research on goal setting point the same way. Specific, challenging goals lead to better performance than telling people to do their best. 'Do your best' sounds supportive, but it has no reference point, so almost any result can count. A better goal for a review team might be cutting the median time from complete plan to decision from 21 days to 14 by June.

Notice that the goal belongs to the team, not to each reviewer. Plan review is shared work. It moves from county to state, and from one reviewer to another. A meta-analysis of group goals found that team goals improved team performance. In the few studies that tested it, individual targets focused on personal output hurt work that depends on teamwork. A count of plans per reviewer looks fair. It can also teach people to pick the easy cases first.

Now suppose no one knows why plans take 21 days. Here the research adds an exception. On new, complex work, a hard target can make people anxious and scattered. They try many things and learn little. A learning goal works better. Within three weeks, find the three most common delays and test one fix for each. The June target stays as direction. The first job is learning.

Every goal needs a limit. Researchers who study the side effects of goals warn that targets narrow attention. They can also invite shortcuts. In public work, the cost of a shortcut often lands on the people with the most complex needs. So write the guardrails first. Accuracy must hold. Appeal rates must not rise. Complex plans must not wait longer than the rest. Check those lines every time you check progress.

Then make the first step concrete. In a large workplace experiment, asking employees to write down the date and time they would get a flu shot raised vaccination rates. Writing only the date did not have a clear effect. The same idea works for a team plan. On Tuesday after the team meeting, two program consultants pull ten stalled cases and note where each one stopped.

Finally, use the vision to choose. You will have two good options and time for one. Ask which one is more likely to get services started sooner. Then explain your choice in those words. A vision earns its place when it helps a team say no to something good, for a reason everyone can see.
