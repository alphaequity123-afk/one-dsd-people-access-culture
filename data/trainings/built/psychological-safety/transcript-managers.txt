Psychological Safety: What happens after someone asks a question, admits a mistake or disagrees, and how to make that moment useful

Why an ordinary question carries risk

Narrator: Welcome. Think about the last time you had a question at work and held it back. Maybe you worried you would look unprepared. Maybe the person you would be questioning had more experience, or more say over your next assignment. Maybe the meeting was moving fast, and nobody else seemed confused. Holding back felt reasonable. Most people have done it. This course is about that moment, and about the moment right after someone does speak up. Psychological safety is about what people expect will happen when they ask a question, admit a mistake, raise a concern or disagree. It is not about being nice or comfortable. It is about whether useful information can reach the work. You will look at what the idea means, why the risk is not the same for everyone, and how to raise and receive a question. Then you will step into a workplace simulation where your choices shape what happens next.

Narrator: Here is a small exchange from one of the readings behind this course. A colleague asks, “Could this instruction leave someone without a usable route?” The reply is, “We have already discussed that.” Nothing dramatic happened. Yet look at what the reply does. It says a discussion took place. It does not say what that discussion settled. The colleague may need a reference, a correction or a clearer answer. Instead, the reply mostly discourages asking again. If questions keep getting replies like that, people may stop offering the uncertainty the work needs. The reading is careful here. That is a possible pattern, not proof of how everyone feels. So start with the specific response, and the work question it leaves open.

Narrator: Why does this matter in public service? Amy Edmondson, a professor at Harvard Business School, is the researcher most associated with the term psychological safety. In her hospital research, the better teams reported more errors, not fewer. They felt able to talk about them. Her work also argues that fear is a poor motivator. It narrows thinking, and it leads people to hold back information that leaders need. Many major failures, in companies and in public organizations, trace back to people who saw a problem and stayed silent. The reading points to human services directly. A case review or an incident debrief that brings problems to the surface, rather than hiding them, matters directly for the safety of the people served. A reflection question from that reading is worth keeping: where would someone hesitate before reporting an error, and what would it cost the person served if they stayed silent?

Narrator: This is not only a research topic. One reading reports that staff in the Disability Services Division described limited psychological safety, performance pressure, and doubt that their input changes decisions. The reading calls those practice problems with practice answers. That framing matters. It means the answer is not to find braver employees. It is to change what happens when people speak. How is disagreement handled? How are mistakes treated? Who carries the repair when something lands badly? And does anyone's input actually change anything? Those are things other people can see. They are also things you can practice, whatever your role.

Narrator: Here is the path ahead. First, what psychological safety is and is not. Then why speaking up costs some people more than others. Next, how to raise a precise question, and how to receive one about your own work. After that, mistakes and repair. Finally, where team habits end and formal routes begin. Each part closes with a few questions to think over, alone or with a colleague you trust. Before you go on, bring one real moment to mind. A time you held a question back, or a time someone questioned you. Keep it with you as you go.

Question: A colleague asks whether an instruction could leave someone without a usable route. The reply is, “We have already discussed that.” What does the reply leave open?
  It says a discussion happened but not what it settled, so the colleague's work question is still unanswered and the next person may think twice before asking.
    Narrator: You kept the work question in view and noticed what the reply teaches. Build on that by asking what the earlier discussion actually established.
    Narrator: Next time you hear a reply like that, try: “What did that discussion settle? I want to make sure this case is covered.”
  Very little, since the team already talked it through, and reopening old discussions every time someone asks can slow the work down for everyone else in the meeting.
    Narrator: Protecting meeting time is a real concern, and repeating old debates can drain a team. The catch is that the reply never showed what the earlier talk decided, so the question stays open.
    Narrator: From the asker's side, the reply can sound like the question itself was the problem. A one-line pointer to what was decided would answer it and save time.

What psychological safety is and is not

Narrator: Edmondson defined team psychological safety as a shared belief held by members of a team that the team is safe for interpersonal risk taking. Interpersonal risks are ordinary work acts that could make someone look ignorant, incompetent or disruptive. Asking a question. Admitting a mistake. Raising a concern. Disagreeing with someone more senior. Notice two things about that definition. First, it is shared. It describes how a group works, not how confident one person is. Second, it is about expectations: what people believe will happen if they take that small risk. So the question is not whether someone is naturally bold. The question is how the team responds when someone takes an ordinary work risk.

Narrator: Her 1999 study looked at fifty-one work teams in a manufacturing company. Psychological safety was associated with more learning behavior, such as asking for help, discussing errors and raising uncertainty. Learning behavior, in turn, was associated with better team performance. The readings are careful about what that means. The study shows association, not proof that one causes the other. The pattern has appeared in many settings since. Still, it does not certify any team at DHS, and it is not a way to diagnose an employee. Think of it as a useful lens for looking at how your team handles uncertainty.

Narrator: Here is the most common misunderstanding. Psychological safety is not being nice, avoiding conflict or lowering expectations. Edmondson's goal is high safety combined with high expectations. A team can hold a demanding standard and still make it safe to say that something is not working. In fact, candor is what lets high standards be met. Her book describes candid organizations, such as the film studio Pixar, as demanding rather than comfortable. In her account, candor is how quality gets made. Discomfort is normal when people learn. The real question is whether speaking up gets punished or humiliated. Leaders, in her account, still sanction clear violations. Safety and accountability sit together.

Narrator: A smooth meeting can feel like a good sign. Nobody objects. Everyone nods. But the readings warn that a meeting with no questions may reflect clarity, a missed opportunity, or worry about the response. The record alone cannot tell you which. Agreement is useful when it is supported. It does not show how the team would handle a challenge. The opposite is also true. A hard conversation does not mean learning is impossible. So do not use calm as proof, and do not use conflict as proof either. Look at what actually happens to a question when someone asks it.

Narrator: A team needs room to challenge a conclusion without the challenge being treated as misconduct. Minnesota Management and Budget's Respectful Workplace policy, Policy 1432, draws the same line. It separates respectful disagreement and normal professional supervision from disrespectful conduct, such as shouting, threats, bullying or demeaning treatment. That gives you two questions to keep apart. Is the claim supported by evidence? And was the exchange respectful? A colleague can be wrong and respectful. A colleague can be right and still speak to someone in a demeaning way. Each question gets its own answer, and neither one cancels the other.

Narrator: Sometimes a leader tries to build safety by promising too much. Every idea will be accepted. Nothing said here will ever leave this room. Nobody will be held accountable. Those promises sound welcoming. The readings say they go beyond both the concept and anyone's authority. Ideas still need evidence. Some concerns a supervisor has to route formally, such as an allegation of discrimination. Accountability still applies. A more honest invitation sounds like this: “Raise questions here. Here is what is still open, who decides, and what has to go through another process.” That is less dramatic. It is also much easier to believe. The readings add one more distinction. Cultural safety, a team's ability to raise concerns, and an organization's systems for reporting and responding are related, but different. One survey or one session does not measure all three.

Question: Which statement fits psychological safety as the readings describe it?

    Narrator: You held both halves together: room to take an ordinary risk, and standards that still apply. Build on it by noticing which half your team tends to drop under pressure.
    Narrator: On your team, what usually happens to the first person who disagrees?
  A team where people feel comfortable and conflict stays low, because members trust each other enough that hard feedback is rarely needed to get the work done well.
    Narrator: Many people picture safety as comfort, and a calm team can feel safe. The readings frame it differently: safety makes hard feedback possible rather than unnecessary.
    Narrator: Compare this with high safety and high expectations. A team can be candid and demanding at once. Low conflict on its own says little about what happens to a question.
  A promise from the leader that every concern raised inside the team stays inside the team, so people can speak without worrying about what might follow.
    Narrator: That promise goes beyond any leader's authority. Some concerns a supervisor has to route formally. A stronger invitation explains what is open and what has to go elsewhere.
    Narrator: Picture the person who later learns their concern had to be reported after all. An honest limit at the start would have served them better than a promise.
  A trait that confident employees bring with them, which is why some people on a team speak up easily in meetings while others usually stay quiet.
    Narrator: Edmondson describes a team condition, not a personality trait. The useful question is how the team responds to a risk, not who is naturally bold.
    Narrator: When someone is quiet, try looking at the conditions first: how was the last question received, and was there another way to contribute?

Why the risk is not the same for everyone

Narrator: The same invitation can land very differently on two people in the same room. A senior employee and someone in their first month may both hear, “Any questions?” Their position and their past experience differ, so the risk feels different. The readings name several things that shape that risk. Position. How earlier questions were received. How visible the person is. What support they can reach. None of that creates a rule about any one person. It does mean you should not read someone's confidence, experience or agreement from silence alone. And repeatedly asking the least secure person to be braver can leave the real conditions unchanged.

Narrator: Many leaders say their door is always open. That statement helps only when what happens next supports it. The person who invites input may also control assignments, evaluations and development. Staff can reasonably weigh those consequences before disagreeing. In one reading's practice case, a temporary staff member sees an access problem in a form. She expects that challenging the project lead in public could harm her future assignments. A prepared written route, or a private conversation, may make that contribution usable. It should not require her to explain her personal history. And a lack of visitors through the open door does not show that no concerns exist.

Narrator: The Fearless Organization reading adds a point about inclusion. Staff from underrepresented groups often bear the highest cost for speaking up. So a climate that punishes candor tends to silence them first. The same reading notes a common critique. Edmondson's framework says little about how power and identity shape who feels safe. Her later writing addresses this, the reading notes, but practitioners should still add an equity lens. Here is the guardrail. This is about conditions on a team, not about what any one person feels. You cannot know someone's experience from a group they belong to. The reading asks whether people from different identity groups experience the same level of safety on your team, and how you would find out. Finding out is the point. Guessing is not.

Narrator: One reading tells a practice story about two colleagues reviewing a draft. One asks questions right away, as the draft is read aloud. The other wants to review the wording first and send a prepared question. At first, each misreads the other. The quick questioner sees the delay as reluctance to challenge the team. The careful preparer sees the quick questions as putting colleagues on the spot. Then each explains what their habit means to them. For one, asking right away showed care: you name a problem while others can still fix it together. For the other, arriving with a considered question respected everyone's preparation and the relationship. Both cared about the work. Those meanings belonged to those two people, who chose to share them. Neither one speaks for a group.

Narrator: The lesson is not to list styles or ask people to be tolerant. It is to change what you do. Make the open question visible. Let people raise it in the room, or send a prepared question in time for the decision. Then check that the written route actually reaches the person responsible before the choice closes. A comment box nobody reads is not a real option. Moving someone's contribution out of the room and then forgetting it keeps the look of choice while losing the knowledge. Privately, notice which signal you tend to reward. Speed. Certainty. Preparation. Direct challenge. Each one can be a form of care.

Narrator: One more guardrail. A person should not have to share a personal history, or explain an experience of exclusion, before their question is taken seriously. Nor should anyone be asked to speak for a whole community, or to carry the work of educating others because of who they are. A practical concern about a form or a process can stand on its own. Ask for evidence and perspective, not identity stories. If someone chooses to share more, receive it with care and keep it with them.

Question: A newer colleague rarely speaks in team meetings but sends careful written questions afterward. Which response fits the readings best?
  Make sure written questions reach the person deciding before the choice closes, and do not read the colleague's quiet in meetings as low confidence or commitment.
    Narrator: You treated the written questions as real contributions and made sure they can still matter. Build on it by telling the team when written input is due and who reads it.
    Narrator: To the colleague, a prepared question may be the respectful way to contribute. Seeing it reach the decision tells them the route is real.
  Encourage the colleague to raise questions in the room next time, since live questions get discussed by everyone and their good ideas deserve more visibility on the team.
    Narrator: Wanting a colleague's good ideas to be seen is generous. The catch is that it makes speaking in the room the measure of contribution, and the written route already works.
    Narrator: A stronger response changes the arrangement instead of the person: written questions reach the decision in time, and live questions stay welcome too.
  Ask the colleague what in their background makes speaking in meetings hard for them, so the team can adjust the way it runs meetings to fit their culture better.
    Narrator: This asks for a personal story and assumes the reason is cultural. A stronger move asks what would make contributing easier, without needing to know why.
    Narrator: Try instead: “Your written questions have been useful. Is there anything about how the team takes input that would make it easier for you?”

Reflect: Who on your team has the most influence over other people's assignments? How might that shape what gets said in the room?
Reflect: Which signal of engagement do you tend to reward: speed, certainty, preparation or direct challenge?
Reflect: If you wanted to know whether everyone on your team can raise concerns equally, how would you find out without guessing?

Raising a question and receiving one

Narrator: When you raise a concern, precision helps the work. One reading offers a simple shape. Name what you observed. Explain how it could affect the task. Ask for what you need. In the reading's practice story, a team member notices that the current source lists two contact options, while a draft notice lists only the web contact. She first considers saying, “You are hiding the access problem.” That adds a motive the material does not show. Instead she says, “This paragraph names only the web contact. The current source includes another option. Which one applies to this notice?” The second version gives the person responsible something concrete to check, instead of a character to defend.

Narrator: You can name your uncertainty honestly. “I may be missing a condition” invites a check without giving up the concern. That is different from an apology that erases the question: “Sorry, I am probably wrong, so ignore this.” Neither inflated certainty nor self-dismissal makes the evidence stronger. And precision should never become an entry fee. A person under pressure may not phrase a concern neatly. If you are on the receiving end, hear the substance and ask a clarifying question. “This instruction names no contact” is easier to examine than “Nobody cares.” But someone who says the second one may still have found something real.

Narrator: Choose a realistic route. If the person responsible for the draft is in the meeting, point to the paragraph there. If another role must interpret the condition, help bring the question to that role. You do not need to quietly become the final policy authority, or promise a correction you cannot approve. Clear responsibility for receiving the question matters more than copying a long list of people on a message. And this is worth saying plainly. Better wording is not your job alone. A precise question can still meet a poor response. How the team receives questions matters just as much as how you ask them.

Narrator: Now flip it. Someone questions your work. In another practice story, an experienced colleague has prepared a careful draft. A new colleague asks whether one sentence explains the next contact step accurately. The experienced colleague feels the effort behind the draft is being overlooked. She starts to say, “You would understand if you had been here longer.” The draft may well be sound. But that reply puts length of service where the evidence should be. A more useful opening is, “Thanks for pointing to that sentence. What next step does it suggest to you?” That gathers how the sentence actually reads, so it can be compared with the source.

Narrator: Taking a question seriously does not mean agreeing with it. You can thank the person, check the source, and then explain why the wording is accurate. Or you can change it through the proper review. Or you can name a condition that another role has to settle. The value is in examining it. A warm “Great question,” followed by no look at the sentence, leaves both the work gap and the interpersonal gap in place. Make the answer usable. Tell the person what you found, or who is checking and how the answer will come back. A note that says “received” is not the same thing as an answer.

Narrator: One last trap. When a question feels personal, it is tempting to defend your good intention, or to ask the other person to confirm that everything is fine. But your intention to help does not answer whether the instruction worked. If that defense becomes a habit, colleagues learn that feedback mainly creates a need to comfort the receiver. Do not make agreement, loyalty or a personal story the price of getting a question examined. Privately, notice what you hear when someone questions your work. Does a sign of status or preparation make the question sound like disrespect? Asking one clarifying question before you explain is a small practice that changes a lot.

Question: A new colleague asks whether a sentence in your draft explains the next contact step correctly. You are fairly sure it does. What is the most useful first response?
  Thank them for pointing to the sentence, ask what next step it suggests to them, and compare it with the current source before you explain what you find.
    Narrator: You stayed open even though you expected to be right. That keeps the question about the sentence, not about either of you. Build on it by telling them what you found.
    Narrator: If the source backs you up, try: “Here is the condition it follows. Does that make the sentence clearer, or should the wording change?”
  Walk them through the background behind the wording and how long the team has used it, so they can see why the sentence reads the way it does now.
    Narrator: Explaining the background is a natural, helpful instinct, especially with someone new. The gap is that history shows how the sentence came to be, not whether it reads clearly today.
    Narrator: A stronger first move asks how the sentence reads to them. Then the background can answer the actual confusion instead of a guessed one.
  Tell them it is a great question and you appreciate them speaking up, then keep the sentence as written, since you are fairly sure it is right.
    Narrator: The thanks is warm, but the sentence never gets examined. Recognition works best as the start of a check, not a replacement for one.
    Narrator: To the new colleague, praise followed by no change and no explanation can feel like a polite way of closing the door.
  Change the sentence right away to match their reading, since a new person's confusion is enough evidence that the wording is not working for anyone reading it.
    Narrator: Welcoming the question does not settle its answer. A stronger move checks the source first, then changes the wording if the check supports it.
    Narrator: What would you want to know before deciding the sentence is wrong for everyone?

Reflect: Think of a concern you raised that turned into a debate about motives. How could you have named only what you observed?
Reflect: When someone questions your work, what is your first reaction? What clarifying question could you ask before explaining?
Reflect: Where have you seen a friendly acknowledgment stand in for an actual answer?

Mistakes and repair

Narrator: Mistakes are where psychological safety is tested hardest. Picture a service message that goes out with the wrong date. The readings say to keep two needs in view. First, the present problem. The date may need correcting right away, by the person responsible for the message. Second, the learning question. How did the wrong date get in, and how did it get past review? Calling the employee careless can hide a confusing source or a missed review step. Saying mistakes never matter ignores the effect on the people who read the message. A thoughtful response handles the current problem first, then asks a specific learning question.

Narrator: In another practice story, a team member sends a review package without its source attachment. The receiver cannot check a condition and sends it back. The team member offers to talk about the mistake at the next team learning meeting. Reflection may help, but it does not supply the missing attachment. Start with what the work needs now. Then look at how it happened. In the story, the package checklist covered the draft but not its source. The sender also assumed the receiver could find the source alone. A specific fix might add the source to the checklist. A promise never to make a mistake again offers certainty that nobody can deliver.

Narrator: Under pressure, teams often reach for one of two shortcuts. One is public blame. Name the person and move on. That can produce a quick explanation. It also discourages people from disclosing errors, and it can hide a gap in the process. The other shortcut is to declare that nobody is responsible, because the system caused it. That can make disclosure easier for a moment. But it goes beyond a leader's authority, and it can leave the next task with no owner. The readings describe a third path: learning with boundaries. Correct the work. Examine conditions and choices together. Keep any formal matter, such as a personnel question, in its own route.

Narrator: Some mistakes are not about documents. They are about how someone was treated. One reading puts it directly. When a comment or decision lands badly, the person with more power leads the repair. The leader names it and takes responsibility. The person harmed should not have to explain, educate or forgive on a schedule. Most awkward moments come from not knowing, and the fitting response is information and a chance to change. A lack of knowledge does not erase harm, though. And it does not decide whether a formal reporting process applies. Intentional harm goes to the formal channels.

Narrator: Here is a practice case from the readings. A supervisor of a policy team dismisses a newer analyst's concern that a notice will confuse families. There is no time to revisit it, she says. Two weeks later, a county partner raises the same problem. The supervisor notices the analyst has stopped speaking in meetings. What now? Telling him privately to speak up more puts the burden back on him. Waiting for him to raise it puts repair on the person with less power. The reading's stronger response: at the next team meeting, she says he raised this first, that she should have listened, what will change in the notice, and how she will handle concerns from now on. That works because his concern was raised in an open meeting. If someone raised a concern privately, ask them before you credit them in front of others.

Narrator: You do not have to be a supervisor to repair a response. If you brushed off a colleague's question, you can say so. “My reply cut off a relevant question. Here is how it will get an answer now.” That is more useful than asking the colleague to confirm that everything is fine. An apology can matter without settling what the other person experienced. Also check where your first reading traveled. Did it end up in a note, a summary, or someone's view of a colleague's ability? A private change of mind may not fix a label that has already moved. Correct what you can, within your role.

Question: A teammate realizes a notice went out with an outdated date. The team lead wants to handle it well. Which response fits the readings best?
  Correct the date through the person responsible now, then look at how the old date got in and past review, keeping any formal matter separate.
    Narrator: You put the people reading the notice first, then made room to learn. Build on that by picking one specific step to change and checking whether it helps next time.
    Narrator: For the teammate, seeing the problem fixed and the process examined, rather than their character, makes it easier to speak up the next time something slips.
  Put the error on the agenda for next month's team learning review, so the whole team can examine it together without anyone feeling singled out in the moment.
    Narrator: Wanting to learn as a team without singling anyone out is a good instinct. The catch is timing: the people who got the wrong date need a correction now, not next month.
    Narrator: Try doing both: correct the notice today, then bring the how-it-happened question to the review.
  Reassure the teammate that nobody is responsible because the process caused it, so that people across the team feel safe admitting mistakes in the future.
    Narrator: The process may have played a part, but declaring that nobody is responsible goes beyond what a lead can promise and can leave the fix with no owner.
    Narrator: How could you name the process gap and still keep the next step owned by someone?
  Name the teammate in the team meeting so the cause is clear, then ask everyone to check their work more carefully from now on.
    Narrator: Public blame gives a quick explanation but can hide the step that let the date through. A stronger response corrects the notice and examines the review process.
    Narrator: Everyone else in the meeting learns something too: that admitting an error gets you named in front of the team.

Reflect: When a mistake surfaces on your team, which shortcut is more tempting: blame, or declaring that nobody is responsible?

Team habits and formal routes

Narrator: Psychological safety makes it more likely that people raise problems early. It does not replace formal routes, and it does not make anyone an investigator. The respectful workplace reading separates three layers. Federal and Minnesota law set the floor for illegal harassment and discrimination. State policy sets a higher standard for daily conduct. Policy 1436 covers unwelcome conduct tied to a protected class, including offensive jokes, slurs, deliberately misgendering someone, and deliberately mispronouncing their name. Policy 1432 covers disrespect that is not tied to a protected class, such as shouting, bullying or demeaning treatment. Here is the practical point. You do not have to decide which layer something fits before you raise it. Sorting that out belongs to Human Resources and the DHS Equal Opportunity and Access Division.

Narrator: If you see disrespect or harassment happen, you have options. Right To Be, a national nonprofit, teaches five of them, known as the five Ds. Distract: interrupt the moment, perhaps with a work question or a short break. Delegate: bring in someone with more authority, such as a supervisor or a meeting chair. Document: make a factual note of what happened. Delay: check in afterward, say it was not okay, and ask what the person needs. Direct: name the behavior briefly, but only when it is safe and likely to help. With any note you make, ask the targeted person what they want done with it, and do not share it without their permission, unless you are a supervisor with a duty to report. Delay is often the most underused option. It leaves the targeted person in control of what happens next.

Narrator: If a colleague brings you a concern, listen and thank them. Do not judge whether it is serious enough. Be honest about what you can and cannot keep confidential. Then share the routes. A supervisor. Human Resources. The Equal Opportunity and Access Division. A union representative. The Employee Assistance Program. And agencies outside DHS, such as the Minnesota Department of Human Rights or the federal Equal Employment Opportunity Commission. Do not interview other people or try to work out what happened. Route the concern; do not investigate it. And watch for retaliation. No one may be punished for reporting, for opposing harassment or discrimination, or for taking part in an investigation. Retaliation can be obvious, like a worse assignment, or subtle, like being left out of meetings.

Narrator: If you are a manager or supervisor, there is a specific duty. Under Policy 1436, managers and supervisors immediately report protected-class allegations or incidents to Human Resources or the agency Affirmative Action Officer, and follow agency procedures. DHS policy directs supervisors to refer these complaints to the Equal Opportunity and Access Division, and not to investigate them. That means no interviewing witnesses, no asking the person to prove what happened, no confronting the person accused, and no mediating. You cannot agree to keep protected-class harassment secret. And a team conversation, an apology or an attempt at repair can never delay that report. Repair may matter later. It is never a gate.

Narrator: Back to everyday team habits. Edmondson's toolkit for leaders has three parts. Set the stage by framing the work as uncertain and tying it to purpose. Invite participation through humility, inquiry and structured forums. Respond productively by appreciating input, removing the stigma from failure, and still sanctioning clear violations. In practice, “Any questions?” at the end of a meeting is often too broad and too late. A useful invitation names what is being decided, what is still uncertain, who decides, and how people can respond before the choice closes. One reading adds a simple habit: before deciding, ask for the strongest case against your own idea. Her book adds one more point. A leader's reaction to the first person who speaks up shapes whether anyone else will.

Narrator: An invitation is only a beginning. People watch what happens next. Did the question get an answer? Did the answer address what was asked? One reading suggests a regular habit. Say what changed because someone spoke up, and if nothing did, say why. You do not have to agree with every suggestion. What helps is a useful account of how the question was considered. The same reading adds: watch the grind. Constant urgency silences people. Protect time for thinking, and for saying no. Thank people who raise concerns, in a way that respects their preferences. Public thanks may be welcome. It should never expose someone who raised a concern privately.

Narrator: Finally, be careful how you judge progress. Fewer questions could mean clearer instructions, less opportunity, or reluctance to ask. More concerns could mean the route works better, or that a new problem appeared. A count alone cannot settle it. Avoid ranking people by how often they speak, and avoid labels for a quiet colleague. Describe what actually happened. Which questions got answers. Which are still open. What one person told you, kept to its own scope. What nobody has said stays unknown. That kind of honest, mixed account gives the next conversation something real to build on.

Question: You do not supervise anyone. A colleague on another team tells you a teammate keeps joking about their accommodation in front of others. They ask you to keep it between the two of you. What fits best?
  Thank them, say the jokes are not okay, describe routes such as their supervisor, Human Resources or the Equal Opportunity and Access Division, and ask what they want next.
    Narrator: You took it seriously, kept the decision with the person, and made the routes concrete. Build on it by offering to go with them if they choose to report. If you supervise or manage, the answer changes: you report it right away, and you tell them that honestly.
    Narrator: If they choose to tell their supervisor, it helps to mention honestly that a supervisor has to report a concern like this right away.
  Write down what they told you and send it to Human Resources yourself today, so the concern reaches people who can act on it before anything like it happens again.
    Narrator: Wanting it to reach someone who can act is a caring instinct. As a colleague without a supervisor's duty, though, sending it without asking takes the choice away from the person it happened to.
    Narrator: A stronger response shares the routes and asks what they want. Your note can support them, if and when they decide to use it.
  Talk with the teammate yourself and ask what happened, so you can tell whether the jokes were meant to hurt before anyone decides to involve Human Resources.
    Narrator: Finding out what happened is investigating, and it belongs to the people responsible for that process. It can also expose the colleague who confided in you.
    Narrator: Whose decision is it whether the teammate hears about this, and when?
  Agree to keep it between the two of you, since it is their experience to handle, and check in with them next week to see whether the jokes have stopped.
    Narrator: Honoring their request is respectful, and as a peer you can keep it private. What it leaves out is the routes, which are what make their choice a real one.
    Narrator: A stronger response keeps it private and still names the routes, so the decision stays with them and is an informed one.

Reflect: If someone brought you a concern tomorrow, would you know which routes to share? What would you need to look up first?
Reflect: Which of the five Ds feels most natural to you, and which feels hardest?
Reflect: When did you last hear someone say what changed because a colleague spoke up? What would it take to make that a habit?

The Guidance Error

If you manage a statewide program, your choices reach every county and every team under you. I have seen how the first response to an error decides whether the next one surfaces early. Here a guidance error reaches counties, a project staff member brings you an early warning and a harder concern, and you decide what changes in the work.

Your role: You are a Human Services Manager running a statewide program. You turn legislative requirements into guidance and services, decide how staff time and budget are used, and manage two supervisors and their teams.

In this scenario: the policy analyst; the team supervisor; the project staff member; the county representative; your division director

Your program recently sent a guidance bulletin to every county and lead agency about a requirement the legislature changed. A policy analyst drafted it, the analyst’s team supervisor reviewed it, and you approved it. A project staff member on a temporary appointment works on the same team. Your division director has asked to hear about anything that goes wrong in county guidance.

Decision
The county representative calls. The bulletin’s eligibility note uses the threshold from before the legislative change, and county workers are already applying it. You confirm it: the policy analyst drafted from a stale copy of the source, and the team supervisor’s review checked the draft against that same copy. At your managers’ meeting, the team supervisor says, “Nobody is to blame here. The system caused this.” Another supervisor says the analyst should have known better.

Counties are applying an outdated threshold today. How do you lead the response?
  Choice: Correct the bulletin to counties today through the usual route. Then trace the source check with the policy analyst and the team supervisor, and keep any performance question separate.
    What happens: The correction reaches counties that afternoon, and the county representative thanks you for the speed. In the review, the policy analyst explains that he pulled the source from a shared folder that still held the old version beside the new one. The team supervisor reviewed against the same file. Each assumed the other had checked the current source. No one is labeled. The open question is whose job the current-source check is, and right now the answer is no one’s.
    Narrator: You put the current harm first and the learning second, and you did not let either replace the other. Tracing the handoff found a gap that blame would have hidden. Build on it by naming who owns the current-source check before the next bulletin.
    Narrator: For the county representative and the workers applying the note, a same-day correction mattered more than any review. For the policy analyst, being asked how the work happened, rather than told what he is, makes the next error more likely to surface early.
    Narrator: When an error reaches the public, what do you reach for first: the fix, the explanation or the person?
  Choice: Agree with the team supervisor that nobody is to blame and the system caused it, so staff see that admitting errors is safe. Send the correction once the template is fixed.
    What happens: Staff are relieved. The template fix takes four days, and counties apply the old threshold for those four days. When you look for who will check sources on the next bulletin, there is no answer, because the account you gave says no one had a part in this one.
    Narrator: Wanting staff to feel safe admitting errors is a good aim. But a system cause does not mean no one had a responsibility, and the correction never needed to wait for the template.
    Narrator: Compare this with correcting today and then tracing the handoff. The analyst and the supervisor both had a part, and so did the shared folder. Naming that is accountability without a verdict.
  Choice: Tell the division in your update that the policy analyst drafted from an outdated source, so everyone understands what went wrong and checks their own sources more carefully from now on.
    What happens: The correction goes out that day, and so does the update. The policy analyst reads it at his desk. Two other analysts start rereading their own drafts alone instead of asking anyone. The review step that used the same stale file is not mentioned anywhere.
    Narrator: Naming one person’s step publicly answered who, not how. It hid the review gap and made the error about a person. Tracing the handoff would have found both.
    Narrator: For the policy analyst, a division-wide update is a public verdict. For everyone else, it is a lesson about what happens to the person whose error is found.
  Choice: Hold a learning review with the whole team next week before anything changes, so the correction to counties reflects everything it finds in one message.
    What happens: The review is thoughtful when it happens. Until then, county workers keep applying the old threshold, and the county representative calls again to ask whether a correction is coming. One message to counties was neater. It was also a week late for the people being assessed in the meantime.
    Narrator: A learning review is worth doing. It cannot do the current work. The correction counties needed today did not depend on knowing how the error happened.
    Narrator: For the county representative, the delay looks like the state knew and waited. That shapes whether the county calls quickly next time.

Decision
The next morning, the project staff member asks for a few minutes with you. He says, “Two weeks before the bulletin went out, I told my supervisor the eligibility note looked like it came from an old source. He said it wasn’t my place. When I raised it again, he made a joke about my disability in front of the team. I’m on a temporary appointment, and he decides my next assignment. I wasn’t sure who else to tell.”

He has raised a work warning and a disability-related comment by his supervisor. What do you do?
  Choice: Thank him, and say you must report the disability comment to human resources this morning and will not investigate it yourself. Ask what he needs to feel safe at work.
    What happens: You say, “Thank you for telling me. Because I manage this area, I have to report the comment about your disability to human resources or the Equal Opportunity and Access Division right away. I won’t look into it or talk to your supervisor about it myself. The people who receive it will contact you. I can’t keep this between us, but I can tell you what happens next.” You ask what he needs now. He asks to sit apart from his supervisor at this week’s team meeting, and you arrange it. You tell him retaliation is prohibited and that you will watch for any change in his assignments. You make the report that morning. Separately, you note his earlier warning about the source as a work fact about how the error happened.
    Narrator: You were honest about your duty before he had to ask, you did not investigate, and you asked what he needed instead of deciding for him. Build on it by keeping his work warning in view: it is a separate question that still deserves an answer.
    Narrator: For a temporary staff member whose next assignment depends on the person he is reporting, the risk of speaking was high. Being told plainly what happens next, and that retaliation is watched for, makes that risk easier to carry.
    Narrator: Do you know, today, how to reach the person in human resources who receives these reports for your area?
  Choice: Promise to keep it between the two of you and talk to his supervisor yourself, so the joke stops quickly, his assignment is protected, and nobody has to go through a formal process.
    What happens: He looks relieved and thanks you. You have promised something you cannot keep. As a manager, you have to report a disability-related comment by a supervisor, and talking to the supervisor yourself is the start of an informal investigation. Before he leaves, you realize the promise and the duty cannot both stand.
    Narrator: Wanting to protect him and fix it fast is a caring instinct. Under Policy 1436, managers and supervisors immediately report protected-class allegations to human resources or the agency Affirmative Action Officer and follow agency procedures. A private talk cannot replace that, or come first.
    Narrator: If the informal talk goes badly, his supervisor will know exactly who spoke. He trusted your promise to manage that risk; the formal route exists partly to manage it better.
  Choice: Suggest a conversation with him and his supervisor together this week, so each can hear the other, and decide afterward whether the comment needs to go to human resources at all.
    What happens: He hesitates and says, “With him in the room?” The suggestion puts a temporary staff member across the table from the person who controls his next assignment, as the price of being heard. It also makes a repair conversation the gate to a report you are required to make immediately.
    Narrator: Repair may matter later. It cannot come first, and it cannot decide whether a required report happens. The stronger move reports now and asks him what he needs.
    Narrator: To him, this offer means facing his supervisor before anyone with authority to act has heard what happened.
  Choice: Before deciding anything, quietly ask two people on the team whether they heard the joke, so you know what actually happened and can tell human resources something solid.
    What happens: You tell him you plan to ask a couple of people what they heard. He goes still. Asking teammates is the start of an investigation that is not yours to run. It can spread private information, weaken a fair process and tell the team who spoke up.
    Narrator: Wanting solid facts is reasonable. Gathering them belongs to the people who receive the report. A referral does not depend on whether you believe the account.
    Narrator: For him, colleagues being asked about the joke means the team learns he reported it, before any protection is in place.

A second chance
He is still sitting across from you. He asks, “So what happens now with what I told you about the comment?” Nothing has been reported or said to anyone yet.

He has asked what happens next. What do you tell him?
  Choice: Say you spoke too soon. You must report the comment to human resources immediately and will not look into it yourself. Ask what he needs in the meantime.
    What happens: You say, “I spoke too soon. I can’t keep this between us or handle it myself. I have to report it to human resources or the Equal Opportunity and Access Division now, and the people who receive it will contact you.” He is quiet, then asks whether his supervisor will know it came from him. You say you cannot promise that, that retaliation is prohibited, and that you will watch for it. He asks to sit apart from his supervisor at this week’s team meeting, and you arrange it. You make the report before lunch.
    Narrator: You took back your first answer quickly and plainly, before it shaped what he did next. Honesty about what you cannot promise is part of what makes the report usable for him. Build on it by checking in after human resources makes contact.
    Narrator: Hearing a manager take back an offer could feel like a loss. Hearing why, and what will happen instead, lets him decide his own next steps with full information.
  Choice: Keep your first answer for now, and tell him you will check back with him in two weeks and report only if the joke has happened again.
    What happens: He thanks you. The comment goes unreported for at least two weeks. If it happens again, there will be no record that a manager knew the first time. His trust rests on a promise that leaves him less protected, not more.
    Narrator: Trust matters, and it is why the honest answer matters. Checking back in two weeks sounds attentive, but a manager’s duty to report a protected-class comment does not wait two weeks, or for it to happen again.
    Narrator: Not wanting to break a confidence is a decent impulse. The duty exists so that protection does not depend on one manager’s judgment of whether a comment counts.
  Choice: Ask him whether he wants it reported. If he says no, respect that, since he knows the situation best and should stay in control of what happens to his own account.
    What happens: He says he is not sure and needs to think. Control over his own account matters, and for a coworker who had only witnessed the joke, it would come first. For a manager, though, the report is not his to waive. Leaving the choice with him puts the weight of your duty on the person with the least power in this story.
    Narrator: Asking what someone wants is the right move for a coworker who saw something. A manager’s report of a protected-class comment is a duty, not a choice to hand over. You can still ask what he needs, and tell him what to expect.
    Narrator: To him, being asked to decide may feel respectful, and also like being asked to choose whether his supervisor gets reported. That is a hard choice to give someone on a temporary appointment.

Decision
Ten days later, the corrected bulletin is out. Your division director asks what you are changing so county guidance does not repeat the error, and whether you need another position. Looking back, two things are clear: no one owned the current-source check, and an early warning about the source never reached the bulletin.

What do you change, and what do you ask for?
  Choice: Add a line in each bulletin packet for open source questions, make the current-source check one named person’s job, and review after the next two bulletins whether it catches anything.
    What happens: The change is small enough to use. On the next bulletin, the policy analyst lists two open source questions; the person checking sources answers one and sends the other to the person responsible for the requirement. When you tell the division about the change, you describe the gap in the source check and do not say who raised what. Your division director asks you to report back after the second bulletin.
    Narrator: The change fits the gap you found: no one owned the source check. It is small, testable and has a review point. Build on it by saying, after the second bulletin, what it did and did not catch.
    Narrator: For the project staff member, his warning shaped the fix without his name attached, while the human resources matter is still open. Keeping the warning out of the announcement protects him and the process.
    Narrator: When you fix a process, how do you check that the fix fits the gap, not just the worry?
  Choice: Add your own sign-off as a second review on every bulletin and guidance document from now on, so nothing reaches counties without a manager’s check, and ask for a position to cover the load.
    What happens: Guidance slows. Your review checks the draft, not the source, so it would not have caught this error. Analysts begin waiting for your sign-off before raising source doubts, since you will see the draft anyway. The position request goes to your division director with a cost and no evidence that it addresses the gap.
    Narrator: Wanting nothing to slip past again is understandable. A new approval layer on every document does not match a gap in who checks the source. A named source check does.
    Narrator: Adding oversight after an error is a common reflex for managers. It can look like accountability while moving responsibility away from the people closest to the work.
  Choice: Send all staff a reminder on Monday, signed by your division director, to double-check sources before any bulletin goes out and to speak up if something looks off.
    What happens: People read it, and the division director’s name gets their attention. The shared folder still holds the old source next to the new one, and no one owns the current-source check. The reminder asks people to speak up when something looks off, which someone already did.
    Narrator: “Be more careful” leaves the same invisible assumption in place, and a senior signature makes it louder, not more specific. The gap was in who checks the source, and a reminder does not assign it.
    Narrator: For the project staff member, being told to speak up when something looks off, after he did exactly that, may land badly.
  Choice: Ask supervisors to track each analyst’s errors in county guidance on a shared dashboard, so patterns show early and your division director can see which teams need more coaching.
    What happens: The dashboard fills slowly, because people stop mentioning small errors they catch themselves. The policy analyst has the only entry. The gap in who checks the source does not appear on the dashboard at all.
    Narrator: Counting errors by person shows who got caught, not how the work failed. Better teams often report more errors because they can talk about them; a ranking can push those reports out of sight.
    Narrator: For analysts, a shared error count turns every catch into a mark against them. The safest move becomes not mentioning what they find.

What you practiced
- Correcting a public-facing error first, then examining how it happened
- Keeping learning about an error separate from any performance question, without erasing responsibility
- Reporting a protected-class comment immediately, without investigating it yourself
- Being honest about what you cannot keep confidential, and watching for retaliation
- Matching a process change to the gap you found, with a review point
- Keeping a private concern out of a team announcement while a formal matter is open

Try this week
- Pick one piece of guidance your area sends out. Write down who checks its source, by role. If no one does, name someone.
- Find out who in human resources receives protected-class reports for your area, and save that contact where you can reach it quickly.
- At your next managers’ meeting, when an error comes up, ask first what the people affected need today.
- Tell your teams one thing that changed because someone raised a concern, after checking with that person how they want it handled.

Your action plan
- In your next meeting, when a question gets a shortcut reply, bring it back with one sentence about the work, such as who can confirm the answer.
- Before you raise a concern, write it as what you observed, how it could affect the task, and what you are asking for.
- When someone questions your work, ask one clarifying question before you explain or defend.
- When you find a mistake, handle what the people affected need now, then name one specific step to change.
- Say once a month what changed because someone spoke up, and if nothing did, say why.
- Save the current contacts for Human Resources, the Equal Opportunity and Access Division, a union representative and the Employee Assistance Program.

Narrator: You started with a small reply: “We have already discussed that.” Every part of this course came back to what a reply like that teaches. Psychological safety is not comfort, and it is not lower standards. It is a shared expectation that a question, a mistake or a disagreement will be examined, not ridiculed or punished. You build it in small moves that other people can see. Ask a precise question. Receive one about your own work without defending first. Handle a mistake in the present, then learn from it. Lead the repair when you hold more power. Use the formal route when a concern needs one. The response to the first person who speaks, especially from a leader, shapes whether anyone else will. Go back to the moment you brought to mind at the start. What would you do differently now? Start there.
