Cultural Humility: Learning what something means to the person, examining your own reading, and minding the influence your role carries

Welcome and why it matters

Narrator: Welcome. You already know a lot about your work. You know the programs, the forms, the steps and the people you work beside. That knowledge matters, and nothing in this course asks you to set it aside. This course is about the moment when your knowledge meets another person's experience, and the two do not quite match. Knowing a service well does not mean knowing what it means to every person who uses it. Cultural humility helps you keep that difference in view. It means learning what something means to the person in front of you. It means looking honestly at the interpretation you bring. And it means paying attention to the influence your public-service role carries. You will look at what that involves, try a few practices, and then step into a workplace situation where your choices shape what happens next.

Narrator: Here is a small example that shows up in several of the readings behind this course. Staff send a letter with the correct building address. A person arrives and walks around the building, unable to tell which door is the public entrance. Staff check the address. It is right. The person's experience is also real. Both accounts matter, and they answer different questions. The address answers where the building is. The person's account answers whether someone new can actually get in. When the reply is, “The directions are complete,” the person's knowledge gets set aside. Nothing dramatic happened. No one meant harm. Yet if unclear routes are always called complete, insiders get in more easily. People meeting the process for the first time do not. Small exchanges like this one, repeated, shape who a public service works for.

Narrator: Counseling psychologist Joshua Hook and three colleagues have studied humility in helping relationships. They describe two sides. The inward side is an accurate view of yourself, including your limits. The outward side is focusing on the other person with curiosity. In their research, when clients rated their therapist as culturally humble, that rating predicted a stronger working relationship and better outcomes. Two cautions come with that finding. Most of the evidence comes from therapy and relies on what clients report about their experience. Using it in case management, benefits work or policy work takes some adaptation. The reading also notes a criticism some people raise: a focus on personal humility can play down inequities built into systems. It works best paired with efforts at the system level. Keep both points in mind as you go.

Narrator: Here is the path ahead. First, what cultural humility is and is not, as the readings define it. Then you turn toward your own assumptions, the meanings you add without noticing. Next come practices for curiosity, listening and respect. After that, what to do when it goes wrong, because sometimes it will. Then you look at how humility fits the structured tools and real duties in DHS work. Each part ends with a few questions to think over, alone or with a colleague you trust. Before you go on, bring one real situation from your own work to mind. A conversation, a letter, a meeting. Keep that question within easy reach as you continue. Let it travel with you through each of the remaining segments.

What cultural humility is and is not

Narrator: The idea of cultural humility comes from a 1998 paper by Tervalon and Murray-Garcia, written for medical education. They described three commitments. First, lifelong self-examination and self-critique: continuing to look at how your own learned expectations shape what you see. Second, attention to power: noticing the imbalance between the professional and the person being served. Third, partnership that benefits both sides and does not decide things for people. Their setting was doctors and patients. Your setting is different, and the readings behind this course are careful about that. They borrow the three ideas, not the clinical procedures. Self-examination, power and partnership can guide how you answer a call, write a notice, run a meeting or review a policy. They do not turn into a legal rule or a step-by-step procedure for every DHS program.

Narrator: One reading uses a story about two friends getting ready for a move. The first friend learned that accepting practical help shows trust and shared care. The second friend learned that making arrangements yourself shows responsibility and avoids imposing on others. When the second friend declines an offer to help pack, the first friend's first thought is, “My friend does not value our friendship.” The second friend has not said that. Neither meaning needs to be tied to a nationality, a family type or a group. The second friend can value the friendship and still decline this one offer. Knowing their own way of caring cannot tell the first friend what the second friend intended. A better next step is a question: “Would help feel useful, or would you rather make the arrangements yourself?” That is the heart of the practice. You keep your first reading open long enough for the other person's meaning to reach you.

Narrator: Cultural humility is easy to misread, so it helps to say what it is not. It is not just being modest, or saying you do not know everything. It asks you to look at how your expectations affect an exchange, and whether another person's view can actually change your response. It is not giving up your expertise. A community brief may explain useful history. Your role may carry knowledge people need. Neither one gives you complete knowledge of a person's priorities. It is not agreeing to every request. You can understand what someone is asking, explain a real limit, and look for an alternative, all without promising something you cannot provide. And it does not set aside your real duties. Humility improves the basis for your response. It does not replace decisions that belong to the person or area responsible for them.

Narrator: You may have heard cultural humility set against cultural competence, as if one knows everything and the other knows nothing. The readings push back on that. The 1998 authors challenged the idea of competence as finished mastery. Hook and his colleagues go further and call mastering facts about groups a goal no one can reach. Yet the National Center for Cultural Competence describes its own framework as developmental, including self-assessment and adaptation as well as knowledge. So the two need not be rivals. One reading shows how they fit. A person researches an old bowl that a friend inherited. The researcher learns its period, its materials and how such bowls were often used. That is useful. Then the researcher assumes its owner should keep it on a shelf, untouched. The owner explains that using it carefully every day keeps a loved person present. History and the owner's meaning answer different questions. The researcher needs both.

Narrator: Hook and his colleagues put it simply: cultural humility is a stance, not a credential. You do not finish it. Finishing this course will not mean you understand everyone's culture, and no label like humble or competent tells you what someone actually understood or did. A specific account says far more. The researcher learned the bowl's history and reconsidered an assumption about the bowl's owner. That leaves room for the next question. The same is true of you. Each conversation can clarify one interpretation and change one response. That is real progress. It is not mastery, and it does not need to be. Hold on to one line from the readings as you go: knowledge informs the question, it does not answer for the individual. Your experience helps you ask better questions. The person still holds the answer about their own life.

Looking at your own assumptions

Narrator: Compare two sentences. “The form was not returned.” That is an observation. “The person is not interested.” That adds a reason. An unfinished step can have many explanations. The person may have misunderstood. There may be a practical barrier. They may disagree, or their plans may have changed, or something else entirely is going on. A sound judgment keeps three things apart: the facts, the possible explanations, and the information you would need to choose a response. This is not about doubting everything. It is about noticing the moment your mind supplies a reason, and asking whether the person supplied it or you did.

Narrator: A public-service version comes from the readings. A staff member has prepared a clear explanation of two appointment options. The person he is serving listens and asks, “Can I compare the arrangements with someone I trust before I choose?” The staff member has a complete answer ready, and takes the question as reluctance to decide. She has not said that. She asked about chosen advice. He added the motive. If he believes she is reluctant, his next move is pressure to decide now. If he keeps her actual words in front of him, his next move is a question: which part would you like to compare? Then he explains what the appointment process actually allows. Same person, same moment. The added meaning changes the whole response.

Narrator: Why did reluctance feel like the natural reading? The story goes further. The staff member remembers a school debate group where you prepared a complete position before inviting questions. Doing the work first showed respect for the audience. A colleague of his learned something different in a community orchestra. Members asked each other what a passage meant before deciding how to play it. An open question showed responsibility for a shared result. Both are ways of acting responsibly. Then the person he is serving adds, “I want to ask my friend about travel time. I still want to tell you which route I choose.” Now the staff member can see that talking it through may prepare her own decision rather than delay it. The colleague's account belongs to that colleague. It is not a rule for a nationality, a family or all musicians. Learned meanings come from experience within its setting. They are not predicted from someone's identity.

Narrator: Looking at your own assumptions can feel uncomfortable, so it helps to know what this is not. Go back to the two friends. The first friend heard the second friend's refusal as distance, and remembers neighbors sharing tasks during a hard time. Accepting help meant belonging. That memory explains the reaction. It does not establish what the second friend owes. The first friend has three paths. Self-blame says the value is foolish and tries to suppress it. That swaps one rigid judgment for another. Self-defense lists reasons why help is good and concludes the second friend should accept it. That leaves the mistaken inference untouched. Self-examination notices the link between help and belonging, then asks whether it fits this refusal. A value is not an entitlement. The first friend can keep caring about mutual help and still change how it is offered. Self-critique examines your reasoning. It does not ask for shame, and it does not ask you to tell anyone your history.

Narrator: Assumptions also hide inside good intentions. In one reading, staff invite family to a planning conversation because family involvement is usually helpful. The person asks to speak privately. The request tells you something real: the arrangement the person wants. It does not tell you why, and you do not need to fill that gap with a cultural explanation. The person also does not owe you a cultural biography to justify asking. Someone can value family and still want to discuss one issue alone. A value is not a fixed instruction for every situation. Circumstances and preferences change. So ask about the present purpose, the chosen support and the arrangement the person wants, and let the answer shape what you offer.

Narrator: Here is a practice you can use anywhere. Take a label that comes easily. One reading offers three: declined help, quiet in the meeting, asked about the instruction. Notice how much narrower each one is than the label it tends to grow into. Quiet in the meeting is narrower than not committed. Asked about the instruction is narrower than does not understand the service. Write down what you actually observed. Name one assumption you could reconsider. Then write a question that could change it. Finally, trace what might happen if the wider label stayed. Would it end up in a note someone else reads? Would it mean fewer choices offered next time? You can do this in a minute, in your head, before you hit send. The point is to keep room for evidence that does not fit your first account.

    Narrator: You put the actual request back at the center. From there he can ask what she wants to compare and explain what the process allows. Try spotting the same gap in your own next exchange.
    Narrator: When did you last hear a question as hesitation, and what did the person actually ask?
  Which cultural or family background might explain her hesitation, so he can respond in a way that fits what people like her usually expect.
    Narrator: A group explanation predicts her meaning without her. Examining his own inference lets him respond to what she actually said.
    Narrator: She asked a practical question. Being explained by a group label could leave her feeling unheard as a person.
  Whether his explanation of the two options was clear and complete enough, since a gap in what he said could be why she wants a second opinion.
    Narrator: Good preparation can make an answer feel complete. Being prepared is valuable. It still cannot tell him which question remains open for her.

Curiosity, listening and respect in practice

Narrator: Curiosity is at the center of cultural humility, but not every question is curious in a useful way. Compare two questions from the readings. One asks, “Why are people from your community reluctant?” The other asks, “What part is difficult to use?” The first assigns a group explanation before the person speaks. The second points at the actual task and invites the person's own account. Professional curiosity is strongest when it has a clear purpose. A question about someone's whole cultural history may be interesting to you and still be unnecessary for the task. Before you ask, try a quick test. What will I do with the answer? If you cannot say, the question may be serving your interest more than the person's need.

Narrator: Curiosity can also become a burden. In the readings, the staff member wants to understand the person he is serving better, so he asks about the family tradition behind seeking advice. She answers, “I would rather just compare the travel arrangements.” That is not a refusal of help or a sign of distrust. It tells him which question she wants answered now. So he goes back to the travel times and explains what the appointment process allows. If she later chooses to share more, he can listen, without assigning her account to everyone with a similar background. Practical help must never depend on someone sharing a personal story. And no one should be treated as the spokesperson for a culture. Hearing one person's account can improve your understanding of the moment. It does not tell you what a whole group wants.

Narrator: What about background reading? Community briefs can help. They introduce history, language and experiences with institutions that you might otherwise miss. The risk is using a brief as the answer. One reading names the trap directly: a claim about “how Hmong families are” turns background information into a stereotype. Reading that some communities value family involvement in planning does not tell you whom this person wants involved, what they wish to share, or whether the meeting arrangements work for them. So turn the background into open questions. Then listen for what fits, what differs and what is missing. Watch the premise in your wording too. In the bowl example, the researcher is tempted to ask the bowl's owner why she is not following the proper custom. That question already treats a pattern as a rule. Asking what matters to her about everyday use leaves her answer open.

Narrator: Hook and his colleagues add a useful idea. They call it cultural opportunities: moments in a conversation when someone's identity or background comes to the front. Noticing those moments, and choosing to explore them with care, is a skill people can learn. The same authors note that anxiety reduces curiosity. So part of the work is building comfort with these conversations, through practice and reflection rather than lectures. They also stress that no single identity explains a person. Someone is never only their language, their age, their faith or their disability. When an opening appears, ask one relevant question, and make it easy to decline: the person chooses what to share.

Narrator: Listening and sharing influence are not the same thing. Go back to the two friends. The first friend owns the vehicle for the move and proposes leaving early. The second friend says the timing makes an important goodbye hard. The first friend says, “I understand,” and changes nothing. Listening happened. Influence did not. The first friend may have a real limit: the vehicle must be returned that afternoon. Humility does not mean promising an impossible schedule. It means separating the real limit from a preferred departure time, then exploring what is actually possible. Work has the same pattern. A supervisor announces an assignment, then asks the employee for ideas. The employee cannot tell what is still open. A clearer approach says what is decided, what remains open, and how a concern could affect the next step. Honest limits protect trust better than an invitation that cannot change anything.

Narrator: Finally, notice how your role shapes the encounter. Staff often choose the questions, the order of an explanation, the words in a record and the route a request takes. That is influence, even in an ordinary exchange. In the readings, the staff member writes a short summary for the staff who handle appointments. His first draft says, “The person has not made a decision.” That is accurate as far as it goes, but it leaves out her actual question. The next reader may simply send reminders to choose. His revision says she wants to compare travel conditions with someone she trusts before choosing, and that the allowed arrangements still need checking. Cultural humility asks you to look at that kind of influence, not give up the responsibility attached to it. The way you carry someone's question forward can keep their meaning or quietly lose it.

    Narrator: A line that works here: “Sure. Which travel times would be most helpful to look at together?”

When it goes wrong and how to recover

Narrator: The research says something plainly. You will miss things. Hook and his colleagues write that missed cultural opportunities and microaggressions will happen. What matters is what comes next. They name repairing those moments without defensiveness as a core skill. They also found that people judge their own humility poorly. You cannot see your blind spots by looking harder at yourself. That is why feedback from the people you serve, your peers and your supervisor matters so much. So the skill to build is recovering well.

Narrator: A correction gives you information your first interpretation missed. You might hear it at work as, “That is not why I asked,” or, “The explanation missed what matters to me.” In the friendship story, the second friend says, “I appreciated the offer. I wanted to manage the packing because accepting help would make me worry about owing you time you may need.” The first friend's rejection story now has clear contrary information. Look at how the first friend can respond: “I heard the refusal as distance, and I understand that was not what you meant.” That names the specific inference. It does not apologize for a whole identity. It does not ask the second friend for comfort. A further offer can stay open, with the second friend free to say no. A correction is about this person and this moment.

Narrator: Repairs tend to go sideways in three familiar ways. The first is defending your intention. You explain at length that you meant well. That may be true, but it does not address what you got wrong, and it can ask the other person to manage your feelings. The second is asking for reassurance. You need to hear that you are still a good person. That gives the other person more work at the very moment they told you something hard. The third is declaring it fixed. You apologized, so it must be resolved. But an apology is an action you took. Its effect on the other person is something you learn from them, over time. A sound repair is smaller and more specific. Name what you misunderstood. Ask whether your new understanding is accurate. Change what you can. Then leave the outcome open.

Narrator: Sometimes the hardest part of a repair is that the first interpretation has already traveled. One reading describes a service note that calls a person unwilling to involve others, when the person had only asked for one private conversation. That broad description could quietly reduce the support offered later. The same thing can happen at work. A newcomer who sent written comments instead of speaking in a meeting gets described as not committed, and that judgment shapes the assignments they are offered. So after a correction, ask where your account went. Is it in a note, a summary, a planning discussion or someone's memory of a meeting? If it is, a private change of mind will not undo its effects. Address it through the responsibility you have, keep the correction specific, and respect privacy.

Narrator: One boundary needs to be clear. An ordinary misunderstanding and a formal concern are not the same thing. If someone alleges harassment or discrimination based on a protected characteristic, a reflective conversation or a private apology is not enough on its own, and it must never delay the required process. Under Minnesota Management and Budget Policy 1436, managers and supervisors report those allegations or incidents immediately to HR or the agency Affirmative Action Officer and follow agency procedures. They do not wait for proof, for a group discussion or for their own reflection to finish. The readings give a wider rule as well. When a right, a complaint, a labor process, a safety duty or Tribal consultation is involved, contact the person or area responsible and follow its required process. Humility can shape how respectfully you respond. It never delays or replaces the duty itself.

Narrator: After a misstep, a common reaction is to pull back. If asking caused trouble, maybe it is safer not to ask at all. In the friendship story, the first friend considers quietly withdrawing and never raising the offer again. That avoids an awkward moment, but it leaves the rejection story unexamined. Avoidance keeps the existing default untouched. Remember the finding that anxiety reduces curiosity. The answer is not to force yourself into hard conversations all at once. It is to build comfort step by step. Ask one relevant question with permission to decline. Notice one opening and respond to it. Each small, honest attempt makes the next one easier.

Question: A colleague tells you, “That is not why I asked for the meeting to be moved.” You had told the team it was about avoiding a tough topic. What is the strongest next move?
  Name the specific assumption you made, ask whether you now understand it correctly, and correct what you told the team.
    Narrator: You named the inference, checked your new understanding and followed it to where it traveled. That last step is the one people most often skip. Keep doing it.
    Narrator: Words that fit: “I assumed it was about the topic, and I was wrong. I will let the team know that was my guess, not your reason.”
  Explain that you were only trying to help the team understand the change, so your colleague knows you meant well and were not criticizing them.
    Narrator: Your intention may well have been good. Opening with it, though, keeps the focus on you. Naming the assumption first addresses what your colleague actually raised.
    Narrator: Your colleague may now be wondering what the rest of the team believes about them. That question matters more to them than your intent.
  Apologize warmly and sincerely, thank your colleague for telling you, and let the matter rest so it does not become a bigger deal for them.
    Narrator: Wanting a clean ending after an awkward moment is very human. An apology is a good start. The team still holds the wrong reason unless you correct it.
    Narrator: Where did your first explanation go, and who still believes it?

Reflect: When someone last corrected your reading of them, what did you do in the first ten seconds?
Reflect: Which of the three sideways repairs, defending, seeking reassurance or declaring it fixed, do you fall into most?
Reflect: Who could give you honest feedback about how you come across across differences, and what would make it easy for them?

Bringing it into your work

Narrator: One reading describes a worker who refuses the Equity Analysis Toolkit because, as he puts it, humility is not a form. The readings disagree. Both are true at once. Learning about a community does not tell you what an individual wants, so you still need the conversation. And a structured process does a different job: it explains how a decision was made, whose experience informed it, and what benefits and burdens were weighed. A team can complete every field and still miss an important perspective. A team can also be very curious and never follow through. So use the analysis process that applies to the decision, and while you do, ask the people affected what the available information misses. One line from the readings sums it up: a fact sheet is not a person, and a blank analysis is not humility.

Narrator: Checklists can support humility when the evidence behind each answer is examined. Here is a practice case from the readings. A team reviewing a service marks interpreter services as available, because a contract is in place. But no one has checked how people learn about the service, how requests get arranged, or what happens when an interpreter is not available. A contract establishes an arrangement. It does not show that a person could get interpretation that was timely and useful. The strong response is not to throw out the checklist. The strong response keeps the contract information, names the access questions that are still unanswered, and asks the people responsible for the service how those conditions are checked. The same goes for organizational self-assessments from the National Center for Cultural Competence. They help you examine conditions. They do not rate any one employee.

Narrator: In one reading, a community member tells a staff member that a proposed instruction describes the route accurately but never says how to recognize the public entrance. The community member also describes the feeling of a service hidden behind insider knowledge. The staff member can learn from both. Partnership means asking which part should be clearer, explaining what staff can change, and offering the community member a chance to review the revision if they want one. It does not mean asking them to rewrite every letter or to speak for all service users. It also does not mean asking for a thank-you on an unchanged draft. That turns participation into endorsement. If staff hold the decision, say so honestly. Repeated invitations that collect stories without considering them use up people's time while the barrier stays in place.

Narrator: Openness also needs capacity to matter. In a practice orientation from the readings, the coordinator tells newcomers they can ask questions freely. One participant explains that raising an unfinished question in front of the group can feel like challenging the facilitator before understanding their purpose. The participant would welcome time to think first. The coordinator had seen quick public questions as a sign of engagement. The participant's account changes that reading, and the coordinator does not need to decide what it says about the participant's identity. The coordinator can share the discussion questions ahead of time and explain the purpose of the activity. That only works if people get the questions in time and know how to respond. A promise nobody can keep is not capacity. The same pattern runs through the employee life cycle, from hiring to onboarding to supervision.

Narrator: Keep real responsibilities clear, too. In one practice example, an employee tells a supervisor that hearing loss makes spoken briefings difficult and asks for written information. The employee never uses the words reasonable accommodation, and does not need to. Under the state's policy, whoever receives the request forwards it promptly to the person who decides. At the same time, they tell the employee who that is. Supervisors and managers do not ask for medical information or documentation. Requests for documentation belong to the agency ADA Coordinator. A learning conversation should never ask the employee to explain a diagnosis or put the adjustment to a team vote. Respect the employee's own description of what would help and let the actual process decide. The same applies to equity analysis for a real decision and to any formal process. Your reflection supports that work. It never replaces or delays it.

Narrator: Last, follow through and describe it honestly. For any concern people raise, use a simple sequence. Confirm what you heard. Identify the role that can respond. Explain what will be considered and how people will learn what happened. Then set a date to come back to it. Without that, a team can record that engagement occurred while the barrier stays untouched. When you report what changed, keep three claims apart. What you learned, such as a new understanding of someone's meaning. What you did, such as a revised letter. And what the effect was, which needs its own evidence from the people affected. No new feedback does not prove the barrier disappeared. One thank-you does not prove it works for everyone. The Hook reading asks a good question here: how could your team build feedback loops so staff learn how people actually experience them? Keep the unanswered questions visible.

Question: A service review marks interpreter services as available because a contract exists. No one has checked how people learn about it or what happens when no interpreter is free. What should the team do?

    Narrator: You kept what is known and made the gap visible. Next, add a date to come back to the answer so the question does not quietly close.
    Narrator: Try framing it as: “The contract is in place. What do people experience when they ask for an interpreter, and how would anyone know?”
  Treat the contract as enough evidence for this review, since it commits the provider to the service, and note access as a topic for next year.
    Narrator: The contract shows an arrangement exists. The stronger response asks whether people can actually use it, which is the question the checklist was pointing at.
    Narrator: For someone waiting at a counter without the words they need, a contract on file does not help unless the request works in that moment.
  Stop using the checklist for this item, since a checked box cannot prove access, and replace it with an open discussion at the next team meeting.
    Narrator: Frustration with box-checking is common and often fair. The checklist still surfaced a useful question. The fix is evidence and follow-up, not dropping the tool.
    Narrator: Which checklist in your own work has a box that deserves a closer look?

Reflect: Which structured tool in your work could do more if you asked the people affected what it misses?
Reflect: Where have you seen people asked for input with no word afterward about what happened to it?
Reflect: Pick one recent change you made. What did you learn, what did you do, and what do you still not know about its effect?

Turning a new law into a notice people can use

If you run a program, a plan crosses your desk long before anyone feels its effects. In this version you turn a new law into a notice, weigh two supervisors’ views on one budget, and report on how the launch went. Watch the difference between an arrangement on paper and help that people can actually use.

Your role: You are a Human Services Manager. You run a statewide program and supervise three supervisors. You turn legislative requirements into services, decide how staff time and budget are used, and sign off on implementation plans.

In this scenario: the call-line supervisor; the notices supervisor; the county relations supervisor; the county eligibility supervisor; your director

The legislature changed your program. In six months, everyone enrolled must get a reassessment notice 60 days before their eligibility review, and the notice must explain how to ask for help. You have a fixed amount of new money. Your implementation plan is due to your division director in three weeks. Three supervisors report to you: the call-line supervisor; the notices supervisor, who leads notices and policy; and the county relations supervisor, who works with counties and lead agencies. The plan includes an equity analysis and a language-access checklist.

Decision
In the plan review, the notices supervisor walks through the checklist. Next to “People are informed about interpreter services,” the box is checked. The note says, “Interpreter contract in place.” The call-line supervisor frowns. Call-line staff hear from people who did not know they could ask for an interpreter, he says, and some calls end before the person reaches one. The notices supervisor says the contract covers every language the program sees. Both are describing something real.

The checklist says met. Your call-line supervisor says people cannot use it. What do you do with that line before you sign the plan?
  Choice: Leave the box checked. The contract covers every language the program sees, which meets the requirement, and the call-line issue is a training question for the call-line team to handle.
    What happens: The plan is on track to reach your director on time. The call-line supervisor tells his staff to remind callers about interpreter help. The notice still says nothing about it. The notices supervisor moves on to the next line. The call-line supervisor says little for the rest of the review. He came with a real pattern from the phones, and it was filed as his team’s problem.
    Narrator: Contracts and checklists feel solid, and deadlines push toward signing. But a contract shows an arrangement, not that people can get the help. The checklist was asking a question the contract does not answer.
    Narrator: Compare this with keeping the contract information and asking how requests work in practice. That takes a week. It finds the gap before launch, not after.
  Choice: Keep the contract details, but mark the line not yet supported. Ask the call-line and notices supervisors how a request works on a real call, and name who checks by when.
    What happens: The notices supervisor is a little stung, then interested. The contract is real; no one had asked her how it works on the phones. The call-line supervisor pulls two weeks of call notes. They show that the current notice never mentions interpreter help, and that staff transfer callers to the interpreter line without explaining the wait. The county relations supervisor adds that counties field the same confusion. The plan now has a named gap, an owner and a date, instead of a checked box.
    Narrator: You kept what is known, the contract, while making the evidence gap visible. That is what a checklist is for: pointing to questions, not settling them. Build on it by asking people who call the line how the help worked for them.
    Narrator: For the notices supervisor, being told her line was wrong would have felt like blame. Being asked how it works in practice let her help find the answer.
    Narrator: Which checked boxes in your current plans rest on an arrangement, and which rest on evidence that people can use it?
  Choice: Drop the checklist from the plan. If a checked box cannot show whether people actually get help, it is not worth the time, and cutting it frees time for the work that matters.
    What happens: The notices supervisor removes the checklist. The plan is shorter. The call-line supervisor’s concern is still there, but now nothing in the plan asks about language access at all. The question that might have led your team to the notice wording leaves with the form.
    Narrator: You saw the limit of a checked box, which is real. The stronger move keeps the tool and asks for the evidence behind it. A checklist can still point to a useful question.
    Narrator: Next time, ask: “What would show that this line is true for the people calling?” Keep the line open until someone can answer.
  Choice: Act on the call-line concern now. Direct the notices supervisor to translate the notice into ten more languages and add a second interpreter vendor before launch, without waiting for more information.
    What happens: The notices supervisor starts the translation work, which would use most of the new money. The call-line supervisor points out that what his staff hear most is not about the notice language. Callers do not know they can ask for an interpreter, and transfers end without explanation. The money is close to committed before anyone has checked what the barrier is.
    Narrator: Acting fast on an access concern comes from a good place. But a change made before the concern is examined can miss what people actually face.
    Narrator: A stronger move asks the call-line supervisor what the call notes show first. The evidence may support translation, or something closer to the barrier and less costly.

Decision
Now the budget. The new money covers one of two options. Option one adds call-line staff for the months around review dates. Option two rewrites the notice in plain language, adds a line about free interpreter help, and tests the draft with people who receive it. The notices supervisor would add one line about interpreter help and keep the rest of the notice. “The same words for everyone is fair,” she says. “Consistency protects people.” The call-line supervisor wants staff. The law requires certain content and the 60-day timing. It does not require the current wording.

Your supervisors disagree, and the money covers one option. How do you make the call?
  Choice: Separate what the law requires from habit. Honor the notices supervisor’s value of consistency, ask whether the same words give people the same chance to act, and say how you will decide.
    What happens: You list the legal requirements on the whiteboard: certain content, 60-day timing, how to ask for help. Everything else is open. You tell the notices supervisor that consistency matters, and ask whether the current wording, full of terms only staff use, gives people an equal chance to act. She looks at the call notes and says, “Probably not.” The county relations supervisor suggests testing a draft with county workers and a few people who get the notice. You choose the rewrite, keep a small amount for peak staffing, and explain your reasons to both supervisors. The call-line supervisor is disappointed. He also knows his evidence shaped the choice.
    Narrator: You separated a real requirement from an avoidable habit, and you treated the notices supervisor’s value as worth keeping, not wrong. That let her reconsider without losing face. Build on it by telling the team how the test results could change the plan.
    Narrator: To the notices supervisor, consistency is a way to protect people from unfair treatment. Asking whether equal wording gives equal access respects that value and widens it.
    Narrator: Which parts of your program’s routines are required, and which are habits that feel required because they are familiar?
  Choice: Choose the notice rewrite now. The call notes already point clearly to the notice, so there is no need to reopen the debate with the notices supervisor or spend another meeting on it.
    What happens: The rewrite is funded. The notices supervisor carries it out, but she still believes the old wording was fairer, and she drops the test with readers to save time. The new notice reads better to staff. No one knows yet whether it reads better to the people who get it.
    Narrator: The evidence did point to the notice. Skipping the notices supervisor’s value left her carrying out a change she did not believe in, and the test that would show the effect fell away.
    Narrator: To the notices supervisor, consistency is a way to protect people from unfair treatment. Being skipped told her that value did not count, so she had little reason to protect the parts of the plan she did not choose.
    Narrator: When the evidence seems to settle a choice, whose reasons do you still need to hear before you decide?
  Choice: Go with the notices supervisor’s view. She leads notices and policy, the notice is her area, and she knows the wording rules better than anyone else at the table.
    What happens: The notices supervisor adds the interpreter line and keeps the rest of the notice as is, and the money goes to call-line staff. The call-line supervisor notices how the choice was made: the call notes about confusing terms never came up. He takes it as a sign that call-line knowledge does not count in policy choices. In his next staff meeting, he stops raising caller patterns. The disagreement is settled, and an important source of knowledge goes quiet.
    Narrator: To the call-line supervisor, deferring to the policy lead says that role decides whose knowledge counts. If only expert accounts count, barriers that front-line staff see can stay outside decisions.
    Narrator: Relying on the person whose area it is can feel like good delegation. It works best when that person’s view has been tested against what others know.
  Choice: Split the money evenly between the two options, so neither supervisor feels overruled and both of their concerns get at least some of the new funding this year.
    What happens: Each supervisor gets half. Half is not enough to staff the peak months or to test a new notice. The notices supervisor makes small wording edits without testing them. The call-line supervisor adds one part-time worker. Neither change is big enough to tell whether it helped. The decision avoided the conflict, and the access question with it.
    Narrator: Splitting the difference avoids choosing, but it also avoids asking which option meets the barrier. A clear decision with stated reasons respects both supervisors more than a split that serves neither.
    Narrator: Next time, try: “Here is what the law requires. Here is what the evidence shows. Here is my decision and why. Tell me what I might be missing.”

A second chance
After the meeting, the call-line supervisor stops by with a printout of call notes. One reads: “Caller asked if someone could explain the letter in her language. Did not know to ask for an interpreter. Transfer dropped.” Another: “Caller’s son interpreted. Unsure of review date.” He says, “This is what the box doesn’t show.”

You have the plan in front of you and new information from the call line. What do you do now?
  Choice: Reopen the language-access line. Ask the call-line and notices supervisors to trace one call together, from notice to interpreter, and name who checks the fix by when.
    What happens: The two supervisors spend an afternoon walking through a single call. They find that the notice says nothing about free interpreter help, and that transfers have no warm handoff. They also flag the call where a caller’s son interpreted: if the notice and the call line made interpreter help clear, no one would need to rely on family for a review date. The plan now names two fixes, who owns each, and a date to check whether callers reach help. The box stays unchecked until then.
    Narrator: You let a concrete account change a plan you had settled. Tracing one real path is often faster than debating the whole system. Next, decide how you will hear from callers after the change.
    Narrator: For the call-line supervisor, bringing the notes was a risk. Seeing them change the plan tells him it was worth it.
  Choice: Thank the call-line supervisor for the notes and add a line to the plan: “Call-line staff report some confusion; monitor after launch.” That records the concern without holding up the deadline.
    What happens: The line is accurate and asks nothing of anyone. No one owns the monitoring, and no date is set. The call-line supervisor files the printout. The dropped transfers will keep happening after launch, now with a note in the plan saying the agency knew.
    Narrator: A note that names a concern without an owner or a date hears it without acting on it. Naming who will check, what, and when turns it into follow-through.
    Narrator: Try asking both supervisors: “Can the two of you own this? What would you need to check it by the end of next month?”
  Choice: Ask the call-line supervisor which caller groups seem most confused, so the plan can target outreach and translated materials to those communities and make the most of a tight budget.
    What happens: The call-line supervisor hesitates. The notes show individual callers who did not know they could ask for help. They do not show that a group is confused. A fix built on a guess about which communities struggle would miss the people the guess leaves out, and it would not touch the notice or the transfers.
    Narrator: Wanting to target effort makes sense with a tight budget. But a few individual accounts do not make a group profile. These notes point to a process problem: the notice and the transfer.
    Narrator: When you hear a few accounts, how quickly do you turn them into a statement about a group?

Decision
Four months after launch, your director asks for an update. The notices supervisor’s draft report says, “No complaints received. Notice rollout successful.” The same week, the county relations supervisor forwards an email from a county eligibility supervisor. Her workers still get calls from people who do not understand what the notice asks them to do. She writes, “Maybe it’s just our county.”

The report says success. A county partner says otherwise. What goes to your director?
  Choice: Report what was done and what is still unknown. Ask the county eligibility supervisor what her workers hear, ask for call-line patterns, and say when she will hear back.
    What happens: Your update says the notice went out on time, no formal complaints were filed, and early county reports suggest some people still do not understand the next step. It names a check: the call-line team will sample calls, and the county relations supervisor will ask three counties what workers hear. Results go to your director in six weeks. You call the county eligibility supervisor yourself. She says it helps to be asked directly. Her answer points to one confusing sentence about where to send forms.
    Narrator: You kept what was done, what was heard, and what is still unknown as separate claims. No complaints is missing information, not proof. Calling the county eligibility supervisor closed the loop with the person who raised it. Build on it by telling her what changed because of her email.
    Narrator: For the county eligibility supervisor, “Maybe it’s just our county” was a way to raise a concern without seeming to complain. Your call told her a county account counts as evidence.
    Narrator: In updates to your director, try three parts: “Here is what was done. Here is what is known about the effect. Here is how and when the agency will learn more.”
  Choice: Send the notices supervisor’s report. No complaints after four months is a good sign, the notice went out on time, and one county’s email is not enough to change the story.
    What happens: Your director shares the report upward. The county eligibility supervisor does not hear back. Three months later, completed reviews drop in several counties, and staff trace it to the same unclear sentence about where to send forms. The report that said success is the record leaders relied on.
    Narrator: It is natural to trust a clean report after a hard launch. But no feedback does not show a barrier is gone. People who struggle with a notice often do not file complaints.
    Narrator: Compare this with reporting what is known and what is not, and asking the county eligibility supervisor what she sees. One county is not proof of a statewide problem. It is a reason to look.
  Choice: Add the county email to the report as an exception, so the county concern is on record, and keep the overall finding of success that the lack of complaints supports.
    What happens: The report now says success, with one county reporting issues. Your director reads it as a local problem. The county eligibility supervisor gets a thank-you email from the county relations supervisor. No one checks whether other counties see the same thing, so the confusing sentence stays in the notice.
    Narrator: Naming the county concern was better than leaving it out. Labeling it an exception decides what it means before anyone checks. A stronger update keeps the effect open and says how you will find out.
    Narrator: When one partner raises a problem, how do you decide whether it is local or a sign of something wider?
  Choice: Ask the county eligibility supervisor to survey her workers and the people they serve, and send you a full analysis before you report anything, so the update rests on real evidence.
    What happens: The county eligibility supervisor’s team is already stretched. She replies that she cannot run a survey for a state program. Your update to the director slips. The county’s knowledge reached you, and the request to do the agency’s analysis pushed it back out.
    Narrator: Wanting solid evidence before you report is responsible. But asking a partner to do your team’s analysis shifts staff work onto them.
    Narrator: Ask her one focused question, such as: “What do workers hear most often about the notice?” Then have your own team do the checking.

What you practiced
- Treating a checked box as a question that needs evidence, not as proof of access
- Telling an arrangement, like an interpreter contract, apart from whether people can use it
- Separating what a law requires from habits that grew around it
- Asking whether the same words give people the same chance to act
- Weighing front-line and county knowledge alongside policy expertise
- Reporting what was done, what is known, and what is still unknown
