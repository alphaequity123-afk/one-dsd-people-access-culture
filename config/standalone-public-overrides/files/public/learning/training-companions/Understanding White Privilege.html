Understanding White Privilege

One DSD People, Access and Culture

Foundation learning for all staff and supervisors

White privilege describes advantages associated with being perceived as white in a society where racial inequality shapes opportunities and treatment. It does not mean that every white person has an easy life or that effort is unimportant. These activities help you examine how an ordinary practice can make access easier for some people, and what a more equitable approach could involve.

For all staff, including supervisors and managers. Participation is voluntary. You may use the practice cases, reflect privately, discuss an example, or pass. No one needs to disclose personal experiences, identities, or assessment results.

1 Recognize advantage without ranking hardship

A person can work hard and still benefit from a barrier they did not have to face. Race, income, disability, family responsibilities, and other circumstances affect experience in different ways. Recognizing one kind of advantage does not settle every question about a person's life. It gives us a more specific question: does this practice create an additional racial barrier, and what evidence would help us understand it?

Consider both personal interactions and repeated arrangements. Who is assumed to belong? Whose expertise is recognized without extra proof? These are questions to investigate, not conclusions to attach to a person because of their identity.

Explore the example

Practice example: Two colleagues describe working their way through college. One also describes repeated doubts about whether they earned their place because of their race. A listener responds that everyone has struggled, so race cannot be relevant. Shared hardship is real, but it does not answer the specific concern about racial treatment.

Try it

Write a response that acknowledges both accounts without comparing whose life was harder. Then identify the specific treatment that needs examination. A useful response might begin: 'Both experiences matter. I also want to understand the additional barrier you described.' No colleague needs to supply a personal story for this activity.

Check your reasoning

What is the most useful next step in this example?

A. Ask who had the harder life before considering the concern.

B. Acknowledge both accounts and examine the particular racial barrier described.

C. Assume racial advantage explains every difference between the colleagues.

Answer B. The second response keeps the specific concern visible without ranking hardship or treating race as the only influence on a person's life.

Reflect if useful: When do I use a shared experience to connect, and when might it keep me from hearing an important difference?

2 Look at how opportunity travels

An opportunity can be formally open while reaching only a familiar circle. Invitations, informal sponsorship, and unwritten expectations affect who can take part. A repeated pattern can preserve an existing advantage even when no one explicitly announces an exclusion.

Do not assume that a small example establishes a racial disparity. Ask what is known about access, who has not been heard, and whether the same practice repeats. When reliable evidence indicates a racial pattern, examine the process that may sustain it rather than reducing the explanation to a single person's intention.

Explore the example

Practice example: A policy team shares a development opportunity during an informal gathering. Several people who rarely attend hear about it after selections are made. The team knows the invitation was uneven. It does not yet know whether the pattern differs by race, work schedule, disability, or another circumstance.

Try it

Map how someone would hear about the opportunity, understand the criteria, and express interest. Identify one step that relies on an existing relationship. Draft a clearer invitation and a consistent selection approach. Identify what further information would be needed before claiming a racial pattern; do not collect colleagues' personal identity details for a learning exercise.

Check your reasoning

Which conclusion is supported by the information available?

A. Access to the invitation was uneven; the team should examine the process and any supported patterns.

B. The example proves the racial identity of everyone who benefited.

C. An opportunity cannot be inequitable unless someone intended exclusion.

Answer A. The first response separates a known access problem from an unverified explanation. Repeated processes deserve examination even when exclusion was not intended.

Reflect if useful: Which opportunities in my work depend on knowing whom to ask?

3 Keep discussion open without putting someone on display

Learning about racial advantage can bring questions, uncertainty, or discomfort. A useful discussion examines ideas and evidence while respecting each person's choice about what to share. It does not require a colleague of color to prove the subject through a personal experience or speak for an entire community.

Listen to the concern before explaining your intention. If an action caused harm, a specific acknowledgment and a practical correction can be more useful than asking the affected person to reassure you. An educational discussion also should not decide the validity of an individual complaint; that belongs in the appropriate process.

Explore the example

Practice example: During a learning discussion, someone asks the only Black participant to tell a painful story so others will understand. The facilitator pauses the request and offers a prepared practice example instead. The participant remains free to contribute, stay quiet, or leave the discussion.

Try it

Write two sentences a facilitator could use. First, remove the expectation of disclosure. Second, return to the learning question. For example: 'No one needs to share a personal experience. Let's examine who can access the opportunity in our example and what might need to change.'

Check your reasoning

Which response supports both learning and the participant's choice?

A. Ask again because a personal story will be more convincing.

B. Stop all discussion of racial inequality.

C. Use a prepared case and let each person decide whether to contribute.

Answer C. The third response keeps the subject available while removing pressure on one person to educate the group through personal disclosure.

Reflect if useful: What helps me stay curious when a discussion challenges how I understand fairness?

4 Turn understanding into a fairer opportunity

A practical response begins with the process you can influence. You might explain selection criteria, make an invitation easier to find, examine whose ideas receive credit, or improve the way feedback reaches a decision. Ask affected people what would help without assuming that your preferred solution meets their needs.

A change should be judged by what happens after it is used. More invitations may broaden awareness, but they do not by themselves establish that selection was fair. Review the relevant steps and remain open to changing an approach that leaves a barrier in place.

Explore the example

Practice example: A team replaces informal invitations with an open notice and explains how proposals will be considered. A staff member points out that the notice still uses unexplained terms. The team revises the notice and checks whether people outside its usual network can understand how to participate.

Try it

Choose one improvement in the practice process. Explain the barrier it addresses, the part still uncertain, and what feedback would help judge its usefulness. Use the application's Equity Analysis Toolkit when a broader policy or program decision needs examination. Do not promise a change outside your own responsibility.

Check your reasoning

What would help the team learn whether its change is useful?

A. Check whether people can understand and use the invitation and examine how selection works.

B. Count posted notices and declare the problem solved.

C. Require everyone to report a personal privilege score.

Answer A. The first response examines access and the decision process. A notice count or personal score cannot establish that the opportunity became fairer.

Reflect if useful: What is one ordinary practice I can examine without making assumptions about another person's life?
