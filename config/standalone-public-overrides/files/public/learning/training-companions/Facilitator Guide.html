Facilitator Guide

One DSD People, Access and Culture

Use this guide with the three adapted training companions and their corresponding courses in the full application. It supports thoughtful discussion, clear explanations, and voluntary practice for staff and supervisors.

For all staff, including supervisors and managers. Participation is voluntary. You may use the practice cases, reflect privately, discuss an example, or pass. No one needs to disclose personal experiences, identities, or assessment results.

Use the courses together or separately

These companions support the full application's courses on white privilege, unconscious bias, and intercultural competence. Choose the subject that fits the group's question. Read the explanation before using the case, then allow people to compare responses and examine the feedback. No fixed session length or compulsory sequence is part of this guide.

The One DSD Team and consultant can review these materials together when choosing Learning Lab topics and resources for further development. Learning Labs already take place every other meeting. The Team decides what to explore and who facilitates. Decisions about developing a full Articulate 360 course for DHS Pathlore remain a shared selection process; these materials do not represent a completed Pathlore course.

Prepare for participation

Read the selected lesson and its sources. Have the practice case and answer explanation available in a readable format. Offer spoken or written participation, time to consider a question, and the option to pass. Describe any visual information needed for an activity rather than relying on an image alone.

Use the glossary to explain an unfamiliar term briefly, then return to the question. Check the actual accessibility and communication arrangements for the meeting. Do not ask someone to disclose a disability or personal history in order to choose a learning format.

Open with clear choices

A suggested opening is: 'We are here to examine how everyday decisions and communication affect people. We will use practice examples. You are welcome to ask questions, write privately, join the discussion, or pass. No one is expected to speak for a community or share a personal experience.'

Invite respectful disagreement with ideas and attention to evidence. Do not promise absolute confidentiality in a workplace discussion. Ask participants to avoid identifiable details about colleagues, community members, or service recipients. Explain any actual meeting recording or sharing arrangements before the activity; this guide does not authorize recording.

Facilitate white privilege learning

Begin with the distinction between effort and an additional barrier a person may or may not face. Use the college example to examine that distinction without ranking hardship. Then use the opportunity example to move from individual experience to a process the group can examine.

If someone asks a colleague of color to provide proof through a personal story, remove that expectation and return to the practice case. Keep the learning open to all staff. Do not assign attendance or participation by racial identity, use a public privilege score, or require a personal disclosure activity.

If someone questions the term, ask which part is unclear and explain the particular advantage or barrier under discussion. Questions are an opportunity to clarify the reasoning. Neither disagreement nor discomfort is evidence of a person's character or intercultural stage.

Facilitate unconscious bias learning

Ask participants to separate what they can observe from what they have inferred. Use the phone example instead of asking people to judge strangers from photographs. Discuss more than one plausible explanation, then identify information that could distinguish them.

In the proposal example, compare evidence against the same relevant criteria. When discussing affinity, confirmation, halo, and perception effects, examine the reasoning rather than diagnosing the person. An affinity group or a relationship is not itself proof of an unfair decision.

Use the complaint example to show how an unsupported label can become a repeated practice. Do not debate the merits of an actual complaint during the learning session. No IAT result is required, collected, or used to evaluate an employee. Older national occupational statistics are not evidence of the current DSD workforce.

Facilitate intercultural learning

Connect each example to a question about meaning. A document put aside, a pause, or a concern about a service can have several explanations. Ask what the person has said and what remains unknown before proposing an adjustment.

Use the five IDC orientations as concepts for understanding ways of approaching difference. Do not invite participants to estimate their own placement, move themselves down an imagined scale, or identify a colleague's orientation. Personal assessment and feedback are separate from these learning activities.

When the group discusses adaptation, ask what changed because a perspective was understood more fully. A helpful action by itself does not prove assessed developmental movement. Culture should inform inquiry without becoming an all-purpose explanation for language access, disability, organizational expectations, or an individual's preference.

Respond when a discussion becomes difficult

Pause a personal label or stereotype and bring the discussion back to the specific words, decision, or practice. You might say: 'Let's separate the action we can describe from the motive we are assigning.' If a participant identifies harm, acknowledge the concern and offer a choice about continuing; do not require public reconciliation or reassurance.

When a real personnel, discrimination, service, or safety concern arises, explain that the learning discussion cannot decide it. Support use of the relevant agency process without asking the group to investigate. Native American and Tribal-specific questions should be referred to the appropriate DHS or ADSA office responsible for Tribal matters; this guide does not supply that specialized instruction.

Close with a useful reflection

Invite each person to identify one question they understand differently or one practice they may want to examine. Sharing is optional. Someone may choose a personal reflection, a practice example, or an issue within their responsibilities. Do not create reporting duties, deadlines, or assignments that the group has not agreed to.

Ask for feedback on clarity, relevance, and accessibility. Treat a correct activity response as evidence of that response, not a diagnosis or a guarantee of future conduct. Connect further questions to the existing courses, Equity Analysis Toolkit, or an appropriate source of guidance.
