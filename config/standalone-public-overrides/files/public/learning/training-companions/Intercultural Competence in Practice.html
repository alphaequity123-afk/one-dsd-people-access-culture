Intercultural Competence in Practice

One DSD People, Access and Culture

Foundation learning for all staff and supervisors

Intercultural learning helps us examine our own assumptions, understand how another person makes meaning, and adjust our response thoughtfully. These activities connect that learning to communication, team decisions, policies, and services across the division. Cultural knowledge can prepare a useful question; the person and situation still matter.

For all staff, including supervisors and managers. Participation is voluntary. You may use the practice cases, reflect privately, discuss an example, or pass. No one needs to disclose personal experiences, identities, or assessment results.

1 Connect a good intention with a useful response

A helpful intention does not tell us whether a response fits another person's needs. We may offer the kind of explanation or support that works for us and assume it works for everyone. Intercultural practice asks us to examine that expectation and learn what the other person means.

In a work setting, adapting a response includes clarity about what can change. You can explain information differently, ask about communication preferences, or make a discussion easier to join while keeping relevant requirements clear. Understanding a perspective does not require agreeing with every request.

Explore the example

Practice example: A team sends a detailed written summary because the writer finds long explanations reassuring. Several readers say they cannot locate the action they need to take. The writer's intention was helpful; the format still needs attention.

Try it

Identify the writer's assumption. Draft a short opening that explains the action and offers access to detail. Ask what response format would be useful without assuming that a reader's identity explains their preference. Compare the revised response with the original purpose.

Check your reasoning

Which response best connects intention with the reader's experience?

A. Keep the message unchanged because the writer meant well.

B. Assume all readers want less information.

C. Ask what readers need to act and revise the format while preserving necessary detail.

Answer C. The third response checks whether the communication works for its audience while keeping the important information available.

Reflect if useful: What do I tend to offer because it helps me, before asking whether it helps someone else?

2 Ask about meaning without surrendering judgment

Curiosity works best when it has a relevant purpose. You do not need a person's family history or cultural identity to ask what a word means to them in the current conversation. Explain why you are asking, listen to the response, and avoid pressing for personal information that the task does not require.

Openness also includes examining your own interpretation. A person may use a familiar word differently or associate a service with an earlier experience. You can acknowledge that meaning and explain the actual program without promising eligibility, changing a requirement, or dismissing the concern.

Explore the example

Practice example: A person declines information about a service because they call it 'welfare' and do not want to apply. The staff member does not assume this reflects the beliefs of all people in the person's town, age group, or community. They ask what concerns the person has and explain the service accurately.

Try it

Write a respectful question about the concern. Then write a clear explanation using only information you would need to verify about the actual service. Leave the choice with the person. In this practice activity, do not invent eligibility rules or a guaranteed benefit.

Check your reasoning

What should guide the staff member's response?

A. The person's stated concern and accurate information about the service.

B. A general assumption about rural communities.

C. A promise that applying will produce the preferred result.

Answer A. The first response attends to this person and the actual service. A group stereotype or an unverified promise would not establish understanding.

Reflect if useful: How could I ask a useful question without making someone feel they must explain an entire culture?

3 Use cultural knowledge as a starting point

People draw on many experiences, including family, profession, language, community, and organizational habits. A reference about a cultural pattern may help you recognize a possibility. It cannot tell you why a particular person acted or what they need. Treat it as preparation for listening, not as a personal profile.

The same care applies to your own familiar practices. What a team calls normal may depend on experience that newcomers do not share. Explaining those expectations can reduce the need to guess and make disagreement easier to express.

Explore the example

Practice example: A colleague pauses when asked to disagree with a proposal in a large meeting. Someone attributes the pause to the colleague's nationality. Later, the colleague says they needed to read an attachment before responding. The group description did not explain this exchange.

Try it

List the observation, the initial interpretation, and two other explanations. Draft a question about what the colleague needs to respond. Identify one team habit that could be made clearer for everyone, such as sharing the material before asking for a decision.

Check your reasoning

How should a cultural reference be used here?

A. As proof of the colleague's reason for pausing.

B. As background that may inform a question, while checking the actual situation.

C. As a reason to stop asking the colleague for input.

Answer B. The second response allows context to inform inquiry without substituting a group description for the person's own account.

Reflect if useful: Which unwritten expectations have become invisible to me because I know the setting well?

4 Check understanding when a resource is not used

Providing a resource does not establish that it is understandable or useful. A translated document, for example, may still contain unfamiliar terms or fail to answer the person's question. Putting it aside does not establish a literacy level, a cultural belief, or a lack of interest.

Ask about the task and the person's preferred way to discuss it. When communication support is needed, use the appropriate agency arrangements. A learning example should not encourage a staff member to improvise interpretation or make promises about a service they cannot authorize.

Explore the example

Practice example: A staff member offers a translated notice about a renewal process. The person puts it aside and says they have no questions. The staff member asks whether it would help to go through the next step together and what form of explanation would be useful.

Try it

Draft a follow-up that invites a choice rather than testing the person. State what you still do not know. Explain how you would check the actual communication arrangements for the service instead of guessing what assistance is available.

Check your reasoning

What does putting the notice aside establish?

A. That the person cannot read it.

B. That the person's culture rejects written information.

C. Only that the notice was put aside; its meaning still needs to be checked.

Answer C. The third answer stays with the observation. A respectful question is needed before drawing conclusions about understanding or preference.

Reflect if useful: How can I check whether my explanation helped without making the other person feel tested?

5 Practice perspective taking and adaptation

The Intercultural Development Continuum describes five orientations: Denial overlooks cultural difference; Polarization judges it through an us-and-them view; Minimization emphasizes shared experience in ways that may hide difference; Acceptance seeks deeper understanding; Adaptation brings that understanding into a response. This is a brief introduction to the model, not a method for scoring yourself or another person.

You can examine the reasoning in a practice response without assigning an orientation to its speaker. In practice, ask what a different perspective changes about your options. An adjustment should respond to the situation and remain open to feedback.

Explore the example

Practice example: A planning group says everyone values independence, so one approach should work for all. A participant explains that, for them, choosing support from trusted people is part of independence. The group asks how its options could respect that meaning while preserving the person's own choice.

Try it

Identify what the shared word concealed. Draft an option that responds to the explanation rather than treating the group as culturally uniform. Explain why this is an example to discuss, not enough information to assign anyone an IDI result.

Check your reasoning

What can you reasonably conclude from the example?

A. A stated perspective gives the group a reason to reconsider its options.

B. The participant has reached a specific IDI score.

C. Everyone in the participant's community defines independence the same way.

Answer A. The first conclusion follows from the example. One exchange cannot establish an assessment result or a rule about an entire community.

Reflect if useful: What familiar value could carry a different practical meaning for someone else?

6 Use feedback to keep learning

A useful reflection names an assumption, an adjustment, and what you learned about its effect. You can explore your own habits privately or examine a practice example with colleagues. Neither route requires a personal assessment result. If you have an IDI development plan, you may choose learning that fits your own goals without sharing that plan.

Feedback is most useful when it describes an exchange clearly: what was said, what was understood, and what would make the next conversation more useful. Avoid turning one helpful action into a claim that a person has reached a developmental stage or mastered intercultural practice.

Explore the example

Practice example: A team shares questions before a discussion and offers a written response option. A participant says the advance questions helped them prepare, but an unexplained term still prevented a clear response. The team keeps the useful change and revises the term.

Try it

Write a short learning note about the practice case. Name what helped, what remained unclear, and what the team could try next. Connect the question to the application's intercultural continuum or cultural humility course if further learning would be useful.

Check your reasoning

Which feedback best supports continued practice?

A. You are now fully interculturally competent.

B. The advance questions helped preparation; explaining the unfamiliar term would make the next exchange clearer.

C. Anyone who still needs an explanation is at a lower stage.

Answer B. The second response is specific and usable. It describes the exchange without ranking people or claiming a completed developmental journey.

Reflect if useful: What would I like to understand more fully about my own assumptions or another perspective?
