Before You Send That Reply

Host: Have you ever opened a message and started writing a reply before you've even finished reading it? Let's walk through a moment like that. You're helping update a service notice, and a partner writes, we raised this concern last month. Why is it still here?

Co-host: I'd probably feel defensive. We've been working hard on that notice, and now it sounds as though none of that effort counts. My first instinct might be to explain how busy we've been.

Host: That's a very understandable reaction. But before you send it, what would help you find out whether something really was missed?

Co-host: I'd put the reply aside for a moment and open the comments. Was the concern about this version? Did we respond? I can feel frustrated and still check. The feeling doesn't have to make the decision for me.

Host: That's a useful starting point for emotional intelligence: noticing what you're feeling, then choosing what to do with it. You don't have to pretend to be calm. You need enough room to respond thoughtfully.

Co-host: So, the message is what happened. Frustration is what I'm feeling. They don't appreciate our work is my interpretation. Those are three different things, even though they can arrive together.

Host: Exactly. And it helps to ask, what else could explain this? The partner might be worried that someone will receive a notice and have no idea whom to call. That's worth checking, even if the message feels sharp.

Co-host: What would you actually write back? I don't want to leave them waiting while I sort out my reaction.

Host: Something simple: thanks for bringing this back to us. I'm checking your comments against the current notice. I'll get back to you this afternoon with what I find. Choose a time you can meet.

Co-host: That sounds like something I could say. It acknowledges the concern without promising a decision I can't make. And if I need longer, I should tell them before that afternoon commitment passes.

Host: Now you find the comment. The notice tells people to contact their worker, but doesn't explain what to do if they don't know who that is. Where would you go from there?

Co-host: I'd check the right contact with the responsible team, then take the proposed correction to whoever approves the notice. I'd also tell the partner, I can see why that instruction could leave someone unsure where to turn.

Host: That acknowledgment matters. You aren't claiming to know exactly how someone feels. You're showing that you understand the problem they're trying to solve.

Co-host: And I'd keep the promise small enough to deliver. I can bring the concern forward and explain the decision. I can't promise that every suggestion will be accepted or that a release will be delayed.

Host: Suppose you discover this has happened more than once. Would a better reply be enough?

Co-host: Probably not. We'd need a reliable place for comments, someone responsible for reviewing them, and a way to explain the result. A supervisor can help clear up that responsibility. Staff shouldn't have to solve every repeated problem through personal patience.

Host: Let's give listeners a moment to try this. Think about a difficult message, or use the notice we've just discussed. What happened? What are you assuming? What's one question you could check before replying?

Co-host: Then write two sentences: acknowledge the concern, and say what you'll do next. Keep the text in front of you if that helps. You can pause here and take your time.

Host: Afterward, check the result. Did the person receive an answer? Was the instruction clearer? Did the missed review get repaired? A friendly exchange is valuable, and the follow-through is what makes the help dependable.

Co-host: You don't need a perfect response every time. Notice the moment, check your interpretation, and take one useful next step. That's something you can practice in an ordinary workday.
