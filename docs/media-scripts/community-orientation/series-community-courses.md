## Scene 1: What these courses and pages are for (0:00–0:35)
**Visual:** A map of Minnesota with no labels, then the Minnesota Communities page on a laptop screen. Title card reads "Community courses and pages." No real logos or real people.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** The Minnesota Communities section has a page for each of more than forty communities, from Somali, Hmong and Karen Minnesota to rural Minnesota and Deaf Minnesota. Most pages also have a course in the Cultural Intelligence series. Both are written for one purpose: to help you prepare for working with a person and their family. They are a starting point you test with the person, not a description of them.

## Scene 2: A moment at work (0:35–1:15)
**Visual:** Two colleagues at a desk. A referral form is on screen; the country field reads "Eritrea."
**{{NEW_COLLEAGUE}}:** I have a first visit next week with a family from Eritrea. Should I book an Amharic interpreter? That's what we used for the last family from that region.
**{{CASE_MANAGER}}:** I wouldn't guess from the country. Eritrea has several languages, and the one this family uses at home may not be Amharic. Ask the family which language they want, and book that one.
**{{NEW_COLLEAGUE}}:** And the community page?
**{{CASE_MANAGER}}:** Read it before the visit so you know what to ask. Then let the family tell you who they are.

## Scene 3: How a page and a course fit together (1:15–2:00)
**Visual:** A community page scrolls past headings read aloud: "History and place," "Culture and everyday life," "Services, opportunity, and participation." Then a course card with its lesson list.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** Each community page groups its reading into history and place, culture and everyday life, and services, opportunity and participation. Counts are dated and sourced, because population numbers change and different sources measure different things. A page also names the other communities a person might belong to instead, because a shared language, region or faith is not a shared identity. The matching course turns the reading into short lessons with decisions, practice and a job aid. Community courses usually take between one and two hours, and you can stop and return.

## Scene 4: Using them well (2:00–2:40)
**Visual:** A short checklist appears, each line read aloud: "Ask, then listen." "Book the language the person names." "Do not ask a colleague to speak for a community."
**{{NARRATOR}}:** Ask, then listen, and treat what the person tells you as more current than any page. Book the language the person names, through a qualified interpreter. Do not ask a colleague to speak for a community because of their background. Pages about the Tribal Nations of Minnesota work differently: they point you to the Nations' own sources rather than describing them. Keep any reflection notes in your own document, without naming coworkers or the people you serve.

## Closing (2:40–3:05)
**Visual:** The Minnesota Communities index, with the search box highlighted.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** These courses are voluntary and do not replace required training. Decisions about eligibility, accommodation or civil rights stay with the responsible office. When you are ready, open the page for the community you are preparing to work with, then its course.
