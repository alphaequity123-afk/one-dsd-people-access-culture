# Video script: Receiving Feedback About Bias and Making a Useful Repair

- **Status:** Script only, written for the recurring cast (docs/media-scripts/CAST.md). Not recorded.
- **Publish on:** /learning/self-directed/index.html?course=I08
- **Length:** about 1.5 minutes (about 204 spoken words)
- **Cast:** Narrator (C3, Host voice), Supervisor (C6, Co-host voice), Case manager (C5, Host voice) (see docs/media-scripts/CAST.md)
- **Accessibility:** captions and a full transcript are required before publishing (docs/ACCESSIBILITY-STANDARD.md). On-screen text must also be spoken. Describe any visual that carries meaning.

## Scene 1: Opening (0:00–0:21)
**Visual:** A title card reads "Receiving Feedback About Bias and Making a Useful Repair." The narrator (C3) stands beside it in a quiet office.
**Narrator (C3):** Hearing that something you wrote or decided may have carried bias is uncomfortable, and that discomfort can pull a conversation toward defending yourself. Receiving Feedback About Bias and Making a Useful Repair prepares you for that moment, when a specific concern needs your attention before your reputation does.

## Scene 2: A question about feedback (0:21–0:59)
**Visual:** The case manager (C5) stands at the open door of the supervisor's office. The supervisor (C6) is at the desk.
**Case manager (C5):** Can I raise something about my review? My write-up was marked down for being too long. Others on the team turned in work of about the same length, and their feedback didn't mention it.
**Supervisor (C6):** Okay. That's not easy to hear. I've always tried to hold everyone to the same standard.
**Case manager (C5):** I'm not questioning that you try. I'm asking about this feedback.
**Visual:** The supervisor (C6) sets down a pen and pauses.
**Narrator (C3):** The concern is uncomfortable for the supervisor. The course stops here and asks what the most useful next response would be.

## Closing (0:59–1:30)
**Visual:** The narrator (C3) on camera in the quiet office.
**Narrator (C3):** The course offers practice in clarifying an action and its effect without cross-examining the person who raised it, and in offering an apology and a practical change that fit what happened. It also treats recurring feedback as a reason to examine practice, while privacy and formal duties stay intact. A question to take with you: if someone raised a concern about your work tomorrow, what would you want your first sentence to be about?
