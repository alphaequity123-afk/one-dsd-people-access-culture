# Video script: Checking Bias When the Evidence Is Incomplete

- **Status:** Script only, written for the recurring cast (docs/media-scripts/CAST.md). Not recorded.
- **Publish on:** /learning/self-directed/index.html?course=I05
- **Length:** about 1.5 minutes (about 197 spoken words)
- **Cast:** Narrator (C3, Host voice), Supervisor (C6, Co-host voice), Program manager (C2, Host voice) (see docs/media-scripts/CAST.md)
- **Accessibility:** captions and a full transcript are required before publishing (docs/ACCESSIBILITY-STANDARD.md). On-screen text must also be spoken. Describe any visual that carries meaning.

## Scene 1: Opening (0:00–0:21)
**Visual:** A title card reads "Checking Bias When the Evidence Is Incomplete." The narrator (C3) stands beside it in a plain office.
**Narrator (C3):** Most decisions at work are made before every fact is in. Checking Bias When the Evidence Is Incomplete prepares you for that ordinary situation, when a first explanation feels convincing and you need to separate what you actually observed from what you assumed about a person's motives or ability.

## Scene 2: Two applicants, two kinds of doubt (0:21–0:57)
**Visual:** A small interview room after a selection panel. The supervisor (C6) and the program manager (C2) sit with two unlabeled application folders.
**Supervisor (C6):** I've worked with the first applicant on two projects. Their example is solid, and I know they follow through.
**Program manager (C2):** And the second applicant? Their example covers similar work.
**Supervisor (C6):** It could be fine. I just don't know them. I'd want more proof before we move them forward.
**Program manager (C2):** So we'd ask the second applicant for another work sample?
**Narrator (C3):** Two comparable examples, and two different amounts of doubt. The course pauses here and asks what would best support a fair comparison.

## Closing (0:57–1:27)
**Visual:** The narrator (C3) on camera. The two folders rest on the table behind them.
**Narrator (C3):** In the course, you practice finding information that could challenge a first explanation and applying the same relevant questions across comparable decisions. You also look at how small, repeated judgments can shape larger disparities, without assuming a cause from any one case. One question to carry in: in a decision you make often, what evidence counts, and would it count the same way coming from someone you know less well?

## Transcript notes
Two selection panel members weigh comparable applicant examples with different amounts of doubt. An introduction to separating observation from assumption when the evidence is incomplete.
