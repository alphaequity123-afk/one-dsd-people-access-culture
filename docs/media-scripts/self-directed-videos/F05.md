# Video script: Unconscious Bias

- **Status:** Script only, written for the recurring cast (docs/media-scripts/CAST.md). Not recorded.
- **Publish on:** /learning/self-directed/index.html?course=F05
- **Length:** about 1.5 minutes (about 198 spoken words)
- **Cast:** Narrator (C3, Host voice), Case manager (C5, Host voice), Newer colleague (C7, Co-host voice) (see docs/media-scripts/CAST.md)
- **Accessibility:** captions and a full transcript are required before publishing (docs/ACCESSIBILITY-STANDARD.md). On-screen text must also be spoken. Describe any visual that carries meaning.

## Scene 1: A question settled too early (0:00–0:18)
**Visual:** A plain title card reading “Unconscious Bias,” which fades to an illustrated DSD workspace.
**Narrator (C3):** A first impression can settle a question before anyone notices it was asked. Unconscious Bias prepares you for the routine moments in DSD services and workplace decisions where an assumption, working outside our awareness, can quietly become a barrier for someone else.

## Scene 2: A note in the file (0:18–0:48)
**Visual:** The newer colleague (C7) sits at a desk with a case file open on screen, and the case manager (C5) pulls up a chair. No text on the screen is readable.
**Newer colleague (C7):** I’m picking up this file. Two service calls went unanswered, and the record says the person “does not care.”
**Case manager (C5):** So what are you planning for the next contact?
**Newer colleague (C7):** Honestly, the note makes it sound settled. I’m not sure another call is worth it.
**Narrator (C3):** The label is already in the record, and the next decision is about to rest on it. What response would avoid turning that assumption into a service barrier?

## Closing (0:48–1:25)
**Visual:** The closing question appears on screen as the narrator reads it: “Which notes or labels in your work get passed to the next person without anyone checking them?”
**Narrator (C3):** The course explains automatic associations, first impressions, and decision shortcuts without treating them as a complete account of behavior. It helps you separate an observation from a judgment about motivation, credibility, or ability, and use relevant evidence and transparent criteria to interrupt an assumption. It also traces how repeated individual shortcuts can become a routine that distributes opportunity or burden unevenly. A question to carry into the course: which notes or labels in your work get passed to the next person without anyone checking them?
