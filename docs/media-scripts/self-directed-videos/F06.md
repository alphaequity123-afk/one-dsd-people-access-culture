- **Length:** about 1.5 minutes (about 205 spoken words)

## Scene 1: Advantage in ordinary routines (0:00–0:21)
**Visual:** A plain title card reading “Understanding White Privilege,” which fades to an illustrated office hallway.
**Narrator (C3):** Some advantages at work travel through routines that feel ordinary, such as who hears about an opportunity first. Understanding White Privilege prepares you to look honestly at racial advantage in DSD service access, workplace culture, and institutional practice, including advantages that can coexist with effort, hardship, and disability.

## Scene 2: Already filled (0:21–0:51)
**Visual:** The case manager (C5) and the supervisor (C6) talk in a hallway beside a bulletin board. No notice on the board is readable.
**Case manager (C5):** I just heard about the development assignment. I would have applied, but it sounds like it’s already filled.
**Supervisor (C6):** It went around informally first. A few people heard about it early and said yes.
**Case manager (C5):** And that kind of experience tends to come up later, when people are considered for advancement.
**Supervisor (C6):** It often does.
**Narrator (C3):** One opportunity reached an informal circle before others heard about it. The course asks you what this situation actually establishes.

## Closing (0:51–1:28)
**Visual:** The closing question appears on screen as the narrator reads it: “In your own work, how do people usually first hear about an opportunity?”
**Narrator (C3):** The course helps you explain unearned racial advantage while recognizing effort, hardship, disability, and variation within racial groups. It looks at how established pathways, networks, and expectations may be easier for some people to navigate, and how to describe a fairer opportunity without asking an affected person to prove the whole concept. You do not need to disclose personal harm or speak for any group. A question to carry into the course: in your own work, how do people usually first hear about an opportunity?
