## Scene 1: After the team meeting (0:00–0:35)
**Visual:** An ordinary open office in a public agency. A video meeting has just ended on a wall screen; the agenda on screen reads "Updates" with six names listed under it and no discussion item. Three colleagues stay behind at a table. No logos and no real people.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** A DSD team has just finished its weekly meeting. The agenda on the screen lists six updates and no time for discussion. Three colleagues stay behind.
**{{NEW_COLLEAGUE}}:** Can I ask something that might sound odd? I've been here three weeks, and I still can't tell which of our rules are written down and which ones we just follow.
**{{CASE_MANAGER}}:** Like what?
**{{NEW_COLLEAGUE}}:** Like nobody asks questions in the meeting. Then afterward my chat fills up with the questions everyone was holding.

## Scene 2: The template (0:35–1:20)
**Visual:** {{NEW_COLLEAGUE}} turns a laptop toward the others. A standard letter template is open, with one sentence highlighted in yellow. The screen text is too small to read; the highlight is what matters.
**{{NEW_COLLEAGUE}}:** And this. I think there's an error in the renewal letter. It's been going out for months. I almost didn't mention it, because I figured whoever wrote it would think I was criticizing them.
**{{CASE_MANAGER}}:** Honestly, I wrote part of that. And you're right, that line is wrong. But we've always sent letters that way. Families have learned to call us if something's unclear.
**{{SUPERVISOR}}:** That's what stops me. We say we're person-centered. It's on the wall in the break room. But if families have to call us to understand our own letter, who is that working for?
**{{CASE_MANAGER}}:** To be fair, the format exists for a reason. It went through legal review years ago. Nobody wants to be the one who changes it and gets it wrong.

## Scene 3: The question nobody has asked (1:20–2:15)
**Visual:** Close on the three faces, thoughtful rather than tense. Through the window, late-afternoon light.
**{{SUPERVISOR}}:** So which parts of how we work are requirements, and which parts are just habits we learned from each other?
**{{NEW_COLLEAGUE}}:** And how would we even tell the difference?
**{{SUPERVISOR}}:** When I started here, someone told me every letter needs two reviewers before it goes out. I never asked why. A few years later I was telling new people the same thing.
**{{CASE_MANAGER}}:** Same here. Half of what I know about this job, I learned by watching who got thanked and who got corrected.
**{{NEW_COLLEAGUE}}:** So if I stay quiet in meetings long enough, I'll end up teaching the next person to stay quiet too.
**{{CASE_MANAGER}}:** I'm not sure I want to find out everything I do out of habit.
**{{SUPERVISOR}}:** Fair. I'm not sure I do either. But I'd rather look at it than keep teaching it to everyone who joins.

## Scene 4: What the course covers (2:15–3:10)
**Visual:** The scene softens to a simple illustration of three stacked layers. The top layer, labeled "What you see," shows an agenda and a letter. The middle layer, labeled "What we say," shows a values poster. The bottom layer, labeled "What we assume," is shaded and has no picture. The narrator reads each label.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** Every team has a culture: shared assumptions about how work gets done, learned over time and passed to new people mostly without words. The organizational psychologist Edgar Schein described culture at three levels. Here they are labeled what you see, what we say, and what we assume. In this course you'll practice reading all three in DSD meetings, in the notices we send, and in supervision. You'll also explore six cultural tensions in public service, such as speed and thoughtfulness, or standardization and accessibility, and what happens when one side always wins.

## Closing (3:10–3:30)
**Visual:** Return to the empty table. The highlighted template is still open on the laptop.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** This team hasn't decided what to do yet. Neither have you. There are no right answers to memorize here, and no one is being graded; the aim is to see your own workplace more clearly. Start with the first lesson, and bring one routine from your own work to look at with fresh eyes.
