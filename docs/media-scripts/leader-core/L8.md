## Scene 1: What this course is for (0:00–0:29)
**Visual:** A management meeting around a conference table, with a printed agenda and a proposal packet in front of each person.
**Narrator (C3):** "Using your influence: equity at the management table" is an Intermediate course for supervisors and managers, and for colleagues across DHS who contribute to decisions. A useful equity concern makes a decision question clearer: what is changing, who may face a barrier, what evidence is available and what still needs examination. This course helps you raise those concerns in leadership meetings constructively, and follow the answer into action.

## Scene 2: A moment at the table (0:29–1:13)
**Visual:** The program manager (C2) presents a proposal at the head of the table. The supervisor (C6) has the packet open to a page of the task record.
**Program manager (C2):** Under this proposal, requests would come in through the online form only. In the trial, average processing time came down, so we'd like to settle it today.
**Supervisor (C6):** Before we do, I was reading the task record. Some of the recent submissions came in through the other route, the one this would remove.
**Program manager (C2):** Are you saying the form doesn't work?
**Visual:** The supervisor pauses. The rest of the table waits.
**Narrator (C3):** The supervisor has a real observation, but not yet an explanation of why people used the other route or what removing it would mean for them. What is the most useful thing to say next? The course lets you try the choice, with feedback.

## Scene 3: Ideas you will practice (1:13–2:35)
**Visual:** Three short scenes: two colleagues talking in a hallway after a meeting, a meeting record with one item marked as acknowledged, and a decision memo set beside an open question.
**Narrator (C3):** The course follows this practice proposal through review and mixed findings. First, disagreement can carry different meanings. One colleague may see raising a risk in the room as responsibility to the people affected, while another sees speaking with the author first as care for the relationship and a fair chance to respond. These are individual histories, not group traits, and understanding both can change the arrangement without dropping the question. Second, acknowledgment is not an answer. The course separates acknowledgment, review, answer and decision, so a follow-up can ask about what is actually missing instead of assuming progress or dismissal. Third, influence is more than winning. A question that enters the review and shapes the reasoning has mattered, even when the decision keeps part of the original proposal. Formal matters keep their own routes: a disability-related request goes promptly to the appropriate decision-maker, and a supervisor reports a protected-class allegation immediately to human resources or the agency Affirmative Action Officer.
**Visual:** The job aid page, with three headings: "Prepare the inquiry," "Carry the response" and "Return and learn."
**Narrator (C3):** The course ends with a job aid for a specific inquiry, an actual review and an honest return, in three sections: Prepare the inquiry, Carry the response, and Return and learn.

## Closing (2:35–2:58)
**Visual:** The course page, with the first lesson card highlighted.
**Narrator (C3):** As you begin, recall a decision meeting where you noticed something and weren't sure how to raise it. What did you know at that moment, and what was still a possibility? Learning here is voluntary, and private reflection is welcome. When you are ready, open the first lesson, "Bring a question into the decision."
