## Scene 1: What this course is for (0:00–0:30)
**Visual:** A training room after a workshop, chairs pushed back, then a cut to an ordinary desk with an open inbox. No text on screen.
**Narrator (C3):** "Leading People, Access and Culture as systems change, not a one-time event" is an Advanced course for staff and leaders across DHS who examine connected work practices, and a core course for division and section leaders. One event can begin an important conversation. Lasting work also depends on how decisions, responsibilities, resources and feedback connect. The course shows how to treat equity as an ongoing operating practice with feedback loops.

## Scene 2: A moment at work (0:30–1:08)
**Visual:** The supervisor (C6) and a case manager (C5) stand at a whiteboard. It shows two boxes labeled "Inbox A" and "Inbox B" with an arrow between them and a question mark on the arrow. The labels are spoken in the dialogue.
**Case manager (C5):** Everyone on the team went to the clear-communication workshop, and people said they learned useful things. But in the sample of guidance requests we pulled, several people were passed between Inbox A and Inbox B, and nobody told them who to contact next.
**Supervisor (C6):** And each inbox answered its own part of the request.
**Case manager (C5):** So either we run the workshop again, or we admit it didn't accomplish anything.
**Supervisor (C6):** Before the team picks one of those, I need to decide how I'm going to frame what we look at next.

## Scene 3: What the course gives you (1:08–2:30)
**Visual:** A simple process map drawn step by step: "Receive the request," "Identify its subject," "Respond or refer," "Confirm the next contact." Each step is read aloud as it appears.
**Narrator (C3):** The course follows an invented guidance-request process. You trace the work before choosing a repair: receive the request, identify its subject, respond or refer it, and confirm the next contact. For each step, note what enters, what is decided, what leaves and who is responsible, and mark what is observed separately from what is assumed.
**Narrator (C3):** Later lessons look at who experiences the difficulty without inventing a cause or asking anyone to disclose personal information, and at what makes a change usable: agreed roles, capacity and the decisions the work requires. Calling something a pilot does not remove those requirements, including the analysis the DHS Equity Policy calls for. Feedback closes the loop. Using a new arrangement and finding it useful are separate questions, and mixed findings can guide the next step without a success or failure label. An individual concern, such as a harassment or discrimination allegation, keeps its own reporting path to HR or an Affirmative Action Officer and does not wait for an improvement review.
**Visual:** The job aid page, with four headings: "Understand the connection," "Examine experience and evidence," "Prepare and implement appropriately" and "Review and continue." The headings are read in the narration.
**Narrator (C3):** The course ends with a job aid in four parts: understand the connection, examine experience and evidence, prepare and implement appropriately, and review and continue.

## Closing (2:30–2:59)
**Visual:** The course page, with the first lesson card, "Connect learning to the way work happens," highlighted. The card title is spoken.
**Narrator (C3):** Here is a question to consider privately. Think of a learning event your team valued. Where in the everyday work would you look to see whether that learning reached the person on the other side of a handoff? When you're ready, open the first lesson, "Connect learning to the way work happens." The course is voluntary, and you can learn privately, through practice or through appropriate work application.
