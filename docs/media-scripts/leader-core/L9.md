## Scene 1: What this course is for (0:00–0:28)
**Visual:** An office in the morning. A laptop shows an email inbox with a forwarded news summary at the top; the subject line is blurred.
**Narrator (C3):** "Leading with values when guidance shifts" is an Advanced course written for division and section leaders, and open to leaders and colleagues across DHS. This course helps you stay grounded in state law, DHS policy and service values as federal and state direction change, by asking clear questions about where a direction came from, clarifying actual responsibilities and giving an honest account of what is known.

## Scene 2: A moment at work (0:28–1:07)
**Visual:** A newer colleague (C7) stops at the program manager's (C2) desk, holding a phone with the news summary open.
**Newer colleague (C7):** Did you see this? It says an outside agency is going to review a grant-reporting practice. Should we cancel our review of the service-information form?
**Program manager (C2):** I saw the summary this morning. I haven't seen the actual item yet.
**Newer colleague (C7):** People on the team are already asking me what's going on. Could we at least tell them nothing's changing?
**Visual:** The program manager looks at the summary on the phone, then at the form draft on the desk.
**Narrator (C3):** The program manager has a headline, a team that wants an answer and no complete source yet. What should the program manager do next? The course lets you work through the choice, with feedback.

## Scene 3: Ideas you will practice (1:07–2:34)
**Visual:** Three short scenes: two colleagues talking at a whiteboard, a form with two ways to submit it, and a short draft message on a screen.
**Narrator (C3):** The course follows this practice report to a scoped answer. First, steadiness can mean different things. One colleague may show dependable leadership through timely acknowledgment, so a concern doesn't turn into rumor. Another may show it through careful interpretation, so an early explanation doesn't have to be reversed. These are individual histories, not group traits, and one message can carry both. Second, a cautious-looking response can have effects of its own. Removing a way to submit the form while the report is examined raises an equity question, and calling the change temporary does not answer it. Third, a message people can use keeps its conditions. Preparing a draft for review is not the same as publishing it, and a shorter message should not turn one into the other. Formal matters keep their own routes: interpretation questions go to the responsible legal or policy role, a disability-related request goes promptly to the appropriate decision-maker, and a supervisor reports a protected-class allegation immediately to human resources or the agency Affirmative Action Officer.
**Visual:** The job aid page, with three headings: "Locate and ask," "Communicate and respond" and "Return and learn."
**Narrator (C3):** The course ends with a job aid built on the actual item, a relevant question and a usable return, in three sections: Locate and ask, Communicate and respond, and Return and learn.

## Closing (2:34–2:57)
**Visual:** The course page, with the first lesson card highlighted.
**Narrator (C3):** As you begin, think about the last time a change in direction reached you secondhand. What did you know for certain, and what were you filling in? Learning here is voluntary, and the practice cases ask for no live document or public disclosure. When you are ready, open the first lesson, "Identify the actual change."
