## Scene 1: What this course is for (0:00–0:31)
**Visual:** A quiet office desk by a window, with an open notebook, a pen and a cup of coffee.
**Narrator (C3):** "Equity leadership self-assessment and growth plan" is an Advanced course written for division and section leaders, and open to leaders and colleagues across DHS. It offers a reflective look at your strengths and growth areas, and it ends with a personal plan for your own learning. There is no personal score, formal assessment or required live project. Leadership growth can begin with an honest question about a practice or a familiar assumption.

## Scene 2: A moment at work (0:31–1:21)
**Visual:** The supervisor (C6) and a program manager (C2), peers, sit at a small table with a printed meeting invitation between them.
**Supervisor (C6):** The draft comparison went well, I thought. Friendly, quick. But afterward our newest team member asked what the comparison was supposed to be.
**Program manager (C2):** What did the invitation say?
**Supervisor (C6):** I want to get to that. First, though, I'd rather start with me. What assumption did I bring into it?
**Program manager (C2):** Could we start with what the invitation actually said, and what the newer colleague needed? I'm not sure a personal admission is the place to begin.
**Supervisor (C6):** That feels a little like stepping around the hard part.
**Visual:** Both look at the invitation. Neither speaks for a moment.
**Narrator (C3):** Two colleagues who both value honesty, with different ideas of where a responsible review begins. How should the supervisor shape this conversation? The course lets you work through the choice, with feedback.

## Scene 3: Ideas you will practice (1:21–2:38)
**Visual:** Three short scenes: a meeting invitation with one line circled, the same three avatar icons repeated on four calendar entries, and a notebook page split into two columns.
**Narrator (C3):** The course follows one practice self-review. First, examine a practice, not your character. A friendly tone is one observation; whether the invitation made the task understandable is another, and neither a flattering nor a punishing verdict answers it. Second, a pattern locates a question without proving its cause. If the same familiar colleagues are invited four times, that is worth looking into, but it does not settle why, or what the right change would be. Third, a direction can be inward or practical. You might privately explore an assumption, or carry a specific question to the person responsible for the work. Either way, keep a plan, a completed action and an observed result separate. A reflection aid never delays a formal duty. A disability-related request goes promptly to the appropriate decision-maker, and a supervisor reports a protected-class allegation immediately to human resources or the agency Affirmative Action Officer.
**Visual:** The job aid page, with three headings: "Reflect and inquire," "Choose a direction" and "Keep responsibilities and review clear."
**Narrator (C3):** The course ends with a job aid for a meaningful question, relevant evidence and a direction worth exploring, in three sections: Reflect and inquire, Choose a direction, and Keep responsibilities and review clear.

## Closing (2:38–2:59)
**Visual:** The course page, with the first lesson card highlighted.
**Narrator (C3):** As you begin, consider one leadership practice you rely on often. What would you want to understand about how it works for others? Learning here is voluntary, and sharing a reflection is always your choice. When you are ready, open the first lesson, "Begin with a question worth understanding."
