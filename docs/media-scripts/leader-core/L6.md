# Video script: Accountability without blame: tracking equity commitments

- **Status:** Script only, written for the recurring cast (docs/media-scripts/CAST.md). Not recorded.
- **Publish on:** /courses/dc-l6-accountability-without-blame-tracking-equity-com
- **Length:** about 3 minutes (2:57; about 413 spoken words)
- **Cast:** Narrator (C3, Host voice), Program manager (C2, Host voice), Newer colleague (C7, Co-host voice) (see docs/media-scripts/CAST.md)
- **Accessibility:** captions and a full transcript are required before publishing (docs/ACCESSIBILITY-STANDARD.md). On-screen text must also be spoken. Describe any visual that carries meaning.

## Scene 1: What this course is for (0:00–0:27)
**Visual:** A team workspace with a progress tracker on a large screen. Most rows hold a figure; one row is empty.
**Narrator (C3):** "Accountability without blame: tracking equity commitments" is written for leaders, with division and section leaders in mind, and it is open to staff and leaders across DHS. It asks how a team can follow through on an equity commitment without turning every unanswered question into personal fault, and it gives you practice measuring progress, naming gaps and correcting course in a supportive way.

## Scene 2: A moment at work (0:27–1:13)
**Visual:** The program manager (C2) and a newer colleague (C7) sit at a desk, looking at the tracker. The newer colleague points to the empty row.
**Newer colleague (C7):** The letter review is nearly done. In twenty of the twenty-five practice letters, the reader could find the next step. The only empty row is the other communication route. We never got to that one.
**Program manager (C2):** So what were you planning to put there?
**Newer colleague (C7):** Honestly, I was going to mark it as fine. Everything else went well, and a blank row makes the whole commitment look unfinished.
**Program manager (C2):** I can see why you want the page to look complete. Give me a minute before we decide what goes in that row.
**Visual:** The program manager looks from the empty row to the rest of the page.
**Narrator (C3):** What should that row say? The course gives you this choice to work through, with feedback on each option.

## Scene 3: Ideas you will practice (1:13–2:33)
**Visual:** A simple illustration of a service-information letter, then three short notes appearing beside it, each shown as an icon with no text.
**Narrator (C3):** The course follows one practice commitment: making the next step in a service-information letter easier to understand. Three ideas run through it. First, a useful activity and a demonstrated effect are different claims. A translated draft can be real preparation without showing that a reader can find the next step. Second, a figure needs its task, period and set beside it. Twenty of twenty-five describes the examined practice letters, not everyone DHS serves, and information that is missing stays unknown rather than counting as success. Third, a progress account is most useful when it separates prepared, reviewed, authorized and sent, so the next colleague can see where the work actually stands. Formal duties keep their own routes: a disability-related request goes promptly to the appropriate decision-maker, and a supervisor reports a protected-class allegation immediately to human resources or the agency Affirmative Action Officer. A project review never delays either.
**Visual:** The job aid page, with three headings: "Clarify and examine," "Retain responsibilities" and "Return and continue."
**Narrator (C3):** The course ends with a job aid built around a clear purpose, useful evidence and an honest next responsibility. Its three sections, Clarify and examine, Retain responsibilities, and Return and continue, give you questions for revisiting a commitment.

## Closing (2:33–2:57)
**Visual:** The course page, with the first lesson card highlighted.
**Narrator (C3):** Before you begin, think of one commitment you have seen reported. What would a reader need to know about what was examined and what is still open? Learning here is voluntary, and the practice cases ask for no live plan or private information. When you are ready, open the first lesson, "Make the commitment clear."

## Transcript notes
Media manifest summary: A short introduction for DHS leaders on tracking an equity commitment honestly: separating activity from evidence, keeping figures with their scope, and reporting gaps without blame.

The practice figures (twenty of twenty-five letters, the unexamined communication route) come from lesson 2 of the course; the leader's decision point matches the lesson 2 practice case and its answer is left to the course.
