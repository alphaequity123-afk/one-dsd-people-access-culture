## Scene 1: What this course is for (0:00–0:25)
**Visual:** A hybrid team meeting: people at a table, two colleagues on a video screen, a shared chat panel open at the side.
**Narrator (C3):** "Modeling inclusive leadership behavior" is a Foundations course for managers, supervisors, team leads and project leads, and it is open to leaders and colleagues across DHS. Inclusive leadership shows up in ordinary moments, and this course focuses on specific everyday behaviors that signal inclusion to a team, and on what those behaviors actually make possible in the work.

## Scene 2: A moment at work (0:25–1:12)
**Visual:** After a meeting, the supervisor (C6) and a case manager (C5) stand by a table with a stack of meeting notes.
**Case manager (C5):** Can I ask about the findings presentations? I've prepared the notes for the last four meetings, and each time our more experienced colleague has presented them.
**Supervisor (C6):** Your notes are a big part of why those presentations go well.
**Case manager (C5):** Thank you. I'd like to practice presenting the source work I helped put together. The next one includes the discrepancy I found.
**Supervisor (C6):** The next presentation is tomorrow, and you're still carrying your usual assignments. Let me think about how to answer that properly.
**Visual:** The supervisor looks at the calendar on the wall, then back at the case manager.
**Narrator (C3):** What would a fair answer look like here, for the case manager and for the work? The course gives you this decision to work through, with feedback on each choice.

## Scene 3: Ideas you will practice (1:12–2:36)
**Visual:** Three short scenes in sequence: a question waiting in a meeting chat, a colleague writing a question at a desk, and a wall sketch beside a shared document on a laptop.
**Narrator (C3):** Three ideas run through the practice cases. First, an invitation needs a way to be received. When a remote colleague's question sits unnoticed in the chat, a stronger opening statement does not answer it; it needs someone to receive it and a way for the answer to come back. Second, the same invitation can mean different things to different people. Being named in a meeting may feel like recognition to one person and like pressure to answer too soon to another. These are individual meanings, not traits of a group, and understanding both can change the method without dropping the work. Third, shared is not the same as usable. A sketch on a wall, or an image on a screen, may still leave some people unable to follow the step being discussed. Formal matters keep their own routes: a disability-related request goes promptly to the appropriate decision-maker, and a protected-class allegation is reported immediately to human resources or the agency Affirmative Action Officer.
**Visual:** The job aid page, with three headings: "Invite and prepare," "Carry actual responsibilities" and "Return and learn."
**Narrator (C3):** The course ends with a job aid for a meaningful contribution, a useful response and an honest next responsibility, in three sections: Invite and prepare, Carry actual responsibilities, and Return and learn.

## Closing (2:36–2:58)
**Visual:** The course page, with the first lesson card highlighted.
**Narrator (C3):** As you start, think of an invitation you made or received at work recently. What happened to the contribution after it was offered? Learning here is voluntary, and you can practice with the course examples or reflect privately. When you are ready, open the first lesson, "Make inclusion visible in the work."
