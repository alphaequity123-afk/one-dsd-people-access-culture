## Scene 1: What this course is for (0:00–0:34)
**Visual:** A team's work plan on a wall screen. At the top, a sentence reads "We will make our guidance more usable for partners." The sentence is spoken in the narration.
**Narrator (C3):** "From commitment to practice: building equity into team goals" is an Intermediate course for leaders and staff across DHS who prepare team goals, work plans, position descriptions or priorities, and it is a core course for division and section leaders. Many teams already hold a commitment such as "we will make our guidance more usable for partners." This course offers ways to write that kind of commitment into work plans, position descriptions and team priorities as a clear proposed practice.

## Scene 2: A moment at work (0:34–1:12)
**Visual:** The supervisor (C6) and a case manager (C5) look at a laptop showing a planning table. One row reads "Review a guidance sample with willing users before release," and the cell under "Responsible" is empty. The row and the column name are spoken in the dialogue.
**Supervisor (C6):** This line is close: "Review a guidance sample with willing users before release." We record their questions and consider revisions before the guidance goes out.
**Case manager (C5):** The "Responsible" column is still empty. Why not put the county partner there? They know this guidance better than anyone, and it would make the line look accountable.
**Supervisor (C6):** They would be good reviewers. We haven't talked with them about any of this yet, though.
**Case manager (C5):** Then what goes in that column?
**Supervisor (C6):** That's what I need to settle before this plan goes to anyone.

## Scene 3: What the course gives you (1:12–2:28)
**Visual:** Three labels appear side by side, each read aloud: "Commitment," "Proposed practice," "Implemented result."
**Narrator (C3):** The course starts by separating three things that blur easily: a stated commitment, a proposed practice and an implemented result. A well-written line can make coordination clearer, but it does not show that the work happened or that it worked. Four optional drafting questions help: what task would change, who has responsibility and authority, what information could show what happened, and when a review would be useful.
**Visual:** Three simple icons with no readable text: a release log, a page of usability notes and a chart. The narration names each one.
**Narrator (C3):** Evidence gets the same care. A release log, a usability observation and a later outcome support different claims. A group breakdown helps only where permission, privacy and reliable counts support it, and missing information stays unknown rather than reassuring. For a position description, a draft is preparation; qualification and classification questions go through the applicable HR review. Accommodation requests and workplace reports also keep their own processes, and those decisions stay with the responsible offices.
**Visual:** The job aid's four section headings, read aloud: "Understand the current work," "Prepare the proposal," "Retain actual responsibilities," "Review and continue."
**Narrator (C3):** The course ends with a job aid for clarifying a proposed practice without overstating its authority or its effects, in four steps: understand the current work, prepare the proposal, retain actual responsibilities, and review and continue.

## Closing (2:28–2:58)
**Visual:** The course page, with the first lesson card, "Connect a commitment to a proposed practice," highlighted. The card title is spoken.
**Narrator (C3):** Here is a question to consider privately. Think of one commitment your team already states. What would someone need to see to understand how it shows up in the work, and what would that record still not tell them? When you're ready, open the first lesson, "Connect a commitment to a proposed practice." The course is voluntary, and private reflection is a legitimate way to learn; no live document is required.
