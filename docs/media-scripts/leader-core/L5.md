## Scene 1: What this course is for (0:00–0:31)
**Visual:** A wall clock in an office, then a desk calendar with today's date circled. No text on screen.
**Narrator (C3):** "Equity-informed decision-making under time pressure" is an Intermediate course for staff and leaders across DHS, and a core course for division and section leaders, managers and supervisors. A short deadline can make an answer feel more settled than it is. The course gives you a short set of questions for checking equity impact when a decision is urgent. It does not promise a five-minute solution, and it does not authorize a decision.

## Scene 2: A moment at work (0:31–1:04)
**Visual:** The program manager (C2) on a video call with a community partner (C1). After the call ends, a newer colleague (C7) pulls up a chair. On the program manager's screen are two documents labeled "Published guidance" and "Proposed change, in review." The labels are spoken in the dialogue.
**Community partner (C1):** Could you send me a summary of the guidance by the end of the day? It's urgent on our end.
**Program manager (C2):** We'll get you something today.
**Newer colleague (C7):** While we're writing it, why not also say where the proposed change stands? Everyone is asking about it, and it's the same subject.
**Program manager (C2):** The published guidance is approved. The proposed change is still in review.
**Newer colleague (C7):** We only have a few hours, though.
**Program manager (C2):** I know. Let me think this through before we start drafting.

## Scene 3: What the course gives you (1:04–2:30)
**Visual:** Three cards appear, each read aloud: "Factual answer," "Recommendation," "Authorized decision."
**Narrator (C3):** Across six lessons, the course follows a practice request like this one. It starts by making the task clear before making the answer fast, because a factual answer, a recommendation and an authorized decision are different tasks with different requirements. It then keeps different meanings in view. In one exchange, a manager hears a colleague's question as criticism of their preparation, while the colleague hears the finished draft as closing a question too soon. Hearing what care means to each person widens the frame, without turning anyone's style into a group label.
**Narrator (C3):** Another lesson compares what each option does, not only whether it is called temporary, since a shortened answer can still shape someone's next action. Urgency also leaves formal duties in place. Managers and supervisors still report a protected-class allegation immediately to HR or the affirmative action officer, and promptly forward an accommodation request to the appropriate decision-maker without asking for medical details. Those decisions stay with HR and the responsible offices. A useful progress note says what is supported, what is still open and who holds the next responsibility.
**Visual:** The job aid's opening line on screen: "A clear task, a supported basis and an honest next responsibility." The line is read in the narration.
**Narrator (C3):** The course ends with a job aid built around a clear task, a supported basis and an honest next responsibility.

## Closing (2:30–2:57)
**Visual:** The course page, with the first lesson card, "Clarify the urgent question," highlighted. The card title is spoken.
**Narrator (C3):** Here is a question to consider privately. Think of the last urgent request you answered. What did the person receiving your answer need to do next, and did your answer give them what they needed? When you're ready, open the first lesson, "Clarify the urgent question." The course is voluntary, and you can learn privately or work through the complete practice path.
