## A small mistake, a big lesson

**Host (female):** Picture a newer colleague three months into the job. They send a client letter with one typo. Within an hour it comes up in a team chat, in a reply-all and in a one-on-one. Nobody is unkind. So what did that colleague just learn?

**Co-host (male):** That a typo is a big deal here. Probably bigger than the policy says. And that the safest move is to have three people check every routine letter from now on.

**Host (female):** Right. Nobody wrote that rule down. The team taught it in an afternoon. That is what culture is: the shared habits and assumptions that tell people what really matters, mostly without words.


## Standard or habit

**Co-host (male):** But accuracy matters. A wrong address on a notice can mean someone misses an appointment. I don't want us to get casual about that.

**Host (female):** Neither do I. The question isn't whether to keep a high standard. It's whether the response matches the stakes. A clear check for things that can hurt someone is a standard. Treating every small slip as a crisis is a habit, and it has costs.

**Co-host (male):** Like the three reviewers for a routine letter. That's time those reviewers aren't spending with families.

**Host (female):** And something quieter. When mistakes feel dangerous, people stop mentioning them. Research on team learning has found that stronger teams sometimes report more errors, not fewer, because people feel safe enough to say so.

**Co-host (male):** So the number of mistakes you hear about isn't the same as the number that happen.

**Host (female):** Exactly. Silence can look like quality from the outside.


## Treating a mistake as information

**Co-host (male):** Say a notice did go out with the wrong office address, and a client traveled to the old location. What does a good response sound like?

**Host (female):** Start with the person affected: fix it for them first. Then ask what about the process let it happen, before asking who. Was the address stored in three places? Did the template pull an old value?

**Co-host (male):** And the person who sent it is in the room, probably feeling awful.

**Host (female):** Which is why the supervisor goes first if they can. Something like, here's what happened, here's what we've already fixed for the client, and here's the part of our process I want us to look at together.


## Raising it when someone is senior

**Co-host (male):** Here's the harder one for me. What if the mistake is the supervisor's? A wrong deadline in the team update, and you're the newest person there.

**Host (female):** Keep it short, private and about the work. I think the report deadline in the update might be the 15th, not the 20th. Want me to check? That's it. No apology tour.

**Co-host (male):** And if the supervisor thanks them for it, in front of the team, the next person speaks up faster.

**Host (female):** That's how a norm shifts. Not with a poster. With what happens the next time someone says, I think we got this wrong.

**Co-host (male):** In the course, you'll practice telling standards from habits, and responding to mistakes in a way that keeps people talking.
