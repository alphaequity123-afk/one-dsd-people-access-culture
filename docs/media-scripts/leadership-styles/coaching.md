# Video script: Coaching or developmental leadership

- **Status:** Script only, written for the recurring cast (docs/media-scripts/CAST.md). Not recorded.
- **Publish on:** /learn/leadership-styles/coaching
- **Length:** about 3 minutes (about 430 spoken words)
- **Cast:** Narrator (C3, Host voice), Supervisor (C6, Co-host voice), Case manager (C5, Host voice) (see docs/media-scripts/CAST.md)
- **Accessibility:** captions and a full transcript are required before publishing (docs/ACCESSIBILITY-STANDARD.md). On-screen text must also be spoken. Describe any visual that carries meaning.

## Scene 1: Building capability (0:00–0:25)
**Visual:** Title card on a plain background: "Coaching or developmental leadership: building capability." It fades to an illustrated office where two colleagues talk over coffee.
**Narrator (C3):** This lesson is about coaching, or developmental, leadership, which the pathway sums up as building capability. The leader uses feedback, reflection, practice and support to strengthen people's skills. In the next few minutes, we'll see how this approach might respond to the common scenario, what it is not, and how it can help or harm equity and inclusion.

## Scene 2: The common scenario (0:25–1:09)
**Visual:** On-screen title: "Who gets the visible work?" The supervisor hands a presentation folder to one team member while the case manager (C5) works through spreadsheets at a nearby desk. A short cut to a team meeting where another colleague, shown from behind, speaks over the case manager (C5).
**Narrator (C3):** The pathway's fictional common scenario is called “Who gets the visible work?” A supervisor keeps giving high-visibility assignments to people they describe as polished, confident and ready to represent the team. The case manager, a Black employee, points out that they get a lot of technical, behind-the-scenes work but fewer chances to present to leadership. In a team discussion, a colleague interrupts: “Everything doesn't have to be about race.” Later, the case manager asks for the assignments to be reviewed and says they're worried that raising it will hurt their opportunities. Any part that calls for a formal process stays with the person or area responsible.

## Scene 3: A stronger version (1:09–1:33)
**Visual:** The supervisor (C6) and the case manager (C5) talk privately in a small meeting room. On-screen label: "A stronger version."
**Supervisor (C6):** We will review the opportunity process. Separately, if you want to build presentation experience, let's identify meaningful opportunities and support.
**Case manager (C5):** I'm glad those are separate. The review shouldn't depend on whether I take you up on the second part.
**Narrator (C3):** The leader keeps the process review and the development offer apart, and the development is the employee's choice.

## Scene 4: A harmful version (1:33–1:55)
**Visual:** The same room. The supervisor (C6) slides a page titled "Communication tips" across the table. On-screen label: "A harmful version." The page title is spoken in the narration that follows.
**Supervisor (C6):** Let's work on how you raise concerns so people don't react badly.
**Case manager (C5):** I was the one who got interrupted. Is anyone looking at that, or at how the assignments are made?
**Narrator (C3):** Handing over communication tips here shifts attention away from the dismissal and the allocation process, and toward the employee's delivery.

## Scene 5: What it is not, and equity (1:55–2:43)
**Visual:** Two illustrated paths from the same desk: one leads to a presentation room, the other to a stack of correction notes. No text on screen.
**Narrator (C3):** Coaching is not a replacement for fixing the process, and it is not teaching people to raise concerns more quietly. At its best, it offers individualized support and widens readiness for advancement. It can also become assimilation training, helping employees conform to dominant preferences instead of questioning them. Coaching can go to favorites, and structural problems can be recast as personal deficits. So watch who receives developmental investment and who receives only corrective feedback. Labels such as “too emotional,” “abrasive” or “not polished” have often been applied unevenly to women of color and other employees whose style differs from the dominant one. Coaching should widen the range of accepted styles, not narrow it.

## Closing (2:43–3:04)
**Visual:** The reflection question on screen: "In the common scenario, why must the presentation offer be separate from the process review?" Then the lesson page's section list.
**Narrator (C3):** A question to consider on your own: in the common scenario, why must the presentation offer be separate from the process review? The rest of this lesson, on this page, covers another conflict, strengths and risks, when to use this approach, complementary approaches and safeguards, and a practice exercise.

## Transcript notes
Shows how coaching leadership might respond to the common scenario, contrasting a process review kept separate from an optional development offer with coaching the employee on how they raise concerns.
