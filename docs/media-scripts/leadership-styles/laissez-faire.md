# Video script: Laissez-faire leadership

- **Status:** Script only, written for the recurring cast (docs/media-scripts/CAST.md). Not recorded.
- **Publish on:** /learn/leadership-styles/laissez-faire
- **Length:** about 3 minutes (about 438 spoken words)
- **Cast:** Narrator (C3, Host voice), Supervisor (C6, Co-host voice), Case manager (C5, Host voice), Newer colleague (C7, Co-host voice) (see docs/media-scripts/CAST.md)
- **Accessibility:** captions and a full transcript are required before publishing (docs/ACCESSIBILITY-STANDARD.md). On-screen text must also be spoken. Describe any visual that carries meaning.

## Scene 1: What this video covers (0:00–0:30)
**Visual:** Title card: "Laissez-faire leadership: absence of necessary action." Plain background, no logos or real people.
**Narrator (C3):** This short video looks at laissez-faire leadership, which this pathway sums up as the absence of necessary action. Here it means avoiding decisions, feedback or intervention when they are needed, which is different from simply allowing people independence. The lesson notes that research has associated this passive pattern with workplace stress, bullying and distress, and that these are associations, not proof that every low-intervention leader causes harm.

## Scene 2: The common scenario (0:30–1:05)
**Visual:** A simple illustrated team meeting. On-screen title: "Who gets the visible work?" The case manager (C5) appears as the employee who raised the concern.
**Narrator (C3):** Every lesson in this pathway uses the same fictional scenario, "Who gets the visible work?" A supervisor keeps giving high-visibility assignments to employees described as polished, confident and ready to represent the team. A Black employee points out that they get a lot of behind-the-scenes technical work but fewer chances to present to leadership, and a colleague interrupts: "Everything doesn't have to be about race." Anything that may involve discrimination or retaliation goes through the agency's process, with the area responsible. Later, the employee goes to the supervisor.

## Scene 3: A stronger version (1:05–1:45)
**Visual:** The supervisor (C6) and the case manager (C5) at a small table in an office.
**Case manager (C5):** I want the assignments reviewed, and I'm concerned that raising this will hurt my opportunities.
**Supervisor (C6):** You raised this with me, and it is mine to act on. I will look at how assignments have been made and get back to you by Friday with what I found and what happens next.
**Case manager (C5):** Friday works. Thank you for giving me an actual date.
**Narrator (C3):** The correction is simple: take responsibility, set a date and follow up. Low interference is fine for routine work, but not when someone reports a barrier.

## Scene 4: A harmful version (1:45–2:05)
**Visual:** The same office.
**Supervisor (C6):** You're all professionals. Work it out.
**Case manager (C5):** (later, to the newer colleague (C7)) I'm supposed to sort this out with the same people who keep getting the assignments.
**Narrator (C3):** The supervisor leaves the employee to negotiate with colleagues while keeping control over assignments. A related version is "My door is always open," followed by no review, no boundary and no follow-up.

## Scene 5: What it is not, and what it means for equity (2:05–2:50)
**Visual:** On-screen list, read aloud: "Not intentional delegation with support and accountability. Not trust in an experienced team that has what it needs."
**Narrator (C3):** Laissez-faire leadership is not intentional delegation with agreed support and accountability, which the pathway calls empowering leadership. It is not trust in an experienced team that has what it needs. In a team that already works well, low interference can leave room for initiative. When formal authority withdraws from a problem, though, informal networks start to control access, and those networks often follow existing lines of race, language, tenure and social comfort. Concerns go unanswered, and responsibility becomes unclear. As the lesson puts it, silence does not leave a situation neutral. It leaves existing arrangements in place.

## Closing (2:50–3:10)
**Visual:** The reflection question on screen, read aloud.
**Narrator (C3):** A question to consider on your own: What is the difference between giving people independence and leaving them alone with a problem? The rest of this lesson, on this page, covers another conflict, strengths and risks, when to use this approach, safeguards, and a practice exercise.

## Transcript notes
Media manifest summary: A fictional team scenario contrasts a leader who takes responsibility and sets a follow-up date with one who leaves the employee to work out an assignment concern alone.
