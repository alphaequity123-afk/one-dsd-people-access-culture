# Video script: Adaptive and situational leadership

- **Status:** Script only, written for the recurring cast (docs/media-scripts/CAST.md). Not recorded.
- **Publish on:** /learn/leadership-styles/adaptive-situational
- **Length:** about 3 minutes (about 418 spoken words)
- **Cast:** Narrator (C3, Host voice), Supervisor (C6, Co-host voice), Case manager (C5, Host voice), Newer colleague (C7, Co-host voice) (see docs/media-scripts/CAST.md)
- **Accessibility:** captions and a full transcript are required before publishing (docs/ACCESSIBILITY-STANDARD.md). On-screen text must also be spoken. Describe any visual that carries meaning.

## Scene 1: Responding to conditions (0:00–0:28)
**Visual:** Title card on a plain background: "Adaptive and situational leadership: responding to conditions." It fades to an illustrated team workspace.
**Narrator (C3):** This lesson is about adaptive and situational leadership, which the pathway sums up as responding to conditions. The two overlap but are not the same. Situational leadership adjusts direction and support to the task and the person's relevant needs. Adaptive leadership helps a group learn its way through problems whose causes or solutions are uncertain. Both separate what needs immediate action from what needs inquiry.

## Scene 2: The common scenario (0:28–1:11)
**Visual:** On-screen title: "Who gets the visible work?" The supervisor hands a presentation folder to one team member while the case manager (C5) works through spreadsheets at a nearby desk. A short cut to a team meeting where another colleague, shown from behind, speaks over the case manager (C5).
**Narrator (C3):** In the pathway's fictional common scenario, “Who gets the visible work?”, a supervisor keeps giving high-visibility assignments to people they describe as polished, confident and ready to represent the team. The case manager, a Black employee, points out that they get a lot of technical, behind-the-scenes work but fewer chances to present to leadership. In a team discussion, a colleague interrupts: “Everything doesn't have to be about race.” Later, the case manager asks for the assignments to be reviewed and says they're worried that raising it will hurt their opportunities. Any part that calls for a formal process stays with the person or area responsible.

## Scene 3: A stronger version (1:11–1:40)
**Visual:** The next team meeting. The supervisor (C6) speaks to the whole group. On-screen label: "A stronger version."
**Supervisor (C6):** We can immediately stop dismissive interruptions while we look into the assignment pattern. We will test a clearer process and revise it using evidence and employee feedback.
**Newer colleague (C7):** So the interruptions stop now, and the assignment question gets an actual test instead of another discussion.
**Narrator (C3):** The leader acts now on the conduct and treats the allocation question as something to learn about, with a test and a review.

## Scene 4: A harmful version (1:40–1:57)
**Visual:** The supervisor (C6) and the case manager (C5) in a hallway. The supervisor (C6) shrugs. On-screen label: "A harmful version."
**Supervisor (C6):** It depends on the situation. Let's see how things play out.
**Case manager (C5):** It has been playing out for a while. That's why I raised it.
**Narrator (C3):** Context becomes a reason to delay. The interruption goes unaddressed and the allocation question drifts.

## Scene 5: What it is not, and equity (1:57–2:39)
**Visual:** An illustrated set of scales, with a small card beside it that is filled in with a written reason. No text on screen.
**Narrator (C3):** This approach is not “it depends,” used to avoid deciding. It is not different standards for different people without a reason that can be stated and reviewed, and it is not an open-ended learning process while harm continues. Flexibility can address different barriers, and learning from evidence can improve what a team tries. But flexibility can also hide favoritism. The fairness question is not whether every employee receives the same support. It is whether differences are relevant, justified, accessible and reviewable. Unexplained flexibility tends to favor people who are already trusted, which can follow lines of race and familiarity.

## Closing (2:39–2:59)
**Visual:** The reflection question on screen: "Which parts of the common scenario need action now, and which need learning?" Then the lesson page's section list.
**Narrator (C3):** A question to consider on your own: which parts of the common scenario need action now, and which need learning? The rest of this lesson, on this page, covers another conflict, strengths and risks, when to use this approach, complementary approaches and safeguards, and a practice exercise.

## Transcript notes
Shows how adaptive and situational leadership might respond to the common scenario, contrasting immediate action on conduct plus a tested process with “it depends” used as a reason to delay.
