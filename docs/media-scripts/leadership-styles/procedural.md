## Scene 1: Rules and reviewability (0:00–0:25)
**Visual:** Title card on a plain background: "Bureaucratic or procedural leadership: rules and reviewability." It fades to an illustrated office with a shelf of binders.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** This lesson is about bureaucratic, or procedural, leadership, which the pathway sums up as rules and reviewability. The leader relies on formal criteria, documentation, roles and established processes. In the next few minutes, we'll see how this approach might respond to the common scenario, what it is not, and how it can help or harm equity and inclusion.

## Scene 2: The common scenario (0:25–1:02)
**Visual:** On-screen title: "Who gets the visible work?" The supervisor hands a presentation folder to one team member while {{CASE_MANAGER}} works through spreadsheets at a nearby desk. A short cut to a team meeting where another colleague, shown from behind, speaks over {{CASE_MANAGER}}.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** The pathway's fictional common scenario is called “Who gets the visible work?” A supervisor keeps giving high-visibility assignments to people they describe as polished, confident and ready to represent the team. {{CASE_MANAGER}}, a Black employee, points out that they get a lot of technical, behind-the-scenes work but fewer chances to present to leadership. In a team discussion, a colleague interrupts: “Everything doesn't have to be about race.” Later, {{CASE_MANAGER}} asks for the assignments to be reviewed and says they're worried that raising it will hurt their opportunities.

## Scene 3: A stronger version (1:02–1:35)
**Visual:** {{SUPERVISOR}} and {{CASE_MANAGER}} talk privately in a small meeting room. On-screen label: "A stronger version."
**{{SUPERVISOR}}:** We will follow the required concern-response process and, separately, review whether assignment criteria are clear and appropriate.
**{{CASE_MANAGER}}:** That helps. I'd rather know there's a set way this gets handled than wonder what happens next.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** The leader uses process for what it does well: protecting the employee's concern and making the review traceable. That formal process belongs with the person or area responsible for it. The leader also treats the assignment process itself as open to improvement.

## Scene 4: A harmful version (1:35–1:57)
**Visual:** {{SUPERVISOR}} taps a closed policy binder on the desk. {{PROGRAM_MANAGER}} sits across the table. On-screen label: "A harmful version."
**{{SUPERVISOR}}:** No rule was broken, so there is nothing to examine.
**{{PROGRAM_MANAGER}}:** Whether a rule was broken isn't really the question, though. The question is whether the way we hand out assignments is working.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** A process can be formally compliant and still need improvement. This response treats compliance as the end of the question.

## Scene 5: What it is not, and equity (1:57–2:41)
**Visual:** An illustrated rule sheet with a magnifying glass over its first line, then the same sheet with notes in the margin. No text on screen.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** Procedural leadership is not “no rule was broken, so there is nothing to examine,” and it is not documentation in place of fairness. Written criteria can reduce arbitrary decisions, make decisions open to review and challenge, and build safeguards that survive staff turnover. But a process can be followed perfectly and still produce unfair results, and old rules can keep old inequities in place. So ask who wrote a rule, who it was designed around, and who finds it hardest to meet. A rule that seems neutral can fall more heavily on people of color, disabled people or people who need language access.

## Closing (2:41–3:02)
**Visual:** The reflection question on screen: "When is 'we followed the process' a full answer, and when is it not?" Then the lesson page's section list.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** A question to consider on your own: when is “we followed the process” a full answer, and when is it not? The rest of this lesson, on this page, covers another conflict, strengths and risks, when to use this approach, complementary approaches and safeguards, and a practice exercise.

## Transcript notes
