## Scene 1: Expectations and consequences (0:00–0:25)
**Visual:** Title card on a plain background: "Transactional leadership: expectations and consequences." It fades to an illustrated team workspace with a wall planner.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** This lesson is about transactional leadership, which the pathway sums up as expectations and consequences. The leader sets standards, monitors performance, and uses recognition, rewards or corrective responses. In the next few minutes, we'll see how this approach might respond to the common scenario, what it is not, and how it can help or harm equity and inclusion.

## Scene 2: The common scenario (0:25–1:09)
**Visual:** On-screen title: "Who gets the visible work?" The supervisor hands a presentation folder to one team member while {{CASE_MANAGER}} works through spreadsheets at a nearby desk. A short cut to a team meeting where another colleague, shown from behind, speaks over {{CASE_MANAGER}}.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** The pathway's fictional common scenario is called “Who gets the visible work?” A supervisor keeps giving high-visibility assignments to people they describe as polished, confident and ready to represent the team. {{CASE_MANAGER}}, a Black employee, points out that they get a lot of technical, behind-the-scenes work but fewer chances to present to leadership. In a team discussion, a colleague interrupts: “Everything doesn't have to be about race.” Later, {{CASE_MANAGER}} asks for the assignments to be reviewed and says they're worried that raising it will hurt their opportunities. Any part that calls for a formal process stays with the person or area responsible.

## Scene 3: A stronger version (1:09–1:30)
**Visual:** A team meeting. {{SUPERVISOR}} stands beside a whiteboard headed "Assignment criteria." On-screen label: "A stronger version."
**{{SUPERVISOR}}:** Future assignments will use defined criteria. We will review whether the process is followed and whether access actually improves.
**{{PROGRAM_MANAGER}}:** So the check is who actually gets the visible work over time, not only whether everyone used the new criteria.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** This makes the commitment concrete, and it checks results, not only completion.

## Scene 4: A harmful version (1:30–1:55)
**Visual:** The same meeting. {{SUPERVISOR}} writes "2 presentations each" on the whiteboard and caps the marker. On-screen label: "A harmful version." The words on the whiteboard are spoken in the line that follows.
**{{SUPERVISOR}}:** From now on, everyone gets exactly two presentations a year. Done.
**{{NEW_COLLEAGUE}}:** Two each? Presenting isn't part of every role here, and nobody asked what any of us actually want.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** This sets an equal count without considering whether presentations fit each role or what employees want. A weak version also measures only whether a form was filled out.

## Scene 5: What it is not, and equity (1:55–2:41)
**Visual:** An illustrated checklist with every box ticked, beside a simple chart of who presented this quarter. No text on screen.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** Transactional leadership is not proof of fairness because a form was completed, and it is not equal counts without regard to the job or people's interests. Its strengths are clarity and follow-through. It can make equity responsibilities part of normal expectations and create records that can be reviewed. The risk is that a biased standard can be enforced very consistently. Rewarding visibility, for example, may disadvantage people who are repeatedly given essential behind-the-scenes work, which is exactly {{CASE_MANAGER}}'s situation. Narrow targets also invite gaming or surface-level compliance. The lesson sees this as a supporting approach: strong for implementation discipline, weak as the only source of equity judgment.

## Closing (2:41–3:01)
**Visual:** The reflection question on screen: "In the common scenario, what would a fair measure of improvement look like?" Then the lesson page's section list.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** A question to consider on your own: in the common scenario, what would a fair measure of improvement look like? The rest of this lesson, on this page, covers another conflict, strengths and risks, when to use this approach, complementary approaches and safeguards, and a practice exercise.
