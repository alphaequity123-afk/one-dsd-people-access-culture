## Scene 1: What this video covers (0:00–0:20)
**Visual:** Title card: "Authoritarian leadership: compliance over challenge." Plain background, no logos or real people.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** This short video looks at authoritarian leadership, which this pathway sums up as compliance over challenge. Here it means a leader keeps authority by demanding obedience, limiting challenge and attaching consequences to disagreement. We will walk through one fictional scenario, two responses, and what this pattern means for equity and inclusion.

## Scene 2: The common scenario (0:20–1:00)
**Visual:** A simple illustrated team meeting. On-screen title: "Who gets the visible work?" {{CASE_MANAGER}} appears as the employee who raised the concern.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** Every lesson in this pathway uses the same fictional scenario, "Who gets the visible work?" A supervisor keeps giving high-visibility assignments to employees described as polished, confident and ready to represent the team. A Black employee points out that they get a lot of behind-the-scenes technical work but fewer chances to present to leadership, and a colleague interrupts: "Everything doesn't have to be about race." Anything that may involve discrimination or retaliation goes through the agency's process, with the area responsible. Later, the employee goes to the supervisor.

## Scene 3: A stronger version (1:00–1:40)
**Visual:** {{SUPERVISOR}} and {{CASE_MANAGER}} at a small table in an office.
**{{CASE_MANAGER}}:** I want the assignments reviewed, and I'm concerned that raising this will hurt my opportunities.
**{{SUPERVISOR}}:** The concern needs an appropriate response. There should be no adverse treatment for raising it. I will follow the relevant process and review how assignments are allocated.
**{{CASE_MANAGER}}:** Thank you. I'll be watching what happens next.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** This keeps the useful part of the pattern, clarity, and drops the intimidation. The supervisor now has to act in line with it, because words alone do not change what people have learned about the cost of speaking up.

## Scene 4: A harmful version (1:40–2:05)
**Visual:** The same office.
**{{SUPERVISOR}}:** I decide who represents this team. Stop making accusations and focus on your work.
**{{NEW_COLLEAGUE}}:** (later, quietly, to {{CASE_MANAGER}}) After that, I'm not sure any of us will raise something like this again.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** The conflict may disappear from meetings, but the problem remains, and employees learn that raising an equity concern can threaten their standing. Even banning interruptions would protect order rather than fair influence if questions about assignments are still off limits.

## Scene 5: What it is not, and what it means for equity (2:05–2:50)
**Visual:** On-screen list, read aloud: "Not an emergency decision. Not a clear, fair standard. Not a personality type."
**{{NARRATOR}}:** Authoritarian leadership is not the same as making a quick decision in an emergency, or holding people to a clear, fair standard. It is not a personality type; anyone can slip into this pattern under pressure, and anyone can change it. Firm rules can support equity when they stop harmful conduct quickly, such as repeated interruptions, or keep a powerful person from bending a rule. The harm comes when voice depends on pleasing the person in charge. Employees with less institutional protection then carry greater risk when they speak, and silenced complaints can be mistaken for a better team climate.

## Closing (2:50–3:10)
**Visual:** The reflection question on screen, read aloud.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** A question to consider on your own: In this scenario, what would show the employee that raising a concern is safe, words or actions, and which actions? The rest of this lesson, on this page, covers another conflict, strengths and risks, when to use this approach, safeguards, and a practice exercise.
