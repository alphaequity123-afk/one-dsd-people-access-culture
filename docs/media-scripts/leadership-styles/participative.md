## Scene 1: What this video covers (0:00–0:30)
**Visual:** Title card: "Democratic or participative leadership: meaningful input." Plain background, no logos or real people.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** This short video looks at democratic or participative leadership, which this pathway sums up as meaningful input. The people affected by a decision help shape it. That does not have to mean everyone agrees or the majority votes, but it does mean their input can change the result. We will look at one fictional scenario, two ways a leader might respond, and what the approach means for equity and inclusion.

## Scene 2: The common scenario (0:30–1:10)
**Visual:** A simple illustrated team meeting. On-screen title: "Who gets the visible work?" {{CASE_MANAGER}} appears as the employee who raised the concern.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** Every lesson in this pathway uses the same fictional scenario, "Who gets the visible work?" A supervisor keeps giving high-visibility assignments to employees described as polished, confident and ready to represent the team. A Black employee points out that they get a lot of behind-the-scenes technical work but fewer chances to present to leadership, and a colleague interrupts: "Everything doesn't have to be about race." Anything that may involve discrimination or retaliation goes through the agency's process, with the area responsible. Later, the employee goes to the supervisor.

## Scene 3: A stronger version (1:10–1:45)
**Visual:** {{SUPERVISOR}} and {{CASE_MANAGER}} at a small table in an office.
**{{CASE_MANAGER}}:** I want the assignments reviewed, and I'm concerned that raising this will hurt my opportunities.
**{{SUPERVISOR}}:** I will handle the reported concern through the appropriate process. Separately, the team can help clarify assignment requirements and identify barriers to getting opportunities.
**{{NEW_COLLEAGUE}}:** (later, reading a team message) There's a form here if we'd rather share ideas about the criteria privately.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** The supervisor keeps the concern in the right process and uses participation for what it is good at, which is shared design. It also offers individual ways to give input, not just a public discussion.

## Scene 4: A harmful version (1:45–2:05)
**Visual:** The team meeting room.
**{{SUPERVISOR}}:** Let's vote on whether assignments have been fair.
**{{CASE_MANAGER}}:** (to {{NEW_COLLEAGUE}}, after the meeting) So now I need the whole room to agree with me before anything gets looked at.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** A vote makes a person's concern depend on their colleagues' approval, and the people who benefited from the current pattern get a say in whether it was fair.

## Scene 5: What it is not, and what it means for equity (2:05–2:50)
**Visual:** On-screen list, read aloud: "Not a vote on rights or needs. Not input after the decision. Not one meeting some people cannot attend."
**{{NARRATOR}}:** Participative leadership is not a vote on someone's rights or needs, and it is not collecting input after the decision is made. It is also not the same as holding one meeting that some people cannot attend. At its best, participation surfaces information leaders lack, builds trust in decisions people helped shape, and moves influence toward the people closest to the problem. It can undermine equity when majority preferences define fairness, when more confident speakers dominate, or when people feel they must disclose painful experiences in public. Attendance does not equal influence, so the lesson suggests tracking whose input actually changes decisions.

## Closing (2:50–3:10)
**Visual:** The reflection question on screen, read aloud.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** A question to consider on your own: How would the people on your team know that their input mattered? The rest of this lesson, on this page, covers another conflict, strengths and risks, when to use this approach, safeguards, and a practice exercise.
