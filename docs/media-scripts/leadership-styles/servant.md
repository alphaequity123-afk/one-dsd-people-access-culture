## Scene 1: What this video covers (0:00–0:30)
**Visual:** Title card: "Servant leadership: support and growth." Plain background, no logos or real people.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** This short video looks at servant leadership, which this pathway sums up as support and growth. The leader puts the growth, well-being and independence of the people they serve first. The lesson draws on Robert Greenleaf's question of whether the least privileged benefit or are further deprived, which makes this an equity question and not only a kindness question. We will look at one fictional scenario and two ways a leader might respond.

## Scene 2: The common scenario (0:30–1:10)
**Visual:** A simple illustrated team meeting. On-screen title: "Who gets the visible work?" {{CASE_MANAGER}} appears as the employee who raised the concern.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** Every lesson in this pathway uses the same fictional scenario, "Who gets the visible work?" A supervisor keeps giving high-visibility assignments to employees described as polished, confident and ready to represent the team. A Black employee points out that they get a lot of behind-the-scenes technical work but fewer chances to present to leadership, and a colleague interrupts: "Everything doesn't have to be about race." Anything that may involve discrimination or retaliation goes through the agency's process, with the area responsible. Later, the employee goes to the supervisor.

## Scene 3: A stronger version (1:10–1:45)
**Visual:** {{SUPERVISOR}} and {{CASE_MANAGER}} at a small table in an office.
**{{CASE_MANAGER}}:** I want the assignments reviewed, and I'm concerned that raising this will hurt my opportunities.
**{{SUPERVISOR}}:** I want to understand what has limited your access to these assignments. You should not have to solve the allocation problem yourself. I will review the process and discuss what support would be useful.
**{{CASE_MANAGER}}:** That helps. I was expecting to be handed the problem to fix on my own.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** The supervisor combines individual support with institutional responsibility, so the employee is supported and the process is examined.

## Scene 4: A harmful version (1:45–2:05)
**Visual:** The same office.
**{{SUPERVISOR}}:** I'm sorry you feel excluded. How can I help you become more confident?
**{{CASE_MANAGER}}:** (later, to {{NEW_COLLEAGUE}}) I asked about how assignments get handed out, and somehow it turned into a conversation about my confidence.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** This assumes the employee's confidence caused the problem before anyone has reviewed the process, when the opportunities themselves may have been unevenly distributed.

## Scene 5: What it is not, and what it means for equity (2:05–2:50)
**Visual:** On-screen list, read aloud: "Not avoiding conflict. Not deciding what people need. Not giving up responsibility to act."
**{{NARRATOR}}:** Servant leadership is not avoiding conflict to keep people comfortable, and it is not deciding for people what they need. It is also not giving up the leader's responsibility to act. It can promote equity through better support, mentoring and access to resources, attention to people the organization tends to overlook, and trust that makes problems easier to raise. It undermines equity when helping becomes paternalistic, when harmony takes priority over confronting exclusion, or when kindness stands in for changes to authority and resources. In the lesson's words, the strongest version shares power and confronts inequity, while the weakest offers sympathy without changing conditions.

## Closing (2:50–3:05)
**Visual:** The reflection question on screen, read aloud.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** A question to consider on your own: When does support become paternalism? The rest of this lesson, on this page, covers another conflict, strengths and risks, when to use this approach, safeguards, and a practice exercise.

## Transcript notes
Media manifest summary: A fictional team scenario contrasts servant leadership that pairs support with a review of the process against a response that assumes the employee lacks confidence.
