## Scene 1: What this video covers (0:00–0:30)
**Visual:** Title card: "Autocratic or directive leadership: centralized decisions." Plain background, no logos or real people.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** This short video looks at autocratic or directive leadership, which this pathway sums up as centralized decisions. The leader keeps decision authority, but unlike the authoritarian pattern, may still consult people and allow respectful disagreement. The key question is what that authority is used for, and we will explore it through one fictional scenario, two possible responses, and what they mean for equity and inclusion.

## Scene 2: The common scenario (0:30–1:05)
**Visual:** A simple illustrated team meeting. On-screen title: "Who gets the visible work?" {{CASE_MANAGER}} appears as the employee who raised the concern.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** Every lesson in this pathway uses the same fictional scenario, "Who gets the visible work?" A supervisor keeps giving high-visibility assignments to employees described as polished, confident and ready to represent the team. A Black employee points out that they get a lot of behind-the-scenes technical work but fewer chances to present to leadership, and a colleague interrupts: "Everything doesn't have to be about race." Anything that may involve discrimination or retaliation goes through the agency's process, with the area responsible. Later, the employee goes to the supervisor.

## Scene 3: A stronger version (1:05–1:35)
**Visual:** {{SUPERVISOR}} and {{CASE_MANAGER}} at a small table in an office.
**{{CASE_MANAGER}}:** I want the assignments reviewed, and I'm concerned that raising this will hurt my opportunities.
**{{SUPERVISOR}}:** I will address the interruption and start the appropriate review. Before the next assignment decision, we will use explicit job-related criteria.
**{{CASE_MANAGER}}:** Okay. So there's a decision already, and it opens things up instead of closing them.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** The supervisor acts quickly and takes responsibility. The authority is used to open a review, not to end one.

## Scene 4: A harmful version (1:35–2:05)
**Visual:** The same office.
**{{SUPERVISOR}}:** I know the assignments have been fair. We're moving on.
**{{NEW_COLLEAGUE}}:** (later, to {{CASE_MANAGER}}) Did anyone actually look at who got what, or was that just the answer?
**{{NARRATOR}}:** Here the supervisor uses authority to close the question without looking at any evidence. Both responses are decisive, and the leader makes the call in each. The difference is whether authority is used to investigate and correct, or to shut down inquiry.

## Scene 5: What it is not, and what it means for equity (2:05–2:50)
**Visual:** On-screen list, read aloud: "Not refusing to listen. Not a reason to skip a required process. Not permanent authority."
**{{NARRATOR}}:** Directive leadership is not the same as refusing to listen. It is not permission to skip a required process because a decision feels urgent, and it is not a reason to keep temporary authority forever. Used well, directive authority can remove a clear barrier promptly, for example by funding interpreting or stopping a harmful practice, and it makes responsibility visible. When evidence and review are missing, though, one person's assumptions shape everyone's opportunities, the people affected may never be asked, and a pattern of quick decisions can turn into a pattern of exclusion. The lesson treats this approach as valuable for immediate action but not enough by itself to diagnose complex inequities.

## Closing (2:50–3:10)
**Visual:** The reflection question on screen, read aloud.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** A question to consider on your own: What separates using authority to investigate from using it to close a question? The rest of this lesson, on this page, covers another conflict, strengths and risks, when to use this approach, safeguards, and a practice exercise.
