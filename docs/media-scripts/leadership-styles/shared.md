## Scene 1: Authority across roles (0:00–0:26)
**Visual:** Title card on a plain background: "Shared or distributed leadership: authority across roles." It fades to an illustrated table where people from different roles sit together.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** This lesson is about shared, or distributed, leadership, which the pathway sums up as authority across roles. Leadership responsibilities and influence are spread across people with relevant expertise and experience. In the next few minutes, we'll see how this approach might respond to the common scenario, what it is not, and how it can help or harm equity and inclusion.

## Scene 2: The common scenario (0:26–1:03)
**Visual:** On-screen title: "Who gets the visible work?" The supervisor hands a presentation folder to one team member while {{CASE_MANAGER}} works through spreadsheets at a nearby desk. A short cut to a team meeting where another colleague, shown from behind, speaks over {{CASE_MANAGER}}.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** The pathway's fictional common scenario is called “Who gets the visible work?” A supervisor keeps giving high-visibility assignments to people they describe as polished, confident and ready to represent the team. {{CASE_MANAGER}}, a Black employee, points out that they get a lot of technical, behind-the-scenes work but fewer chances to present to leadership. In a team discussion, a colleague interrupts: “Everything doesn't have to be about race.” Later, {{CASE_MANAGER}} asks for the assignments to be reviewed and says they're worried that raising it will hurt their opportunities.

## Scene 3: A stronger version (1:03–1:37)
**Visual:** A team meeting. {{SUPERVISOR}} speaks to the group, which includes {{PROGRAM_MANAGER}}. On-screen label: "A stronger version."
**{{SUPERVISOR}}:** A cross-role group can help redesign how opportunities are allocated. Its scope, decision authority, time and relationship to final approval will be explicit.
**{{PROGRAM_MANAGER}}:** Good. If I'm on it, I'd want to know what we can actually decide and how much time we get to do it.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** The group gets real influence and the time to use it. The leader still keeps responsibility for the parts of the concern that need formal authority, which belong with the person or area responsible.

## Scene 4: A harmful version (1:37–1:58)
**Visual:** A team email on screen with the subject line "New equity team." The subject line is spoken in the line that follows. On-screen label: "A harmful version."
**{{SUPERVISOR}}:** We've formed an equity team. They'll meet monthly and send us recommendations.
**{{NEW_COLLEAGUE}}:** A new equity team, sending recommendations to whom? And does anyone have to answer them?
**{{NARRATOR}}:** There is no stated authority, no time set aside and no commitment to respond. The group becomes more meetings without influence.

## Scene 5: What it is not, and equity (1:58–2:40)
**Visual:** The same few illustrated figures appear in one meeting after another, each time with more papers in front of them. No text on screen.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** Shared leadership is not an advisory group that has no decision power, and it is not extra unpaid work for the same few people. Done well, it brings in knowledge from people closest to the work and the community, builds continuity beyond any one leader, and shares agenda-setting, not only advice. Representation without authority, however, becomes tokenism. Do not rely disproportionately on employees of color to educate colleagues, repair culture and design reforms on top of their regular work. When they are asked, they need authority, time and recognition, and the responsibility for results must stay with leaders.

## Closing (2:40–2:57)
**Visual:** The reflection question on screen: "What separates shared leadership from tokenism?" Then the lesson page's section list.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** A question to consider on your own: what separates shared leadership from tokenism? The rest of this lesson, on this page, covers another conflict, strengths and risks, when to use this approach, complementary approaches and safeguards, and a practice exercise.

## Transcript notes
Shows how shared leadership might respond to the common scenario, contrasting a cross-role group with explicit authority and time against an equity team that only sends recommendations nobody must answer.
