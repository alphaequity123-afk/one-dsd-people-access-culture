# Video script: Empowering or delegative leadership

- **Status:** Script only, written for the recurring cast (docs/media-scripts/CAST.md). Not recorded.
- **Publish on:** /learn/leadership-styles/empowering
- **Length:** about 3 minutes (about 449 spoken words)
- **Cast:** Narrator (C3, Host voice), Supervisor (C6, Co-host voice), Case manager (C5, Host voice), Newer colleague (C7, Co-host voice) (see docs/media-scripts/CAST.md)
- **Accessibility:** captions and a full transcript are required before publishing (docs/ACCESSIBILITY-STANDARD.md). On-screen text must also be spoken. Describe any visual that carries meaning.

## Scene 1: Supported independence (0:00–0:25)
**Visual:** Title card on a plain background: "Empowering or delegative leadership: supported independence." It fades to an illustrated open-plan office with a small team at work.
**Narrator (C3):** This lesson is about empowering, or delegative, leadership, which the pathway sums up as supported independence. The leader shares authority while also providing resources, clarity, support and accountability. In the next few minutes, we'll see how this approach might respond to the common scenario, what it is not, and how it can help or harm equity and inclusion.

## Scene 2: The common scenario (0:25–1:09)
**Visual:** On-screen title: "Who gets the visible work?" The supervisor hands a presentation folder to one team member while the case manager (C5) works through spreadsheets at a nearby desk. A short cut to a team meeting where another colleague, shown from behind, speaks over the case manager (C5).
**Narrator (C3):** The pathway's fictional common scenario is called “Who gets the visible work?” A supervisor keeps giving high-visibility assignments to people they describe as polished, confident and ready to represent the team. The case manager, a Black employee, points out that they get a lot of technical, behind-the-scenes work but fewer chances to present to leadership. In a team discussion, a colleague interrupts: “Everything doesn't have to be about race.” Later, the case manager asks for the assignments to be reviewed and says they're worried that raising it will hurt their opportunities. Any part that calls for a formal process stays with the person or area responsible.

## Scene 3: A stronger version (1:09–1:40)
**Visual:** The next team meeting. The supervisor (C6) speaks to the whole group. On-screen label: "A stronger version."
**Supervisor (C6):** The team can help design a more transparent assignment process. I remain responsible for addressing the concern, making sure the process is appropriate, and providing the resources to carry it out.
**Newer colleague (C7):** So we help shape how it works, but the responsibility doesn't just land on us.
**Narrator (C3):** The employee who raised the concern is not handed the job of fixing inequity because they raised it. The leader shares the design work and keeps the responsibility.

## Scene 4: A harmful version (1:40–2:05)
**Visual:** After a meeting, the supervisor (C6) stops at the case manager (C5)'s desk, which is stacked with files. On-screen label: "A harmful version."
**Supervisor (C6):** You raised it, so why don't you lead a group to fix the assignment process?
**Case manager (C5):** On top of everything else I'm carrying? And who decides whether the group's plan actually happens?
**Narrator (C3):** This places the burden of the fix on the person who reported the problem, usually on top of their regular work, and without authority, time or protection.

## Scene 5: What it is not, and equity (2:05–2:51)
**Visual:** Simple illustrations of a desk with a calendar, a key and a shield, standing for time, authority and protection. No text on screen.
**Narrator (C3):** Empowering leadership is not handing off a problem and walking away, and it is not giving the person who raised a concern the job of fixing it. Done well, it distributes meaningful authority, builds skills and visibility for more people, and uses the judgment of those closest to the work. It undermines equity when preparation is uneven, or when responsibility arrives without resources and becomes abandonment. Not every employee starts with equal access to networks or informal sponsorship. Without information, authority, time, support, access to influential partners and protection, delegation can fall hardest on employees of color who are already asked to do extra equity work.

## Closing (2:51–3:12)
**Visual:** The reflection question on screen: "Why should the person who raised a concern not automatically be put in charge of fixing it?" Then the lesson page's section list.
**Narrator (C3):** A question to consider on your own: why should the person who raised a concern not automatically be put in charge of fixing it? The rest of this lesson, on this page, covers another conflict, strengths and risks, when to use this approach, complementary approaches and safeguards, and a practice exercise.

## Transcript notes
Shows how empowering leadership might respond to the common scenario, contrasting shared design work that keeps the leader responsible with handing the fix to the person who raised the concern.
