# Video script: Charismatic leadership

- **Status:** Script only, written for the recurring cast (docs/media-scripts/CAST.md). Not recorded.
- **Publish on:** /learn/leadership-styles/charismatic
- **Length:** about 3 minutes (about 437 spoken words)
- **Cast:** Narrator (C3, Host voice), Supervisor (C6, Co-host voice), Case manager (C5, Host voice), Newer colleague (C7, Co-host voice) (see docs/media-scripts/CAST.md)
- **Accessibility:** captions and a full transcript are required before publishing (docs/ACCESSIBILITY-STANDARD.md). On-screen text must also be spoken. Describe any visual that carries meaning.

## Scene 1: What this video covers (0:00–0:35)
**Visual:** Title card: "Charismatic leadership: mobilizing through personal influence." Plain background, no logos or real people.
**Narrator (C3):** This short video looks at charismatic leadership, which this pathway sums up as mobilizing through personal influence. The leader builds commitment through presence, storytelling, confidence and emotional connection. Charisma can bring energy and attention to a neglected issue, and the risk the lesson names is that the work comes to depend on the person rather than on clear criteria and shared ownership. We will look at one fictional scenario and two ways a leader might respond.

## Scene 2: The common scenario (0:35–1:10)
**Visual:** A simple illustrated team meeting. On-screen title: "Who gets the visible work?" The case manager (C5) appears as the employee who raised the concern.
**Narrator (C3):** Every lesson in this pathway uses the same fictional scenario, "Who gets the visible work?" A supervisor keeps giving high-visibility assignments to employees described as polished, confident and ready to represent the team. A Black employee points out that they get a lot of behind-the-scenes technical work but fewer chances to present to leadership, and a colleague interrupts: "Everything doesn't have to be about race." Anything that may involve discrimination or retaliation goes through the agency's process, with the area responsible. Later, the employee goes to the supervisor.

## Scene 3: A stronger version (1:10–1:45)
**Visual:** The supervisor (C6) and the case manager (C5) at a small table in an office.
**Case manager (C5):** I want the assignments reviewed, and I'm concerned that raising this will hurt my opportunities.
**Supervisor (C6):** Our commitments mean little if opportunity depends on an informal impression of who looks ready. We will make the criteria visible and review the process.
**Newer colleague (C7):** (at the next team meeting) When our supervisor puts it that way, people actually start paying attention to how the criteria are written.
**Narrator (C3):** Here the supervisor uses personal influence to build attention and support for a fair process, not for themselves.

## Scene 4: A harmful version (1:45–2:05)
**Visual:** The same office.
**Supervisor (C6):** You know how committed I am to inclusion. Trust me.
**Case manager (C5):** (later, to the newer colleague (C7)) I don't doubt that our supervisor means it. But nobody said anything about looking at the assignments.
**Narrator (C3):** The issue becomes the leader's reputation instead of the employee's experience, and trust is asked for in place of a review.

## Scene 5: What it is not, and what it means for equity (2:05–2:45)
**Visual:** On-screen list, read aloud: "Not one personality or way of speaking. Not proof of fairness. Not a substitute for evidence."
**Narrator (C3):** Charismatic leadership is not one kind of personality or one way of speaking. It is not proof that a leader's decisions are fair, and it is not a substitute for evidence. Charisma can mobilize resources and attention for neglected priorities, and a compelling message can help people see why equity work matters. It can also reward familiarity, dominant communication norms or loyalty, treat criticism as disloyalty, and leave progress that disappears when the leader moves on. The lesson also cautions against assuming charisma has one appearance, since quiet, reflective, accented or less performative leadership should not be treated as less credible.

## Closing (2:45–3:05)
**Visual:** The reflection question on screen, read aloud.
**Narrator (C3):** A question to consider on your own: What is the difference between using influence for a fair process and asking people to trust you? The rest of this lesson, on this page, covers another conflict, strengths and risks, when to use this approach, safeguards, and a practice exercise.

## Transcript notes
Media manifest summary: A fictional team scenario contrasts a leader who uses personal influence to back a visible, fair process with one who asks for trust in place of a review.
