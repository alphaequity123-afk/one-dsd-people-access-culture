## Scene 1: Belonging and full contribution (0:00–0:25)
**Visual:** Title card on a plain background: "Inclusive leadership: belonging and full contribution." It fades to an illustrated team meeting.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** This lesson is about inclusive leadership, which the pathway sums up as belonging and full contribution. It emphasizes belonging, contribution and valuing differences without requiring people to assimilate. In the next few minutes, we'll see how this approach might respond to the common scenario, what it is not, and how it can help or harm equity and inclusion.

## Scene 2: The common scenario (0:25–1:02)
**Visual:** On-screen title: "Who gets the visible work?" The supervisor hands a presentation folder to one team member while {{CASE_MANAGER}} works through spreadsheets at a nearby desk. A short cut to a team meeting where another colleague, shown from behind, speaks over {{CASE_MANAGER}}.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** The pathway's fictional common scenario is called “Who gets the visible work?” A supervisor keeps giving high-visibility assignments to people they describe as polished, confident and ready to represent the team. {{CASE_MANAGER}}, a Black employee, points out that they get a lot of technical, behind-the-scenes work but fewer chances to present to leadership. In a team discussion, a colleague interrupts: “Everything doesn't have to be about race.” Later, {{CASE_MANAGER}} asks for the assignments to be reviewed and says they're worried that raising it will hurt their opportunities.

## Scene 3: A stronger version (1:02–1:30)
**Visual:** The next team meeting. {{SUPERVISOR}} speaks to the whole group. On-screen label: "A stronger version."
**{{SUPERVISOR}}:** People should be able to contribute without having to resemble one preferred leadership style. Let's look at how presentation opportunities are shared and what preparation people get.
**{{NEW_COLLEAGUE}}:** That's good to hear. I've honestly wondered what “polished” is supposed to look like here.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** The leader connects belonging to opportunity. Inclusion here means more than warmth. It means access to the work that leads to advancement.

## Scene 4: A harmful version (1:30–1:50)
**Visual:** The same meeting. {{SUPERVISOR}} smiles and spreads both hands toward the group. On-screen label: "A harmful version."
**{{SUPERVISOR}}:** We're all one big family here. Let's not divide ourselves into groups.
**{{CASE_MANAGER}}:** I'm not trying to divide anyone. I'm asking who gets to present.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** This treats naming a pattern as the problem. It asks the employee to give up a legitimate concern for the sake of harmony.

## Scene 5: What it is not, and equity (1:50–2:38)
**Visual:** The illustrated team at a friendly lunch, then the same team in a leadership briefing where only two of them are presenting. No text on screen.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** Inclusive leadership is not a friendly climate that leaves opportunity unchanged. It is not asking everyone to fit one preferred style, and it is not “we're one big family,” used to avoid talking about differences. At its best, it builds belonging without assimilation and helps more people contribute fully. Its risk is staying at the level of good feelings, where positive climate language hides unchanged access to power and resources. Inclusion that matters for racial equity reaches decisions: who presents, who is sponsored, whose ideas are adopted and who is promoted. It also does not replace a formal response to reported discrimination or harassment, which stays with the person or area responsible.

## Closing (2:38–2:57)
**Visual:** The reflection question on screen: "What would inclusion look like in who gets visible work?" Then the lesson page's section list.
**{{NARRATOR}}:** A question to consider on your own: what would inclusion look like in who gets visible work? The rest of this lesson, on this page, covers another conflict, strengths and risks, when to use this approach, complementary approaches and safeguards, and a practice exercise.
