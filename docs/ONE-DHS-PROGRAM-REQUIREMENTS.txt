One DHS People, Access and Culture: Program Requirements Document
Nov 16, 2026 · @Gary Banks

Purpose
One DHS People, Access and Culture gives every employee of the Minnesota Department of Human Services (DHS) one place to learn, plan and act so that every person DHS serves gets fair, respectful and understandable service.

DHS serves Minnesotans of every culture, language, faith, ability and background. Many of them meet DHS at hard moments: applying for food or health coverage, arranging care for a family member, or planning support to live and work in the community. How staff listen, explain and decide shapes whether those people can actually reach the help the law promises them.

The application turns that responsibility into everyday practice. It brings together courses, short readings, narrated videos, podcasts, practice cases, planning tools, community briefs, a calendar of cultural observances and a support directory. Everything is written in plain language, open without a sign-in, and ready to use in the middle of a workday.

The Disability Services Division (DSD) serves as the deepest working example. Its program areas, work situations and community outreach show how the same approach works inside a single division, so other areas of DHS can follow the same path.

This document describes what the application is for, whom it serves, how it benefits staff and leaders, and how it strengthens culturally responsive service to the people DHS and DSD serve. It also sets the requirements the application must keep meeting as it grows.

The application serves two groups at once: the people who do the work of DHS, and the Minnesotans that work is for.

The first three groups use the application directly. The last two are the reason it exists. Every course, tool and resource is judged by one question: will this help a DHS employee serve the next person better?

What the application offers
The application holds more than 3,700 courses, resources, briefs and tools, organized so that anything useful is no more than three selections away.

Starting points. The home page asks one question: what are you working on? Choices include making a policy or service decision, planning a meeting, improving a form or process, planning community engagement, reviewing a change, building understanding, and finding who decides. A short Start helper asks about role, the work at hand and the time available, then suggests a first step.

Learning. More than 660 courses are arranged in eight outcome areas, each at foundational, intermediate and advanced levels:

Outcome area

What staff learn

Shared understanding of equity, policy and key terms

The DHS Equity Policy, civil rights duties and common terms in plain language

Disability inclusion and rights

Disability rights, the Olmstead Plan, home and community-based services, person-centered practice

Intercultural competence and community knowledge

Cultural humility, cultural intelligence, and courses on 41 communities that call Minnesota home

Language access and accessible communication

Interpreters, translated materials, plain language and accessible documents

Inclusive team culture and belonging

Respectful teams, psychological safety, conflict and communication

Equitable workforce practices

Fair hiring panels, inclusive role design, support for staff with disabilities

Equity in policy, programs, budget and data

Reviewing decisions, notices, forms and data for fair access

Leading equity and systems change

Leadership approaches, sponsorship and lasting change

Signature trainings include Cultural Humility, a narrated course of about 52 minutes with an all-staff version and situations for each job family, and The Water We Swim In, six modules with two-voice podcasts on Minnesota culture, its history and how culture shows up inside the systems DHS runs. Other collections cover the National CLAS Standards (Culturally and Linguistically Appropriate Services), intercultural learning, community engagement, leadership styles and 304 book summaries.

Media built for every learner. Videos come with captions and transcripts. Podcasts have chapters, speed control and transcripts that highlight each sentence as it is spoken. Courses and resources download as Word, Excel, PowerPoint, PDF or plain text, and each has its own link to share.

Tools for real work. The Equity Analysis Toolkit, required of all DHS staff, walks through a decision step by step: start with the decision and the people it affects, learn from people and evidence, and compare options. Equity reviews come in three sizes: a quick check for routine decisions, a standard analysis for changes that affect access, and an in-depth review for policy or system changes. A document review tool checks a policy, notice or plan against chosen questions and shows where the evidence is strong and where gaps remain. Guided practices cover changing a form or notice, community engagement, team climate, inclusive hiring and planning a learning session.

Ask and Guidance. Ask answers workplace and service questions from the application's own material and links to the sources behind each answer. Guidance offers hints and worked examples during practice.

Communities. Forty-two Minnesota community briefs help staff prepare to serve people well. The Culture Calendar lists 569 observances with sources. The Community Outreach and Engagement hub helps staff prepare to meet a community, find an organization, plan engagement and reach people, with a directory of more than 900 community organizations. Tribal Nations pages point to the person or area responsible for Tribal relations and consultation.

Across DHS. Staff can view resources by job family (eleven families, from eligibility and direct public service to executive leadership) and by administration or area. Choosing a job family reorders what appears first; it never hides anything.

Support. A support directory and a Find the right person tool connect staff to the person or area responsible for a question, and an employee resource groups page lists groups open to all staff, including allies.

The DSD example. The Disability Services Division section shows the full approach in one division: program areas, work situations, team kits, Amplify Equity, 274 disability services policy summaries, 42 equity data resources and the program charter.

Benefits for staff
Staff get practical help at the moment they need it, in a form that fits a full workday.

Help for the task in front of you. Instead of searching many sites, a staff member names the work at hand, such as rewriting a notice, preparing for a home visit or planning a community meeting, and gets matched courses, tools and contacts.
Learning that fits the day. Short readings, ten-minute podcasts and longer narrated courses let people learn in the time they have, at a desk, on a phone or on a commute.
Confidence with people of many cultures. Community courses and briefs help staff prepare to serve Somali, Hmong, Karen, Oromo, Latino, American Indian, Deaf and hard of hearing, rural Minnesotan and many other communities, while reminding them that each person is an individual, not a profile.
Better conversations. Cultural Humility teaches staff to separate what was said from the meaning they added, ask purposeful questions and respond to correction without getting defensive. Those skills lead to fewer misunderstandings and stronger trust with the people they serve.
Clear answers with sources. Ask gives a direct answer and shows where it came from, so staff can check the policy behind it before they act.
Tools that turn learning into action. Equity reviews, guided practices and the Equity Analysis Toolkit give step-by-step help on real decisions, with templates staff can download and use right away.
Room to grow personally. The application is built for the whole person. Staff explore their own cultural background alongside the cultures of others, which builds understanding that lasts beyond any single course.
Learning without fear. Nothing is tracked or scored, so staff can explore hard topics, make mistakes in practice and ask honest questions in private.
Fair for every learner. Captions, transcripts, adjustable playback speed, printable pages and plain language make the material usable by staff with disabilities and staff whose first language is not English.
Benefits for supervisors and managers
Leaders get ready-made support for building teams where staff feel respected and the people they serve get consistent, fair service.

A dedicated leaders' space. Supervisors, managers and directors have their own entry point with ways to lead a team or a decision, compare leadership approaches and follow suggested learning paths.
Team sessions ready to run. Team kits provide complete session plans with timing and an agenda, such as a 20-minute practice case, a 30-minute book discussion or a 45-minute session to prepare a fair hiring panel. A supervisor can run a strong team conversation with almost no preparation.
Coaching support. Leadership courses cover seven leadership styles, conflict, psychological safety and how to support staff with disabilities, giving supervisors language and methods for hard conversations.
Fairer hiring and workforce decisions. Guided practices on inclusive hiring and role design help managers widen applicant pools, structure fair interviews and design roles that people with disabilities can fill.
Sound, defensible decisions. The three sizes of equity review let a manager match the depth of review to the size of the decision, and keep a clear record of what was considered and why.
Trust built in. Because learning is never tracked or reported, supervisors encourage growth rather than monitor it. The conversation shifts from compliance to development.
A view across DHS. Leaders can see how the same approach applies in other administrations and use the DSD example as a model for their own area.
Benefits for the program and the people served
The greatest benefit reaches people who never open the application: Minnesotans who meet a DHS employee better prepared to understand them.

Culturally responsive service across DHS. Minnesota is home to long-established communities and newer arrivals, eleven Tribal Nations, rural and urban families, and people of many faiths and languages. The application prepares staff to meet each person with curiosity and respect, to check assumptions before they become decisions, and to adapt communication to the person rather than asking the person to adapt to the system.

Language access that works. Training on interpreters, translated materials, plain language and accessible documents, together with the National CLAS Standards, helps staff make sure people understand their rights, their options and the decisions that affect them.

Fair access to programs. Equity reviews and the Equity Analysis Toolkit help staff find barriers in forms, notices, timelines and processes before those barriers reach the public. Small fixes, such as a clearer notice or an added interpreter step, can decide whether a family keeps coverage or a person gets services on time.

Culturally responsive disability services. For the people DSD serves, the application treats disability as part of a person's identity and culture, not only as a need to be managed. Staff learn disability rights, the Olmstead Plan, person-centered practice and the history of disability in Minnesota. They also learn how disability intersects with race, language, immigration and rural life. That helps them:

plan services around what matters to the person and family
explain waivers and home and community-based services in ways families understand
recognize when a community may distrust services because of past experiences
spot gaps in access across communities and work to close them
Stronger community partnerships. The Community Outreach and Engagement hub and the directory of community organizations help staff work with trusted community groups and move from collecting input to acting on it. Communities see their input lead to real change.

Respect for Tribal sovereignty. Tribal Nations pages stand as written and direct staff to the person or area responsible for Tribal relations and consultation, so that government-to-government relationships are honored.

A shared language across the agency. When staff in every administration learn the same core ideas, people get more consistent treatment from one program to the next. The DSD example shows other divisions how to build on that common base.

Support for agency commitments. The application helps DHS carry out its Equity Policy, civil rights and accessibility duties, the Olmstead Plan, and the Aging and Disability Services Administration equity and inclusion plan. It turns those commitments into daily practice rather than leaving them on paper.

Privacy, access and trust
Staff learn openly only when they know their learning is their own. The application keeps that trust through firm commitments:

No sign-in. Anyone can read, learn and use the tools without an account.
Learning is private. No record is kept of who visits, what they read, which lessons they take, what they ask or how they answer. Learning is never counted, never shown as totals, and never used as evidence for compliance or performance reviews.
Your work stays with you. Saved items, practice plans and your place in a course stay on your own computer.
Private information stays out. Every place that accepts typed text reminds people to leave out client, case, health and personnel details.
Facts have sources. Every fact names where it came from, and every resource links to its source.
People decide. Tools and answers support a decision. They never replace the judgment of the person or area responsible.
Open to everyone. Pages work with a keyboard, screen readers, magnification and phones. Videos have captions and transcripts, and every page prints in full.
Easy to share. Every course and resource has its own link and downloads as Word, Excel, PowerPoint, a printable page or plain text.
The application also evaluates responses, never people. It gives no scores and does not rank people, teams or beliefs.

Measures of success
Success is measured by changes in the work and in the service people receive, never by tracking who uses the application.

Measure

Evidence

Clearer communication with the public

Notices, forms and letters revised for plain language and language access

Fairer decisions

Equity reviews and decision records that teams choose to keep with their work

Better access to services

Barriers found and removed in processes, timelines and eligibility steps

Stronger community partnerships

Engagement plans carried out, and community input that led to a documented change

Respectful, inclusive teams

Team kits used in meetings, and fair hiring panels and role designs put in place

Culturally responsive disability services

DSD program changes that reflect person-centered practice and close gaps between communities

Staff growth

Outcomes staff choose to share, without names, about what changed in their work

Application quality

Regular checks that answers are accurate and sourced, pages are accessible, and content is current

These measures follow a simple improvement cycle: plan a change, try it, study what happened, and adjust. Each cycle makes the application more useful to staff and DHS service more responsive to the people of Minnesota.
